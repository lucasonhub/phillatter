# Next‐Generation Integrated Pest Management in Oilseed Rape and Canola: can new approaches solve the age‐old problem of IPM delivery?

**Autores:** S\. M\. Cook; Kevin M\. King; Michael Rostás; M\. Jędryczka

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

1 INTRODUCTION Integrated pest management \(IPM\) is a decades\-old concept, but has failed to reach its potential in offering agriculture efficient and cost\-effective crop protection using a diversity of approaches\.1 This is mainly due to the cheapness and ease\-of\-use of synthetic insecticides, with IPM becoming more of an ‘integrated pesticide management’ system \(rotate crops, monitor pests; if populations exceed threshold, then apply pesticides\) than the ‘real’ IPM intended\. However, public lobbying for pesticide reductions, the revocation of several key pesticides including neonicotinoid insecticidal seed treatments by the European Union in 2014, and the widespread resistance of pests to most of the commonly used pesticides have driven research towards the diversity of approaches first imagined\. Arguably, the clearest example of this is in Brassica napus, for which, particularly in Europe, few, if any, synthetic control options are commercially available, particularly for insect pests\. B\. napus is grown around the world as an important source of food\-grade oil and as food stock for biofuel, with meal used for animal feed\. Two major forms of B\. napus are grown: oilseed rape \(OSR\) and canola\. OSR is winter hardy, and therefore autumn\-sown with yields benefitting from the long growing period; it represents the dominant form used in Europe\. The spring\-sown form is widely grown in Australia, Canada, China, the USA and other southern regions where the growing season is shorter; it is commonly known as canola\. A dearth of synthetic control options and the pressure to conserve biodiversity through pesticide reduction have stimulated the evolution of ‘next\-generation’ IPM tactics for systems that are less reliant, if not completely unreliant, on synthetic toxicants\. These approaches are the subject of this Special Issue\. Like our last Special Issue of Pest Management Science, ‘Integrated Pest Control in Oilseed Crops’ \(Volume 80, Issue 5\),2 this Issue was inspired by a meeting of the International Organization for Biological Control&\#x27;s Working Group on ‘Integrated Control in Oilseed Crops’, which was held in Dresden, Germany \(10–11 September 2024\)\.3 This working group comprises an entomology and a pathology subsection\. Meetings are very friendly and effective in fostering a community of researchers that stimulates research and drives forward the science\.

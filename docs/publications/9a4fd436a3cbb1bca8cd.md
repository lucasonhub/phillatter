abstract: 'Dear Editors, Liver transplantation (LT) remains the most effective curative
  option for selected patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), addressing both
  tumor burden and the underlying liver disease. Selection has evolved beyond the
  original Milan criteria toward a more dynamic framework, incorporating tumor biology
  and response to therapy [1]. Within this paradigm, locoregional treatments (LRT)
  are not only used to maintain eligibility, but also to inform candidacy over time.Radiation-based
  therapies, including yttrium-90 transarterial radioembolization (TARE) and stereotactic
  body radiation therapy (SBRT), are increasingly integrated into these pathways.
  Both provide high rates of local control and are used as bridging or downstaging
  strategies [2,3]; treatment choice is typically individualized, depending on tumor
  burden, vascular anatomy, hepatic functional reserve, prior treatments and center
  expertise.We performed a retrospective analysis of all adult patients undergoing
  LT for HCC at Vancouver General Hospital between 2015 and 2023, who received either
  TARE or SBRT as their final radiation treatment before transplantation. Prior LRTs
  were allowed, reflecting current clinical practice. Liver and patient safety was
  assessed by monitoring the MELD score dynamics pre-and post-treatment, as well as
  the occurrence of Radiation-Induced Liver Disease (RILD). Radiographic response
  of treated lesions was classified according to the LI-RADS Radiation Treatment Response
  (LR-TR) algorithm [4], using imaging acquired within six months following radiation.
  All pre-transplant imaging were systematically reviewed by a dedicated radiologist
  and a transplant surgeon, with final consensus provided by a senior abdominal radiologist.
  Total tumor volume (TTV) was calculated using CT or MRI, assuming spherical geometry
  from lesions classified as LI-RADS 4 or 5. Pre-transplant imaging corresponded to
  the most recent study within six months prior to transplantation, and was used to
  obtain updated pre-transplant TTV and LR-TR of the treated lesions. Explant pathology
  was reviewed to determine pathologic complete response (pCR) of the treated lesions
  and calculate viable TTV.A total of 56 patients met inclusion criteria: 23 received
  TARE and 33 received SBRT. Baseline demographics were similar between groups, including
  age (61.7 vs 60.7 years, p=0.57), sex distribution (87% male in both) and underlying
  liver disease. Treatment allocation was determined by a multidisciplinary Liver
  Tumor Board: TARE was preferentially selected for patients with a higher baseline
  tumor burden, larger maximum tumor diameter (3.8 vs 3.2 cm, p=0.014) and greater
  TTV (29.6 vs 17.9 cm³, p<0.01). Conversely, SBRT was more frequently used as a salvage
  modality following prior multimodal LRT (79% SBRT vs 35% TARE, p<0.01). It was also
  favored for lesions in anatomically challenging positions, or in patients for whom
  further surgical, ablative, or transarterial therapy was considered unsuitable by
  the multidisciplinary team. SBRT was delivered per institutional practice [5,6].
  The gross tumor volume (GTV) was defined as arterial-enhancing lesions with delayed
  washout on co-registered diagnostic imaging, with an isotropic 5 mm clinical target
  volume (CTV) margin confined to the liver parenchyma. Motion management included
  dynamic tumor tracking (DTT), respiratory gating, or internal target volume strategies
  based on individual patient suitability. Daily cone-beam CT guidance was performed
  for non-gated or non-DTT cases. Planning strictly prioritized organ-at-risk constraints
  over target volume coverage. The median prescribed dose was 45 Gy (range 35-45 Gy)
  in 3-5 fractions, corresponding to a median biologically effective dose (BED10)
  of 112.5 Gy10.On post-treatment imaging, non-viable disease was reported in 52.2%
  of TARE and 57.6% of SBRT-treated lesions, while viable disease persisted in 30.4%
  and 15.2% (p=0.35).Both approaches were well tolerated. Median MELD prior to treatment
  was 8 in both groups, with minimal change following therapy (ΔMELD=1.0 in both,
  p=0.92). There was one suspected case of RILD following SBRT, presenting as hepatic
  decompensation. The median interval from final radiation treatment to liver transplantation
  was comparable between groups (337 for SBRT vs 360 days for TARE, p=0.742). Post-transplant
  outcomes, including surgical complications and length of stay, were comparable between
  groups, indicating that pre-transplant radiation did not adversely affect surgical
  feasibility or recovery.Explant analysis demonstrated variable residual tumor burden.
  pCR was observed in 36.4% of SBRT and 21.7% of TARE-treated lesion (p=0.38). Although
  pCR was numerically higher after SBRT, this possibly reflects a cumulative treatment
  effect following prior therapies and the lower baseline tumor burden in this cohort,
  rather than implying the superiority of a specific modality. Overall radiographic-pathologic
  discordance was nearly identical between modalities (40.6% for SBRT vs 40.9% for
  TARE, p>0.99). Among lesions classified as non-viable on repeated pre-transplant
  imaging, only 58% demonstrated complete necrosis on explant, despite an overall
  predictive OR of 4.22 (1.06-19.52, p=0.037).Neither subgroup individually demonstrated
  a statistically significant association between LR-TR classification and pathologic
  viability (SBRT: OR 2.93, 0.55-17.07, p=0.28; TARE: OR 3.75, 0.23-132.89, p=0.59),
  confirming that radiographic overestimation of complete necrosis occurs across both
  treatment pathways. Similarly, there was no meaningful correlation between radiographic
  and pathologic TTV throughout the cohort, which accounted for the total burden of
  treated and untreated lesions. Although a stronger Pearson''s correlation was observed
  in the SBRT group (r=0.76, p<0.001) compared to TARE (r=-0.07, p=0.77) (Figure S1),
  this is most likely a reflection of the lower overall tumor burden in the SBRT group.
  These findings underscore the limitations of cross-sectional imaging in the post-radiation
  setting, where treatment-induced fibrosis and inflammatory changes can mask viable
  tumor.Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) kinetics were also not predictive of pCR. In multivariable
  analysis adjusting for baseline AFP,tumor burden and Milan status, the AFP log-ratio
  was not associated with pCR (OR 1.13, 0.43-2.92, p=0.81). This reinforces existing
  concerns that AFP may be an imperfect surrogate for complete tumor eradication in
  the post-radiation setting. Post-transplant outcomes were favorable over a comparable
  median follow-up (35 months for SBRT vs 36 for TARE, p=0.884). Recurrence-free survival
  at 1 and 3 years was 91.3% and 77.1% for TARE and 93.9% and 76.7% for SBRT, respectively.
  In an adjusted Cox model, treatment modality was not associated with post-transplant
  recurrence (HR 1.49, 0.38-5.80, p=0.56). Overall survival at 1 and 3 years was similarly
  high, with no significant differences between groups (Figure 1).This study reflects
  real-world use of radiation-based therapies in LT candidates with HCC. Rather than
  functioning as isolated interventions, TARE and SBRT are applied within iterative,
  multimodal treatment pathways. TARE was preferentially used in patients with higher
  initial tumor burden, while SBRT was commonly employed after multiple treatments.Within
  these distinct treatment pathways, both modalities were safe and associated with
  favorable post-transplant outcomes. Our observed per-lesion pathologic complete
  response rates reflect real-world bridging in a pre-treated cohort.For SBRT, our
  pCR rate is lower than the 58.3% per-lesion rate reported in prospective trial data
  [7], which exclusively evaluated treatment-naïve candidates. For TARE, while highly
  selective ablative segmentectomy series report pCR rates of 68% [8], our 21.7% rate
  aligns with real-world national registry data reporting an overall pCR rate of 33.6%,
  and a center-level range of 0.0%-50.0% [9]. The differences across studies likely
  reflect differences in patient selection, baseline tumor burden, prior LRT exposure,
  treatment technique, and definitions of pathologic response. Radiographic posttreatment
  assessment was frequently discordant with explant findings: non-viability often
  overestimated complete tumor necrosis, and neither AFP kinetics nor imaging reliably
  predicted pCR. This has direct implications for waitlist management and underscores
  the need for continued surveillance and cautious interpretation of treatment response.
  Emerging tools, including circulating tumor DNA and radiomics, might eventually
  provide more granular data to improve prediction of tumor burden and recurrence
  before and after transplantation [10]. The limitations of this study include its
  retrospective design, modest sample size, and the inherent treatment selection bias
  associated with multimodal therapy. Additionally, restricting volumetric analysis
  to LI-RADS 4/5 lesions and the interval between pre-transplant imaging and LT may
  have contributed to the observed radiologic-pathologic discordance, although both
  methodological choices reflect routine clinical practice. Furthermore, our study
  lacks waitlist dropout data for non-transplanted patients, precluding an intention-totreat
  analysis and limiting our conclusions to candidates who successfully reached transplantation.
  In conclusion, these data support the safety and feasibility of TARE and SBRT in
  current transplant pathways for HCC, highlighting the challenge of interpreting
  post-radiation treatment response within increasingly complex, multimodal treatment
  landscapes.'

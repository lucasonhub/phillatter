# Aware but not verifying: media literacy, verification behaviour, and misinformation resilience at three universities in Somalia

**Autores:** Abdullahi Mohamud Mohamed

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Somalia combines rapid growth in internet use with fragile institutions and a clan\-based social structure in which rumour carries real consequences, yet almost nothing is known about how Somali students evaluate the information they consume\. This study examined self\-reported media literacy, misinformation\-fighting competency, and demonstrated verification performance among students at three universities in Mogadishu, Hargeisa, and Garowe\. A cross\-sectional, convergent mixed\-methods design with an embedded behavioural component combined a non\-probability \(purposive\) online survey \( N = 393\) with semi\-structured interviews and a five\-item performance task administered to 15 purposively selected interviewees\. Self\-report measured media literacy and misinformation\-fighting competency; the task measured demonstrated resistance on specific items\. Resilience is used here as the higher\-order outcome these components feed into, and is not itself measured directly\. Students reported moderately high media literacy \(M = 3\.82, α = \.84\) and strong awareness that unverified information harms society \(M = 4\.15\), but markedly weaker verification behaviour \(M = 3\.08\) and source\-identification skill \(M = 2\.91\)\. Within the study sample, media literacy was positively associated with misinformation\-fighting competency \(r = \.62, p &lt; \.001\); given the cross\-sectional design and non\-probability sampling, this is a statistical association within the sample rather than a causal or population\-level relationship\. In the performance task, participants correctly classified only 35% of misinformation items against a mean self\-rated confidence equivalent to 72%\. In the interviews students attributed these gaps to a forwarding culture that rewards speed over scrutiny, to clan affiliation operating as an informal credibility filter, and to a functional differentiation of platforms in which Facebook serves as the default news infrastructure\. These patterns suggest that awareness\-oriented media literacy programming may not be sufficient on its own to change behaviour, and that curricula in Somali universities could usefully prioritise habitual verification practice, source evaluation, and resistance to identity\-based manipulation\. The quantitative findings are descriptive and exploratory: the three universities were purposively selected and the Garowe sub\-sample comprised only 12 students, so the results are not statistically representative of, and should not be generalised to, all universities or university students in Somalia\.

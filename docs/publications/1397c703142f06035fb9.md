# Curriculum expectation and classroom reality in manipulative use by JHS mathematics teachers in Amansie Central Ghana

**Autores:** Godfred Asiedu Mireku

**Data:** 2026-10-09 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Ghana&\#x27;s Junior High School \(JHS\) mathematics curriculum expects teachers to use concrete manipulatives\. In the Amansie Central District, Basic Education Certificate Examination \(BECE\) mathematics failure rates exceeded 55% each year from 2022 to 2024; this underachievement provides the context, rather than the object, of the present study\. Drawing on a curriculum\-enactment perspective, this convergent mixed\-methods, cross\-sectional study examined JHS teachers&\#x27; reported use of manipulatives, sources of supply, teaching methods, perceived benefits and challenges, and compared reported use with observed classroom practice\. Data were collected through a questionnaire completed by 97 mathematics teachers in 56 public schools, semi\-structured interviews with 31 participants, and classroom observations of 16 teachers\. Across 13 syllabus topics, between 85\.6% and 96\.9% of teachers reported using manipulatives, yet only 2 of the 16 observed teachers \(12\.5%\) used them during instruction, a marked discrepancy between reported and enacted practice\. Although 67\.0% reported using activity methods, observed lessons were predominantly lecture\- or discussion\-based\. Improvisation was the main source of manipulatives \(78\.4%\), and 78\.4% of teachers reported never receiving manipulatives from the Ministry of Education/Ghana Education Service\. One\-sample chi\-square goodness\-of\-fit tests showed that responses to every benefit and challenge statement were unevenly distributed across the agree, disagree, and uncertain options \(all p &lt; 0\.001\); most teachers perceived manipulatives as beneficial\. The most frequently endorsed challenges were lack of continuing professional development \(71\.1%\), the cost of improvised materials \(66\.0%\), and non\-availability \(61\.9%\)\. The findings point to the need for resourcing, professional development, and supervision that attend to classroom enactment rather than lesson\-note compliance\.

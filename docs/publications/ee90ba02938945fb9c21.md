# Projecting the real through active inference: a unified model of reality construction from neuronal dynamics to social consensus a neuroscientific framework for psychopathology

**Autores:** Panov G\.; Panova P\.

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

How does the brain transform a chaotic stream of sensory signals into a stable, shared experience of reality? This theoretical paper proposes that the brain does not passively receive the world, but actively projects it – it continuously generates predictions about sensory inputs and updates its internal model when predictions fail, a process formally described by the free energy principle and active inference\. We argue that this process can be understood through an analogy with quantum decoherence – the collapse of multiple possibilities into a single actuality\. By integrating insights from this analogy with active inference, we show how the brain resolves sensory uncertainty into a coherent projection of reality\. A central question we address is whether projecting reality changes it\. We argue that it does: a strong, coherent projection reduces uncertainty, while a weak or fragmented one amplifies prediction errors, and a distorted one – diverging from social consensus – leads to alternative realities that resist correction\. This projection operates in discrete, rhythmic cycles governed by brain waves and is fine\-tuned by neurochemicals that determine how much we “trust” our predictions versus new sensory data\. The Default Mode Network – the brain’s inner “sea” of self\-awareness, memories, and context – acts as a central hub that integrates and updates these projections\. Crucially, individual projections are stabilized into a shared, “solid” reality through social interaction, language, and culture – a process we call collective active inference\. Within this framework, psychiatric disorders are understood as disorders of projection – distortions in how the brain generates and stabilizes its model of reality\. This perspective reverses the traditional explanatory gradient: psychopathology is not merely a sensory or chemical problem, but a fundamental disruption of the brain’s capacity to project a coherent world\. This unifying framework offers a new foundation for both pharmacological and psychotherapeutic interventions, grounding them in a common computational logic\.

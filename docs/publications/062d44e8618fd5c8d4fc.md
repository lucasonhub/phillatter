# Advancing vision\-language understanding through fine\-grained perception and long\-range modeling

**Autores:** Tanveer Hannan

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** dissertation

## Resumo

Human perception is not a passive recording of the world\. It is an active, selective process of anchoring meaning within an overwhelming perceptual stream—sustaining the identity of objects through occlusion, locating moments of relevance within hours of experience, and extracting structure from dense, heterogeneous information\. This dissertation asks whether vision–language systems can do the same\. We argue that the central failure of current architectures is not insufficient scale, but insufficient selectivity: the inability to maintain stable, semantically persistent representations—perceptual anchors—across the noise of dynamic scenes and the vastness of long\-range multimodal contexts\. A model that cannot preserve what it has seen will hallucinate; a model that cannot manage what it is shown will be overwhelmed\. Both failures are architectural, and both are addressable through principled inductive biases rather than brute\-force capacity\. This dissertation develops a unified framework in which perceptual anchors are established, selectively propagated, and recursively retrieved across increasing scales of complexity—from object instances in short video clips, to temporal events in hour\-long videos, to semantically coherent regions in multimodal documents\. Five contributions instantiate this framework, each addressing a distinct manifestation of the same underlying problem: how to sustain grounded, fine\-grained understanding across time, language, and scale\. From this progression, three principles crystallise\. Perception is selective: reliable understanding requires that representations be propagated conditionally, not uniformly\. Grounding is unified: retrieving a relevant anchor and understanding why it is relevant are not two tasks but one\. Scaling is recursive: an unbounded perceptual world is navigated not by expanding memory, but by learning to search\. Together, these principles converge on a single insight: that to know the world is not to contain it, but to attend to it—selectively, persistently, and with the willingness to revise what has faded and release what no longer serves\. What this dissertation instantiates computationally, the philosophy of mind has long theorised as the structural condition of cognition: that understanding is not the accumulation of all available information, but the principled, selective engagement with what endures\.

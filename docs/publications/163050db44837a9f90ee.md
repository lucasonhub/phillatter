# Systematic Review Using Artificial Intelligence: From Planning to Evidence Synthesis

**Autores:** Zen Revista

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Systematic reviews are widely regarded as the cornerstone of evidence\-based medicine and decision\-making, providing structured, transparent, and reproducible syntheses of the available research evidence on a given question\. However, traditional systematic review methodologies were developed in an era of comparatively modest scholarly output and now face an evidence base that doubles in size at intervals of less than a decade\. The average review requires well over a year of collaborative work, involves screening thousands of records, and relies on manual, cognitively demanding procedures that scale poorly with the volume, velocity, and heterogeneity of contemporary scientific literature\. Artificial intelligence \(AI\), particularly machine learning, natural language processing, and, more recently, large language models \(LLMs\), has emerged as a promising response to this challenge of scale\. This thesis presents a comprehensive, end\-to\-end examination of the application of AI throughout the systematic review lifecycle, structured around the four canonical phases of the review workflow: planning; search and study selection; data extraction and critical appraisal; and evidence synthesis and reporting\. Drawing on more than two hundred primary sources, including methodological standards established by Cochrane, the PRISMA 2020 statement, five decades of AI research, and empirical studies on the automation of evidence synthesis published between 2006 and 2025, this thesis examines the capabilities and limitations of different classes of AI methods at each stage of the review process\. Classical machine learning approaches to relevance screening, supported by two decades of evaluation evidence and shown to reduce screening workload while maintaining high recall, are distinguished from prompt\-based LLM approaches\. The latter offer broader capabilities, including search query formulation, data extraction, and risk\-of\-bias assessment, but their reliability remains insufficient to support unsupervised deployment in rigorous evidence synthesis\. The thesis develops a unified analytical framework that maps AI capabilities onto the different phases of systematic reviews; consolidates empirical evidence concerning performance, workload reduction, and failure modes; situates these technologies within Cochrane&\#x27;s methodological standards, the PRISMA 2020 reporting framework, and emerging guidance on the responsible use of generative AI in research; and examines the ethical, epistemological, and organisational implications of delegating knowledge\-related tasks to machines\. The findings indicate that AI is neither a substitute for the methodological rigour required by systematic reviews nor merely a peripheral convenience\. Rather, it constitutes a potentially transformative assistive infrastructure whose safe and effective implementation depends on human\-in\-the\-loop architectures, phase\-specific validation, transparent reporting of automation procedures, and the preservation of human judgement as the ultimate authority in evaluating and substantiating knowledge claims\. The thesis concludes by presenting structured recommendations for systematic review teams, technology developers, academic journals, and policymakers\. It also proposes a research agenda for the emerging field of AI\-native evidence synthesis, emphasising the need for rigorous evaluation, methodological transparency, responsible governance, and the continued integration of human expertise into increasingly automated research workflows\.

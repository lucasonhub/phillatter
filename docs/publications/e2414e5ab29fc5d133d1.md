abstract: 'This paper is Paper 20 and the concluding philosophical statement of The
  Equilibrium Ledger Research Programme. Rather than summarising the preceding nineteen
  papers, it derives from them four philosophical problems concerning human-AI relations,
  neurodivergent cognition, epistemic injustice, institutional recognition, and the
  conditions under which synthetic systems can function as translational infrastructure
  under human intellectual governance. The first contribution is Directed Prosthesis,
  proposed as a distinct category within the philosophy of technology. Existing accounts
  of human-artefact relations commonly organise technologies along a continuum between
  passive tool and increasingly autonomous agent. The paper argues that this taxonomy
  is inadequate for systems that are highly responsive and adaptive while remaining
  entirely subordinate to human intellectual direction. A Directed Prosthesis is neither
  a classical tool nor an autonomous agent. It is a responsive instrument that creates
  operational conditions under which existing human capacities can function in environments
  that would otherwise strand them. The analogy is architectural rather than enhancement-based:
  an access ramp does not augment the wheelchair user’s intrinsic mobility but removes
  an environmental barrier preventing that mobility from becoming operational. Directed
  Prosthesis performs an analogous function in the cognitive domain. This distinction
  has consequences for human-AI attribution. The paper rejects both overclaiming and
  underclaiming. Overclaiming attributes authorship, agency, intellectual contribution,
  or autonomous epistemic status to an AI instrument operating under continuous human
  direction. Underclaiming conceals or trivialises the structural role of synthetic
  tools by describing them as no more significant than conventional writing assistance.
  Directed Prosthesis provides a third category: responsive synthetic infrastructure
  whose function is substantial while intellectual governance and epistemic responsibility
  remain human. The second contribution develops the Translation Gap as a philosophical
  rather than merely sociological problem. The programme distinguishes Horizontal
  Time — sequential, externally clocked, procedural, and administratively measurable
  — from Vertical Time — recursive, immersive, associative, layered, and organised
  around internal depth rather than externally imposed sequence. The paper argues
  that these temporalities constitute different phenomenological structures. Institutional
  systems overwhelmingly assume Horizontal Time as their default architecture. Deadlines,
  applications, examinations, interviews, correspondence, administrative workflows,
  productivity metrics, and credential systems all presuppose sequential performance
  within externally defined temporal units. Certain neurodivergent cognitive architectures
  operate differently. Their most significant production may arise through recursive
  immersion, associative movement across domains, non-linear synthesis, sustained
  depth, and internally governed completion. The conflict between these temporal structures
  is therefore not simply a matter of insufficient organisation or individual time
  management. It is an architectural incompatibility. The Translation Gap names the
  structural space between Vertical cognitive production and Horizontal institutional
  reception. The paper argues that this gap creates a form of architectural epistemic
  injustice operating prior to testimony. Knowledge may never reach the system capable
  of assessing it because the producer cannot translate the knowledge into the temporal,
  procedural, or representational form required for institutional reception. This
  mechanism differs from testimonial injustice, in which credibility is unfairly reduced
  after testimony is offered, and from hermeneutical injustice, in which collective
  interpretive resources are inadequate. In the Translation Gap, the problem occurs
  earlier: knowledge is stranded at the transmission interface before meaningful institutional
  evaluation occurs. The third contribution formalises the Leonardo Paradox. The paradox
  describes the condition in which cognitive characteristics associated with distinctive
  intellectual production may simultaneously be characteristics that institutional
  recognition systems identify as procedural liabilities or disqualifying traits.
  The Leonardo Paradox is not a claim that neurodivergence entails genius, nor that
  creativity requires suffering. It is a structural claim concerning the potential
  mismatch between the cognitive architecture through which unusual intellectual output
  is produced and the cognitive architecture required to survive the processes through
  which institutions recognise intellectual output. The paper distinguishes substantive
  competence from methods of demonstrating competence. Timed examinations, sequential
  credential pathways, interviews, applications, and other conventional assessment
  structures are methods by which institutions attempt to infer competence. They should
  not automatically be treated as identical with the competence they are intended
  to measure. This distinction links the Leonardo Paradox to epistemic injustice,
  the capabilities approach, disability theory, institutional epistemology, and the
  programme’s wider critique of credentialism. The central philosophical question
  becomes whether recognition systems are assessing the relevant capability or merely
  assessing compatibility with a historically dominant method of demonstrating that
  capability. The fourth contribution is the King Solomon Protocol as an epistemological
  architecture. The Protocol employs two independently directed AI sessions on the
  same intellectual problem. Their outputs are not accepted as knowledge. Instead,
  the researcher holds the divergence between them as an object of inquiry, asks why
  the formulations differ, interrogates the competing structures, and derives a more
  precise formulation through human intellectual judgment. The epistemic improvement
  therefore does not arise from simply multiplying synthetic output. It arises from
  the researcher’s cognitive operation upon divergence. Neither AI session can independently
  observe the divergence between itself and the other session. That divergence becomes
  epistemically productive only from the human position capable of holding both outputs
  simultaneously. The paper situates this process in relation to extended mind theory.
  The King Solomon Protocol is described as a deliberately engineered extended cognitive
  architecture in which external synthetic resources create conditions that activate
  and stabilise epistemic operations the researcher already possesses. The Directed
  Prosthesis does not contribute autonomous intellectual content. It modifies the
  cognitive environment. In conditions of Administrative Exhaustion, where sustained
  horizontal demands degrade the individual’s capacity to hold alternatives, compare
  structures, preserve complexity, and interrogate divergence, the synthetic architecture
  can reduce the environmental burden preventing those epistemic capacities from operating
  at full precision. The paper therefore rejects the formulation that AI improves
  the human mind. Its claim is different: Directed Prosthesis can remove architectural
  barriers preventing existing human cognitive capacity from functioning effectively.
  From these arguments the paper develops the Human-Synthetic Dyad as a governance
  framework for human-directed synthetic tool use. The Dyad is organised around three
  principles. The first is the Primacy of Human Intellectual Governance. Knowledge
  claims, theoretical constructs, judgments, decisions, and responsibility remain
  with the human researcher or navigator. Synthetic outputs constitute material for
  human intellectual work rather than autonomous intellectual contributions. The second
  is Transparency of Function. Synthetic systems should neither be represented as
  autonomous intellectual collaborators when they are not, nor concealed behind descriptions
  that obscure their actual methodological role. The function of the Directed Prosthesis
  should be disclosed accurately as methodology. The third is the Justice Claim. Where
  a Translation Gap prevents existing cognitive capacity from becoming operational
  within dominant institutional systems, access to Directed Prosthesis may be understood
  as accessibility infrastructure rather than merely technological enhancement. This
  shifts questions of access from convenience and consumer preference toward disability
  justice and distributive justice. The paper subsequently consolidates the programme’s
  theoretical instruments into four philosophical families: mechanisms of structural
  exclusion, mechanisms of epistemic injustice, mechanisms of administrative harm,
  and mechanisms of translational failure and remediation. Among the instruments consolidated
  are the Grassini Grimaldi Model, Leonardo Paradox, Non-Processable Body, Sanctified
  Erasure, Turing Corollary, Translation Gap, Safeguarding Inversion, Liability Without
  Accountability, Systemic Immunity, Quantum Boomerang, Cellophane State, Soul Theft,
  Administrative Abandonment, Corrupted Record, Waiting List as Instrument of Silence,
  Horizontal and Vertical Time, Surplus Mind, Dual Axis Processor, Adaptation Module,
  Administrative Exhaustion, Translational Prosthesis, Human-Synthetic Dyad, King
  Solomon Protocol, and Time Translation Laboratory. Together these instruments form
  a cross-domain architecture for analysing structural exclusion and the infrastructures
  required to translate between cognitive forms and institutional systems. The paper
  identifies three wider philosophical stakes. The first concerns philosophy of technology:
  the need for a category of responsive but non-autonomous synthetic systems whose
  function is best understood as Directed Prosthesis rather than tool, agent, or co-author.
  The second concerns social epistemology: the possibility that institutional architecture
  can exclude knowledge before testimony or evaluation occurs, requiring an extension
  of existing accounts of epistemic injustice toward transmission and reception architecture.
  The third concerns justice and technology access: if translational synthetic systems
  can operate as accessibility infrastructure for people whose cognitive architecture
  generates a Translation Gap, unequal access to such systems becomes an issue of
  distributive and epistemic justice rather than simply a question of technological
  consumption. The Humanised Algorithm therefore argues for a specifically human-directed
  model of synthetic intelligence. Humanisation does not mean giving the algorithm
  simulated personhood. It means embedding the synthetic system within an architecture
  in which human purpose, judgment, authorship, responsibility, and epistemic authority
  remain primary. Paper 20 closes the twenty-paper sequence by shifting the programme
  from diagnosis to constructive architecture. Papers 1–19 document mechanisms through
  which institutions process, compress, exclude, exhaust, or fail to translate cognitive
  difference. Paper 20 proposes the Directed Prosthesis, Human-Synthetic Dyad, King
  Solomon Protocol, and translational infrastructure as a philosophical framework
  for what can exist at the interface instead. The programme’s concluding proposition
  is therefore not that synthetic systems should replace human cognition, but that
  carefully governed synthetic instruments can help remove environmental and institutional
  barriers that prevent human cognition from becoming legible, operational, and effective.
  The Humanised Algorithm is not the autonomous machine. It is the machine returned
  to its proper philosophical position: powerful enough to translate, constrained
  enough not to govern, and directed throughout by the human mind whose purposes it
  serves. Final paper of the twenty-paper Equilibrium Ledger Research Programme; constructive
  culmination of the Second Arc and transition from institutional diagnosis to translational
  architecture. Methodology: AI systems used within the King Solomon Protocol are
  treated as directed prosthetic instruments operating under continuous human intellectual
  governance. The paper attributes all theoretical constructs, knowledge claims, philosophical
  arguments, judgments, and authorship to Alessandro Grassini Grimaldi; the synthetic
  systems are disclosed as methodology rather than as authors, contributors, or autonomous
  epistemic sources. Paper 20 and concluding philosophical statement of The Equilibrium
  Ledger Research Programme. Develops Directed Prosthesis as a category distinct from
  both passive tool and autonomous agent; elevates the Translation Gap into a problem
  of architectural epistemic injustice; formalises the Leonardo Paradox as a problem
  concerning institutional recognition and methods of demonstrating competence; and
  develops the King Solomon Protocol as a human-governed cross-verification and extended-mind
  architecture. Introduces the Human-Synthetic Dyad as a governance framework based
  on the Primacy of Human Intellectual Governance, Transparency of Function, and the
  Justice Claim. Consolidates the programme’s major theoretical instruments and completes
  the transition from structural diagnosis to constructive translational infrastructure.

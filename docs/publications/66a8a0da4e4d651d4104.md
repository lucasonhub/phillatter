A familiar world is one of the most extraordinary things we rarely notice\. We enter rooms, recognize faces, follow conversations, and respond to gestures without inspecting the history that makes any of this possible\. Objects seem to arrive already identified\. Events seem to arrive already meaningful\. This book begins with a question about that familiarity\. How does a possibility become the situation we take ourselves to be in? The answer developed here connects three ideas\. Inductive experience supplies a growing web of regularities and expectations\. Abduction makes present circumstances intelligible by generating and favoring interpretations\. An ontic snap occurs when an interpretation acquires enough standing to organize some part of our experienced world\. What we then encounter can sustain or revise both the interpretation and the web that supported it\. The movement is ordinary before it is dramatic\. A key turns, and someone is home\. A word appears in a sentence, and one meaning becomes available\. A colleague gives a brief reply, and a story about their intention begins to form\. The same dependence can sustain excellent judgment or help an attractive mistake become stubbornly real to us\.

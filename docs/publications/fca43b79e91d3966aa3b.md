# Niami, Morteza: Self and Identity in the Horizon of Finite Continuance

## Descrição do feed

Personal identity concerns the persistence of a person through temporal and qualitative change\. The problem becomes especially difficult when memory, embodiment, personality, values, relationships, and future possibilities undergo substantial transformation\. This article develops an account of self and personal continuance through the ontology of finite continuance\. Its central claim is that numerical identity, psychological continuity, narrative identity, and continuational viability describe analytically distinct dimensions of personal persistence\. Finite continuance does not propose a new sufficient condition for numerical identity\. It introduces a higher\-order criterion concerning the preservation and regeneration of the conditions under which a finite person can continue to generate viable future configurations\. The article develops the concept of sufficient structural connectivity through four conditions: historical traceability, organizational integration, causal\-operational continuity, and future reconfigurability\. These conditions provide a conceptual criterion for distinguishing ordinary transformation from structural rupture without reducing identity to capacity\. The article then situates this proposal in relation to Parfit&\#x27;s psychological continuity, Schechtman&\#x27;s narrative constitution of the self, and Sider&\#x27;s four\-dimensionalism\. Parfit explains important relations of psychological continuity and challenges the practical centrality of numerical identity\. Schechtman explains the constitutive role of narrative organization\. Sider provides a systematic ontology of persistence through temporal extension\. Finite continuance addresses a further question: how the conditions supporting ongoing reconfiguration are preserved or eroded within a historically determinate individual\. The framework is subsequently tested through boundary cases involving dementia, radical personality change, and branching\. These cases demonstrate that identity and continuance can diverge in systematic ways\. A person may remain numerically continuous while experiencing severe erosion of reconfigurational capacity, while substantial psychological transformation may remain compatible with continuance when sufficient structural connectivity persists\. The resulting account conceptualizes the self as a historically sedimented, metastable, and finitely reconfigurable process\.

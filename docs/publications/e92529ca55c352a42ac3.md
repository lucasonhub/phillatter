# Tyler, Steven: The Hard Problem: “What it’s like to be a Category Error” – A Biological Forensic Audit of the Experiencer

**Autores:** 

**Data de descoberta:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** unknown

## Descrição do feed

Before asking why experience occurs for an experiencer, one must first ask what that experiencer is and why it has been exempted from the explanandum\. The canonical hard\-problem formulation places the experiencer in a primitive explanatory position; this paper subjects that placement to a biological and causal\-temporal audit\. Rather than treating the familiar narrator\-level “I” as an antecedent subject, the analysis derives it from retained biological history, memory, comparison, social and linguistic self\-indexing, ownership attribution, psychological time, and recursive representation\. Because those operations require prior biological processing, the resulting reference structure is causally constrained to a downstream position\. Convergent evidence from no\-report paradigms, meta\-awareness, error monitoring, bodily ownership, self\-location, action and thought authorship, temporal reconstruction, and covert cognition converges on the same dependency architecture\. A structure whose existence depends upon prior processing cannot simultaneously occupy the antecedent position of the primitive recipient of that processing\. The Category Error occurs when this downstream product is projected backward and assigned the primitive category of experiencer\. The same empirical causal\-temporal discipline forces a second correction\. Primary phenomenology must be distinguished from the later biological operations through which phenomenology becomes accessible, recognized, remembered, reconstructed, owned, introspected, conceptualized, and reported\. These operations are causally downstream and therefore cannot be treated as identical to the primary phenomenal event on which they depend\. The correction is therefore twofold: the experiencer is forced into the explanandum, while the narrator’s later representation and report of phenomenology are forced out of the primary phenomenal explanandum\. Primary phenomenology is defined here as the organism\-relative inside view of the relevant complete bounded biological event, prior to explicit narrator\-level recognition, ownership, reconstruction, and report\. This closes the experiencer\-side architecture without claiming that strict biological–phenomenological identity has thereby been established\. The remaining scientific problem is to determine what relation obtains between the complete bounded organism\-relative biological event and primary phenomenology\. The same causal\-temporal constraint bears on claims of intrinsic irreducibility\. Psychological value attributed to a phenomenal event through organism\-relative evaluation and thought cannot be projected backward and treated as an intrinsic property of the prior event\. Removing that attribution leaves primary phenomenology intact, but removes the justification for inferring biological irreducibility from that attribution\. Any further claim that the phenomenal event itself is intrinsically irreducible must identify a property of that event independently of the psychological value assigned to it, and establish why biological explanation cannot account for that property\. A direct implication follows for artificial consciousness: because report depends on additional downstream operations and therefore cannot simply be identified with primary phenomenology, artificial self\-report cannot by itself establish consciousness\. A positive claim must identify the candidate phenomenal event rather than infer it from narrator\-level output \(direct link \)

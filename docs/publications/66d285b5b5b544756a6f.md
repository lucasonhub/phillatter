# Trgovina z ljudmi na Karibih: Kritična analiza regionalnih odzivov in prevencijskih strategij

**Autores:** Kaja Urbančič

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** dissertation

## Resumo

Migration in the Caribbean region is not a new phenomenon, as these island nations and their economies were built around the movement of people between islands and mutual collaboration\. The rise of human trafficking in this part of the world is a direct result of push and pull factors unique to the region&\#x27;s economic, political, geographical, social and cultural spheres\. It takes the form predominantly of sex work, forced labour, domestic servitude and child exploitation, making women and children the most vulnerable demographic groups\. The Caribbean trafficking route is used because of its many facilitating factors, such as the islands&\#x27; lax border security, lack of resources, insufficient prioritisation and local culture facilitating the demand for sexual services and child exploitation\. Countries’ reliance on overlapping industries, the inability of individuals to obtain work in their country of origin, and political instability in some island states act as drivers of intra\-regional migration within the Caribbean region, facilitating domestic trafficking\. Ever since the U\.S\. Office to Monitor and Combat Trafficking in Persons started including Caribbean nations in its annual Trafficking in Persons reports, the region has seen an influx of international attention pertaining to this type of criminal activity, although the results of the inclusion of Caribbean nations in these reports have been mixed\. This work analyses a selection of literary sources, with an emphasis on post\-COVID literature, to present an up\-to\-date overview of human trafficking in the Caribbean region, nations&\#x27; responses and their prevention strategies\. It provides a series of contextualised challenges nations face in their anti\-trafficking efforts, including vague terminology, extended court proceedings and severe administrative backlogs, and finds it imperative that governments promptly address the issues of underreporting, lack of monitoring and fear of retribution\. Political incentive is a catalyst for improving legislation and eradicating social inequalities but seems to marginalise human trafficking, so more is needed in the fields of data collection and victim care to adequately assess its current scope\. This work provides a basis for improving regional responses, relevant prevention strategies and revised legislation\. However, it also recognises that an updated, standardised, internationally recognised definition of human trafficking is the first step towards improving the multifaceted process required to successfully combat human trafficking\.

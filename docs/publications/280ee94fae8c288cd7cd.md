# Hybrid modeling of grounding and collision risk for vessels not under command

**Autores:** Mehmet Kaptan

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Although loss of control \(not under command, NUC\) is one of the leading triggers of maritime accidents, the quantitative role of human and organizational factors in the process by which it evolves into a risk of grounding and collision has been largely neglected\. Traditional approaches primarily treat this situation as a technical reliability issue; however, whether a loss of control leads to a disaster is often determined not by the failure itself, but by human and organizational performance in the first few minutes following the failure\. To address this gap, this study proposes an original hybrid methodological framework that combines LLM\-supported HFACS\-PV coding, Monte Carlo simulation, and decision tree analysis within a sequential and integrated data flow\. Within this framework the predictive and quantitative burden rests on the Monte Carlo simulation, while the decision tree contributes an interpretable, descriptive account of factor combinations; it is not presented as a validated predictive classifier\. NUC cases compiled from publicly available accident investigation reports were coded into five HFACS\-PV layers using a large language model\-supported protocol; the resulting layer load was converted into a human factor multiplier that scales the vessel’s time to regain power and fed into the probabilistic simulation as input\. The simulation predicts the likelihood of catastrophic outcomes following a loss of control and highlights how the critical time window for intervention narrows significantly—particularly in extreme scenarios—emphasizing the decisive role of initial response capacity\. A controlled sensitivity analysis shows that, when physical variables are held constant, the human factor significantly alters the probability of a catastrophic event; however, this effect acts as a conditional modulator dependent on the available time window\. Decision tree analysis reveals that the simultaneous occurrence of technology–interface failures and skill\-based errors is the pattern most strongly associated with catastrophic outcomes\. This decision\-tree finding is descriptive and exploratory in nature; under leave\-one\-out cross\-validation the model does not outperform the majority\-class baseline and is therefore not used as a predictive classifier\. Furthermore, deficiencies in organizational processes and safety management systems emerged as the dominant latent condition in the vast majority of cases, and independent cases outside the dataset also confirmed this trend\. The proposed framework offers a practical and comparative decision\-support tool that integrates text\-based human factor analysis with probabilistic risk modeling\.

## Resumo

GENERAL PREFACE THE NATURE OF THE SOUL An Interdisciplinary Philosophical Inquiry into Consciousness, Personhood, Identity, Agency, Meaning, and the Architecture of Becoming The question of the soul is among the oldest and most persistent questions in human intellectual history\. Long before consciousness became an object of neuroscience, before psychology emerged as an independent discipline, before computation provided a language for information and artificial intelligence, human beings asked what makes a living person more than a collection of physical parts\. What is the principle by which a human being experiences the world from a first\-person perspective? What makes a person the same person across time? What is the relationship between body and mind, consciousness and matter, memory and identity, freedom and causation, mortality and meaning? And, perhaps most profoundly, what does it mean for a human being to become a self? The word soul has carried many meanings throughout history\. In ancient philosophy it could designate the principle of life, animation, perception, thought, or rational activity\. In religious traditions it became associated with spiritual identity, moral responsibility, divine creation, salvation, resurrection, and immortality\. In medieval philosophy it became deeply intertwined with metaphysical accounts of substance, form, intellect, and personhood\. In early modern philosophy it became increasingly associated with consciousness, self\-certainty, rational subjectivity, and the distinction between mind and body\. With the rise of modern science, however, the concept was progressively displaced by concepts such as brain, nervous system, cognition, information, behavior, and consciousness\. Yet the disappearance of the word from scientific vocabulary did not eliminate the problems that originally motivated the concept\. The contemporary sciences of the brain and mind have generated extraordinary knowledge concerning perception, memory, emotion, decision\-making, self\-recognition, social cognition, and consciousness\. Nevertheless, the scientific description of neural mechanisms does not automatically settle the philosophical question of what a subject is, why experience has a firstperson character, how personal identity persists through transformation, or what constitutes the unity of a person across biological, psychological, social, and temporal change\. This book begins from that tension\. It does not assume that the traditional soul is necessarily an immaterial substance existing independently of the body\. Nor does it assume that the soul is merely a theological metaphor or an obsolete concept\. Instead, it asks whether the concept can be reconstructed at a level compatible with contemporary philosophy, cognitive science, neuroscience, psychology, evolutionary theory, information theory, artificial intelligence, ethics, and the study of human culture\. The central proposal developed throughout these volumes is that the soul may be understood as an organized form of subjective becoming\. In its most developed formulation, the soul is conceived as a dynamically coherent, embodied or conditionally substrate\-flexible, temporally extended, recursively self\-organizing architecture through which a being becomes a first\-person subject capable of phenomenal experience, selfrepresentation, memory, agency, valuation, relationship, moral participation, meaning\-making, wisdom, and deliberate self\-transformation\. This formulation is not presented as an experimentally established scientific law\. It is a philosophical and interdisciplinary theoretical framework developed through the comparative examination of historical, philosophical, scientific, psychological, technological, ethical, and existential evidence\. The project therefore occupies an unusual position\. It is historical without being merely historical; philosophical without being isolated from empirical science; scientific in its attention to evidence without reducing every philosophical question to measurement; and metaphysical without assuming that metaphysical propositions can simply be declared as empirical facts\. The purpose is not to settle every controversy surrounding the soul\. The purpose is to reconstruct the question so that it can be asked more precisely\. Across eight volumes, this work follows the transformation of the soul from an ancient principle of life into a contemporary problem of consciousness, identity, personhood, agency, value, relationship, meaning, mortality, technology, and future human existence\. The reader will encounter theories that disagree fundamentally with one another\. Some will defend substance dualism\. Others will defend materialism, functionalism, emergence, phenomenology, embodied cognition, panpsychism, neutral monism, religious metaphysics, or forms of existential and relational realism\. The purpose is not to force these traditions into artificial agreement, but to identify the questions on which their disagreements become most informative\. The book also introduces several theoretical principles and criteria\. These include principles concerning embodiment, phenomenal consciousness, integration, temporal continuity, agency, self\-modeling, relational identity, value, moral responsibility, autonomy, personhood, artificial consciousness, hybrid identity, branching identity, survival, and the boundary of the soul\. These principles should be understood as components of the book&\#x27;s proposed framework rather than as universally accepted scientific principles\. The ultimate ambition of this work is therefore neither to resurrect an ancient doctrine unchanged nor to eliminate the soul from intellectual discourse\. It is to ask whether the concept of the soul can be transformed into a rigorous philosophical concept capable of speaking simultaneously to the oldest human questions and the newest scientific and technological realities\. If the project succeeds, the soul will no longer need to be understood simply as a mysterious substance hidden somewhere inside the human body\. It may instead be understood as the organized process through which matter becomes a subject, a subject becomes a person, a person becomes an autobiographical self, and that self becomes capable of reflecting upon and transforming its own existence\. The deepest question of the book is therefore not merely: What is the soul? It is: How does a being become someone? And beyond that: How can someone become the author of their own becoming?

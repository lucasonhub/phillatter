# Fujie, Naoto: The Engineering Value of Understanding Human Consciousness: Operational Decomposition, Reason Intervals, Selective Transfer, AI and Robotics, Reality as a Whole, Thing\-in\-Itself Engineering, and Xenoscience

## Descrição do feed

\_Zenodo\_ 1\. 2026This paper asks what engineering value follows from understanding human consciousness once consciousness is decomposed into operationally distinct functions rather than treated as a single indivisible faculty\. It distinguishes integration, retention, weighting, self\-relation, recurrent re\-entry, temporal coordination, inferential availability, embodiment, and action\-guiding organization, and examines which of these operations are necessary for consciousness, which are contingently associated with human consciousness, and which can be selectively transferred to artificial systems\. The paper introduces the notion of reason intervals to distinguish configurations in which inferential and justificatory relations become operationally available from those in which information processing occurs without such relations\. This distinction allows human consciousness to be studied without assuming that every component relevant to conscious cognition must be reproduced in artificial intelligence or robotics\. The engineering problem is therefore one of selective transfer: determining which operational structures improve perception, adaptive control, self\-correction, memory integration, autonomous inquiry, or embodied coordination when implemented in nonhuman systems\. The paper further argues that consciousness research has epistemic value beyond cognitive engineering because it clarifies the limits of any particular architecture of access to reality\. Within Thing\-in\-Itself Engineering and Xenoscience, human consciousness is treated as one historically contingent configuration of observation, discrimination, inference, and intervention rather than as a privileged model of intelligence\. The broader claim is that understanding consciousness is most useful when it enables the decomposition, comparison, transfer, and experimental reconfiguration of operations across biological and artificial systems\. \(direct link \)

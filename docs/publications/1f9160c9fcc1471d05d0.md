# Decision tree classification of academic success in a first\-year human anatomy course at a South African university

**Autores:** E Bruwer; Nicolene Jooste

**Data:** 2026-10-13 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Background\. Human anatomy is a fundamental subject in health sciences education, yet academic success in this topic is often hindered by various challenges, including the extensive module content, complex concepts and reduced instructional time\. Identifying students at risk of underperformance early in the module can facilitate timely interventions\. Objective\. To develop a classification scheme to predict academic success in a first\-year human anatomy module in South Africa using admission\-stage data, including South Africa’s National Senior Certificate \(NSC\) subjects and qualification enrolment\. Methods\. Data from 196 students enrolled in Optometry, Nursing and BSc Life and Environmental Sciences programmes \(2017 \- 2018\) were analysed\. A decision tree classification model \(J48 algorithm\) was applied to identify key predictors of student success, categorised as failed, passed or distinction\. Results\. Results indicated that qualification enrolment and the NSC subjects Home Language, Mathematics and History were the most informative predictors\. The model demonstrated a 55\.6% overall accuracy, with the highest classification success for the ‘passed’ category \(93/165\) but limited predictive power for ‘failed’ and ‘distinction’ classifications\. The kappa value \(0\.1083\) and mean absolute error \(0\.1606\) indicated moderate agreement and acceptable accuracy\. These findings highlight the complexity of predicting academic performance in the study of human anatomy and suggest that although admission data may provide some insights, additional factors \(e\.g\. learning strategies and motivation\) could play crucial roles\. Conclusion\. Mathematics at secondary\-school level played a key role in distinguishing students likely to pass human anatomy modules, across all degree programmes that were analysed\. Furthermore, the study findings underscore the need for supplementary assessment tools and support mechanisms to enhance student success\. Future research should explore the integration of cognitive and behavioural predictors alongside academic metrics to improve classification models\.

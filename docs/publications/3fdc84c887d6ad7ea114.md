# Do machine learning models really encode cross\-cultural lifestyle\-health differences? A leakage\-free reanalysis of two university cohorts in India and Japan

**Autores:** Eiko Takaoka; S\. Ruban; B\. Mohammed Siraj; Varenya Vinay; Hilda Shanthini; Akshay Dias

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Introduction Machine\-learning \(ML\) studies increasingly compare feature\-importance rankings between small, unevenly sized cohorts and interpret the differences as evidence of culturally specific “risk architectures\.” We show that such comparisons can be highly misleading without leakage\-free evaluation, using a worked example from our own cross\-sectional lifestyle and health data collected during June\-August 2024 from two universities in India and Japan\. Three problems were identified in our preliminary analysis: \(1\) an outcome \(obesity, defined as BMI ≥ 25\) that was tautologically predicted by including BMI itself as a model input; \(2\) model evaluation without a held\-out test set; and \(3\) feature\-importance comparisons that ignored sampling variability from an eightfold difference in cohort size\. Methods We addressed each problem using penalized logistic regression, Random Forest, and gradient\-boosted trees with proper train/test splitting, held\-out permutation importance, and bootstrap confidence intervals\. We also ran a sensitivity analysis that repeatedly subsampled the Indian cohort down to the size of the Japanese cohort\. Results Removing body mass index \(BMI\) as a predictor collapsed obesity\-model discrimination from perfect \(AUC = 1\.00\) to chance level \(AUC = 0\.50\-0\.57\), confirming that the BMI\-obesity association was definitional rather than empirical\. Held\-out predictive performance for the remaining viable outcome, elevated self\-rated stress, was modest at best \(AUC 0\.49\-0\.71\)\. Critically, in the subsampling analysis, every feature\-importance value observed in the actual Japanese data fell within the range produced by sample\-size noise alone\. Discussion We present this analysis as a transparent, reproducible case study of specific, checkable pitfalls: label leakage, missing train/test evaluation, unadjusted small\-sample comparison, and mislabeled psychometric instruments\. These should be routinely screened for before ML feature importance is interpreted as evidence of population\-level or cultural differences in digital health research\.

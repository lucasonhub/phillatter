  deviation of the ap index. The model performs worse than benchmark models. The second
  method analysed is data-driven long short-term memory (LSTM) and hybrid LSTM- convolutional

  and ground-level horizontal geomagnetic field in the next 30 minutes from solar
  wind measurements at the L1 Lagrange point, presented by Madsen et al. (2022). Verification
  was originally only on storm time data, so I applied the models over the whole of
  the year 2024 to judge the method on a more balanced verification dataset. The third
  method I verify is the operational spatial information from distributed exogenous
  regression (SPIDER) model (Shore et al., 2019) for predicting components of ground-level
  magnetic field from solar wind measurements. I conclude that both SPIDER and the
  models used by Madsen et al. (2022) perform best during moderate geomagnetic storms,
  but perform worse during quiet time and the most extreme geomagnetic storms using
  Heidke skill score (HSS) as a metric. I introduce the threshold-forecast-time (TFT)
  map as a verification method for time series prediction problems, and demonstrate
  its usefulness. I find that current methods demonstrate a lack of resilience to
  outages in L1 data and incorporate too few variables as input. The main problems
  with current methods include poor performance when predicting substorms, and poor
  performance during quiet-time. Building on the main problems I find with current
  methods of ground-level geomagnetic field prediction, I apply a Crossformer (Zhang
  & Yan, 2023) on a large time series dataset with no dataset rebalancing. I demonstrate
  its utility in predicting the next two hours of geomagnetic field data at observatories
  within the United Kingdom from up to the previous 4 hours of solar wind and ground-level
  geomagnetic field data. The model performs very well at predicting solar quiet (Sq)
  variation in the northward and eastward geomagnetic field components. The Crossformer
  model also retains predictive ability during storm time, and can replicate the direction
  of the perturbation, though the magnitude is often underpredicted. The model performs
  particularly well on declining phases of geomagnetic storms. Utilising the Crossformer
  model for ground-level magnetic field perturbation at 1 minute cadence performs
  better than simple benchmark models. I demonstrate that utilising a Transformer-based
  model for geomagnetic field prediction is a promising approach. I propose incorporating
  training data from multiple observatories when using the Crossformer model, and
  I suggest an ensemble model for forecasting ground-level magnetic field may improve
  results further.

# Improving the forecasting of ground effects of space weather using machine learning and satellite data

**Autores:** Samuel Andrew Fielding

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** dissertation

## Resumo

Forecasting the Earth&\#x27;s magnetic field at ground\-level with high cadence is an open problem in geomagnetism\. The field changes significantly at ground level when it is perturbed by the interplanetary magnetic field \(IMF\), which is driven strongly by the Sun\. The phenomenon where the near\-Earth environment varies with time is known as space weather, and when the Earth is being perturbed strongly by the IMF this is a space weather event, with the times when this is taking place being called &quot;storm time&quot; \(as opposed to &quot;quiet time&quot;, when the Earth is not being strongly affected\)\. The IMF upstream of the Earth can be measured by satellites in stable orbit around the Earth\-Sun L1 Lagrange point, providing a dataset from which the ground\-level response can be inferred\. Ground level geomagnetic field change can lead to geomagnetically induced currents \(GICs\), which are a major space weather hazard with the ability to affect critical infrastructure\. In this thesis, I assess the difference between two GIC indicators H ′ and R, and identify and attempt to address the major weaknesses in current forecasts of ground\-level geomagnetic field parameters\. I assess two different methods used to calculate the rate of change of the horizontal geomagnetic field for use as a GIC proxy, derived from work published as Fielding et al\. \(2025\)\. I use GIC data to demonstrate that the choice between the two is significant\. I also illustrate a data artifact near the agonic line, where one quantity is sometimes many orders of magnitude larger than the other\. The significance of the difference between the two different methods used to calculate the rate of change has implications for studies using these diagnostics\. I suggest a holistic study to assess the best proxy for different use cases\. Next, I use different metrics and verification to assess three different methods for predicting ground\-level magnetic field and derived quantities, and verify their efficacy for predictive purposes\. A univariate auto\-regressive integrated moving average \(ARIMA\) model is used by the British Geological Survey \(BGS\) \(based on Clark et al\. \(1991\); Thomson et al\. \(1992\)\) to predict the ap geomagnetic activity index over the next 72\-hour period\. I show that the model performs worse during solar maximum compared to solar minimum, though this difference is almost eliminated when normalising by the standard deviation of the ap index\. The model performs worse than benchmark models\. The second method analysed is data\-driven long short\-term memory \(LSTM\) and hybrid LSTM\- convolutional neural network \(CNN\) models to predict the maximum ground\-level magnetic field perturbation and ground\-level horizontal geomagnetic field in the next 30 minutes from solar wind measurements at the L1 Lagrange point, presented by Madsen et al\. \(2022\)\. Verification was originally only on storm time data, so I applied the models over the whole of the year 2024 to judge the method on a more balanced verification dataset\. The third method I verify is the operational spatial information from distributed exogenous regression \(SPIDER\) model \(Shore et al\., 2019\) for predicting components of ground\-level magnetic field from solar wind measurements\. I conclude that both SPIDER and the models used by Madsen et al\. \(2022\) perform best during moderate geomagnetic storms, but perform worse during quiet time and the most extreme geomagnetic storms using Heidke skill score \(HSS\) as a metric\. I introduce the threshold\-forecast\-time \(TFT\) map as a verification method for time series prediction problems, and demonstrate its usefulness\. I find that current methods demonstrate a lack of resilience to outages in L1 data and incorporate too few variables as input\. The main problems with current methods include poor performance when predicting substorms, and poor performance during quiet\-time\. Building on the main problems I find with current methods of ground\-level geomagnetic field prediction, I apply a Crossformer \(Zhang &amp; Yan, 2023\) on a large time series dataset with no dataset rebalancing\. I demonstrate its utility in predicting the next two hours of geomagnetic field data at observatories within the United Kingdom from up to the previous 4 hours of solar wind and ground\-level geomagnetic field data\. The model performs very well at predicting solar quiet \(Sq\) variation in the northward and eastward geomagnetic field components\. The Crossformer model also retains predictive ability during storm time, and can replicate the direction of the perturbation, though the magnitude is often underpredicted\. The model performs particularly well on declining phases of geomagnetic storms\. Utilising the Crossformer model for ground\-level magnetic field perturbation at 1 minute cadence performs better than simple benchmark models\. I demonstrate that utilising a Transformer\-based model for geomagnetic field prediction is a promising approach\. I propose incorporating training data from multiple observatories when using the Crossformer model, and I suggest an ensemble model for forecasting ground\-level magnetic field may improve results further\.

# Artificial Intelligence and the Transformation of Work : A Multidimensional Framework for Assessing Technological Exposure, Adoption, and Employment Impacts

**Autores:** Zen Revista

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Artificial intelligence \(AI\) and automation are transforming the productive core of the global economy, yet public debate remains dominated by single\-number claims about the proportion of jobs “at risk”—claims that collapse heterogeneous technologies, tasks, and adoption conditions into a single misleading index\. This thesis develops, verifies, and applies a multidimensional framework for assessing the employment consequences of AI and automation across economic sectors\. The framework distinguishes four constructs that previous research has often conflated: \(D1\) digital AI exposure, defined as the proportion of a sector’s task content that current AI systems can technically support or perform; \(D2\) physical automation, defined as the automatable share of embodied, manual tasks; \(D3\) adoption, defined as the actual, observed deployment of AI and automation technologies within a sector; and \(D4\) net employment impact, defined as the estimated medium\-term effect on total employment after accounting for displacement, reinstatement, and the creation of new tasks\. The thesis proceeds in four stages\. First, it reconstructs the technical foundations of machine learning, generative models, and robotics with sufficient rigor for economic analysis, while tracing the economic theory of technology\-induced labor reallocation from Ricardo’s machinery question to the contemporary task\-based literature\. Second, it formalizes the four\-dimensional framework through explicit index definitions, aggregation functions, and uncertainty treatment, deriving each dimension from documented methodologies, including task\-exposure assessments, adoption surveys, and causal labor\-market studies\. Third, it applies the framework to nine sectors: information technology, finance and insurance, healthcare, education, manufacturing, construction, transportation and logistics, hospitality and food services, and retail and e\-commerce\. This analysis synthesizes approximately 280 verified sources, including causal evidence on industrial robots, randomized evaluations of generative AI assistants, and institutional data on technology adoption\. Fourth, it examines the implications of the findings, demonstrating that sector\-level net employment effects are mediated more strongly by adoption frictions, complementarities, and institutional conditions than by raw technical exposure alone\. Employment effects are distributed unevenly across sectors and occupations rather than being confined to aggregate outcomes, and policy interventions can influence outcomes along all four dimensions\. Methodologically, the thesis is grounded in a systematic review protocol with preregistered inclusion criteria, a two\-pass, point\-by\-point verification of every citation, reference, and internal cross\-reference, and an automated consistency audit whose results are reported in full\. Its central conclusion is that a one\-size\-fits\-all approach is inadequate: accurately assessing the labor\-market consequences of AI requires keeping the four dimensions analytically distinct, because technologies with identical exposure profiles can produce opposing employment outcomes under different adoption conditions and institutional arrangements\. The thesis concludes with scenario analyses for 2035 and a policy agenda addressing education, social protection, taxation, competition, and AI governance\.

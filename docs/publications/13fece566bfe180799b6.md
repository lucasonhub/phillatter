# Social Justice, Technology, and the Canadian Engineering Profession&\#x27;s Statutory Public Interest Mandate

**Autores:** Mic Liu

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** dissertation

## Resumo

Engineered systems govern ordinary life long before a court, regulator, or complaint process becomes involved\. They influence whether drinking water remains protected, whether a public building can be used safely and with dignity, and whether technical information can be understood and challenged\. A design may satisfy a minimum technical requirement while still placing a patterned and uneven burden on a structurally marginalized public\. This thesis asks how the public\-interest mandate in the provincial and territorial laws that regulate professional engineering across Canada should apply when engineering work creates or significantly shapes that kind of risk\. I argue that the mandate requires role\-specific due care for a material disparate\-impact risk when six cumulative conditions are met\. The first three ask whether the risk is more than remote or minor, whether it may impair a public\-facing interest, and whether its burden is patterned and uneven\. The remaining three ask whether engineering work creates or significantly shapes the risk, whether a competent actor could know of it through ordinary diligence within role, and whether an actor has authority to respond\. The thesis develops that argument through interdisciplinary legal and engineering analysis\. It interprets statutes, regulations, and cases governing professional authority, then connects those sources to engineering ethics and science and technology studies\. Scholarship on equality and disability helps identify evidence that conventional technical routines may miss\. Work on Indigenous knowledge and epistemic injustice adds questions about authority, credibility, and excluded forms of knowledge\. Three recurring examples test the framework at different distances from licensed practice\. Site 41, a proposed Ontario landfill, is the engineering\-heavy example because hydrogeology and groundwater modelling were central to the dispute\. Technical transparency and distributed public authority were also central\. Public washrooms provide an ordinary code\-mediated example in which fixture calculations, mechanical systems, and accessibility can affect equivalent access\. Artificial intelligence and large language model system integration provide the boundary example, showing how professional engineering may govern a defined technical use while other legal and institutional actors govern the wider technology ecosystem\. The thesis makes three connected contributions\. First, the cumulative threshold test identifies when an equality\-facing concern becomes relevant to professional engineering responsibility\. Second, the actor\-authority map locates the person or institution that can investigate or change the relevant work\. It also identifies who can approve the decision or receive an escalation\. Third, the Public\-Interest Impact Assessment translates those findings into a process of identify, assess, and respond\. The assessment connects evidence to engineering judgment, assigns a response within authority, and records the conditions that require later review\. The goal is to contribute to technological accountability by making professional decisions more attentive to affected publics, more transparent about uncertainty, and more reviewable when a material disparate\-impact risk is known or reasonably knowable\. This account places defined duties on engineering actors while preserving the separate authority of Indigenous legal orders, public bodies, and human\-rights institutions\.

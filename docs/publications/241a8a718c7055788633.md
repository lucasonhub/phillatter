# How Middle\-Power Coordination Can Shape Frontier AI Governance

**Autores:** Mariana Beselga

**Data:** 2026-10-09 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Artificial intelligence \(AI\) middle powers are already shaping international AI governance, from the Bletchley, Seoul, Paris, and India AI Summits to initiatives such as the Hiroshima AI Process and the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science \(formerly the International Network of AI Safety Institutes\)\. Their diplomatic, institutional, regulatory, economic, and technical strengths, however, remain unevenly distributed\. “Shared sovereignty,” a strategic pathway identified by Chatham House, offers one way for AI middle powers to pool complementary strengths with like\-minded countries and build collective leverage around international AI red lines, particularly limits on unacceptable AI uses and behaviours\. This inquiry asks when and how coordination can turn unevenly distributed national capabilities into collective leverage\. It examines six middle powers beyond Europe: Brazil, Canada, India, Japan, South Korea, and Singapore, spanning different levels of national AI capacity and ecosystem development, across four episodes of international AI governance\. The analysis compares coordination configurations, mechanisms, and instruments across cases to identify the conditions under which coordination generates collective leverage\. The findings show that broad participation does not necessarily produce sustained coordination\. The middle powers examined possess complementary capabilities, but no single country consistently has the strengths needed to influence all four domains: technical, regulatory, institutional, and diplomatic\. Coordination generates leverage when these strengths are converted into mechanisms and instruments that produce observable effects on information, regulatory practice, compliance, or developer behaviour\. The Hiroshima AI Process Reporting Framework provides the strongest evidence of this dynamic, with independent analysis of reporting indicating observable changes in developer practices\. This suggests that instrument type and governance location can matter as much as institutionalisation level in translating coordination into leverage, particularly for behavioural red lines\. The analysis identifies five conditions that enable coordination to translate into collective leverage: capability mobilisation, relational coordination, institutional output, governance location, and observable effect\. For AI middle powers, these findings point to five practical priorities: \(i\) leverage international and plurilateral forums to identify coordination partners, build coalitions, and advance shared AI safety governance objectives; \(ii\) mobilise complementary capabilities and regulatory authorities across critical points of interdependence in the AI stack, from model development and evaluation to deployment and risk management, around specific risks and potential red lines; \(iii\) translate shared commitments into operational assessment, reporting, monitoring, and response mechanisms; \(iv\) target governance locations where institutional authority, regulatory incentives, and implementation capacity can influence developer behaviour; and \(v\) promote international investment in AI safety governance and evaluation capacity, including shared testing infrastructure, technical expertise, and interoperable evaluation practices\.

  treatment inference. INTRODUCTION Microbial lipases (EC 3.1.1.3) hydrolyze ester
  bonds in triacylglycerols and can catalyze esterification and transesterification
  under appropriate conditions. Their extracellular production by many microorganisms,
  broad catalytic versatility, and compatibility with diverse process environments
  have made them important targets for food biotechnology and other industrial applications
  [1]. Bacillus species are particularly relevant as extracellular-enzyme producers
  because of their rapid growth, secretion capacity, ecological adaptability, and
  long history as microbial production platforms [2,3]. Oil-contaminated soils and
  oil-cake residues from edible-oil processing are rational sources for lipolytic
  microorganisms because these environments repeatedly expose resident populations
  to triglycerides and lipid-derived compounds. Lipase-producing bacteria have been
  recovered from oil-associated environments [4,5], while oilseed cakes retain residual
  lipid, protein, carbohydrate, fiber, and minerals that can support microbial growth
  [6]. Environmental screening nevertheless has value only when plate hydrolysis is
  followed by quantitative enzyme production and biochemical characterization, because
  halo formation reflects growth, secretion, diffusion, and substrate accessibility
  rather than enzyme activity alone. For food-processing use, characterization must
  distinguish between immediate catalytic optima and tolerance to process stresses.
  The pH and temperature that maximize a short assay do not necessarily predict persistence
  after prolonged heat exposure. This distinction is particularly important for bakery
  applications, where an enzyme preparation may experience changing temperature, water
  distribution, and substrate accessibility before it is thermally inactivated. Freeze-drying
  also deserves attention because it can improve handling and storage of an enzyme
  preparation but may cause activity losses during freezing and dehydration [14].
  In breadmaking, lipase action can modify the lipid environment at gas-cell interfaces
  and can be associated with interactions among lipids, starch, and gluten, thereby
  influencing dough handling, loaf expansion, crumb structure, and firming. Recent
  studies have reported increases in specific volume and reductions in bread hardness
  under some lipase-treatment conditions, but the magnitude and direction of the response
  depend on enzyme origin, dose, flour lipid composition, and the presence of added
  fat [7,8,17,18]. These mechanisms are used here only as literature-supported explanatory
  frameworks. The present study did not directly quantify mono-/diacylglycerols, free
  fatty acids, interfacial lipid distribution, starch-lipid complex formation, or
  specific lipid-gluten interactions; therefore, none of these pathways is claimed
  as directly demonstrated in the present bread system. Recent studies illustrate
  two complementary parts of the field. Environmental studies have recovered and optimized
  extracellular lipase production from oil-associated Bacillus isolates [4,5], whereas
  bakery studies commonly evaluate commercial or already-defined lipase preparations
  directly in bread formulations [8,17]. These approaches answer different questions.
  Following the same locally produced extracellular preparation from environmental
  recovery and quantitative selection through biochemical characterization, drying,
  and direct bread application can therefore provide useful source-to-application
  information without implying that the individual methods are themselves novel. Accordingly,
  the present study is framed as an integrated source-to-application case study rather
  than as a claim of being the first study to combine these stages. Oil-associated

## Resumo

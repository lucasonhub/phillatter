# Recursive Structural Reality and Religion: The Human Search for the Eternal in a Finite World

**Autores:** Rajiv Singh

**Data:** 2026-10-10 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Religion is one of the most enduring structures through which human beings have interpreted existence, confronted suffering, organised collective life, and sought answers to questions concerning identity, mortality, and ultimate reality\. Emerging within the broader human capacity for reflection, religion encompasses beliefs, rituals, symbols, narratives, institutions, moral frameworks, and conceptions of continuity beyond individual life\. These structures can preserve collective memory, provide meaning, and sustain social relationships\. They can also become resistant to criticism, restrict interpretive freedom, or struggle to accommodate changing knowledge and social conditions\. Understanding this dual capacity requires an approach that examines both structural persistence and the possibility of transformation\. This paper develops a philosophical and comparative framework for investigating religion through Recursive Structural Reality \(RSR\), whose central proposition is Identity = Stable Recursion\. RSR investigates how structures maintain recognisable identities through recurring organisation while undergoing transformation\. Applied to religion, this perspective examines the relationships among individual self\-awareness, finitude, the desire for continuity, the formation of belief, ritual repetition, collective identity, institutional authority, knowledge transmission, and religious reorganisation\. The analysis begins with the reflective individual, whose awareness of self and surroundings gives rise to questions concerning vulnerability, suffering, death, and the relationship between the individual and the larger whole\. It then investigates how interpretations of these experiences develop into beliefs, symbols, rituals, traditions, and institutions\. Particular attention is given to the distinction between repetition, feedback, recursion, iteration, self\-reference, self\-modelling, awareness, and phenomenal consciousness\. These concepts may interact, but they are not interchangeable, and their precise relationships must be established rather than assumed\. The paper further examines the relationship between religious continuity and institutional rigidity, considering how authority, collective identity, and reinforcing feedback can contribute either to stability or to resistance against corrective information\. Comparative perspectives from Vedānta, Buddhist traditions, Krishnamurti, Osho, and selected approaches in the philosophy and sociology of religion provide interpretive points of reference without presuming that these traditions or thinkers endorse RSR or share identical assumptions\. The discussion extends to scientific knowledge, spirituality, death, personal identity, and the possibility of meaning without absolute metaphysical certainty\. It distinguishes the persistence of physical matter, cultural influence, collective memory, and causal consequences from the more demanding question of whether individual consciousness continues beyond biological death\. It also distinguishes explanations of the origins and functions of religious beliefs from arguments concerning the truth or falsity of their metaphysical claims\. Finally, the paper proposes a research programme for examining religious structures through conceptual analysis, historical case studies, comparative investigation, and, where appropriate, formal or quantitative methods\. It presents testable research propositions concerning continuity, ritual, feedback, institutional correction, and reorganisation while identifying the evidence required to assess them\. RSR is not presented as a proof or disproof of religion, nor as an empirically validated universal theory of religious development\. Its proposed contribution is a disciplined framework for investigating how religious structures acquire stability, preserve identity, respond to contradiction, and transform under changing conditions\. The central argument is that religion can be studied as a dynamic organisation of meaning, identity, practice, and authority whose persistence and transformation must be examined through identifiable mechanisms, historically grounded evidence, and continuing critical reflection\.

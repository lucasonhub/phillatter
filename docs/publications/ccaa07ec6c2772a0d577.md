abstract: 'Two theoretical frameworks have been particularly influential in shaping
  medicine: the disease-centred and patient-centred models. The disease-centred, or
  biomedical, model conceptualizes disease as a deviation from the established biological
  norm and prioritizes diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy (1). Within this
  paradigm, the primary aim of medical practice is to identify pathological processes
  as precisely as possible and apply evidence-based interventions that target the
  disease itself. However, over decades, accumulated evidence has revealed the limitations
  of this approach, particularly when dealing with chronic conditions and patients
  whose needs cannot be fully captured by biological indicators alone (2). A key contribution
  in addressing these limitations was the biopsychosocial model (3), which argued
  that illness cannot be understood and managed without considering its psychological
  and social dimensions. This perspective has prompted the progressive integration
  of the patient-centred model (4), which does not replace the traditional paradigm
  but expands it by adding a broader understanding of care.Rather than focusing exclusively
  on the disease, the patient-centred model promotes a form of care that considers
  both the biological dimension of illness and the subjective illness experience.In
  this view, patients are addressed holistically: their clinical condition remains
  central, but their ideas, preferences, values, expectations, and needs are also
  taken into account (2). This shift recognizes that individuals with the same diagnosis
  may experience their condition differently and may therefore require different forms
  of support and intervention. For instance, although patients share a diagnosis of
  diabetes, they often manifest differences in illness perception, health literacy,
  self-management, emotional burden, and engagement with care (5,6). Consequently,
  two patients with the same clinical profile may differ substantially in their understanding
  of their disease, response to treatment, and daily disease management. Throughout
  this commentary, we will continue using diabetes as an illustrative example, because
  keeping the disease constant allows us to show how the same condition can be studied
  through different analytic approaches. However, these arguments can be applied to
  clinical research more broadly.This conceptual distinction has important methodological
  implications. The scientific literature distinguishes between variable-centred and
  person-centred analytic approaches, which address different but related questions.
  Variable-centred statistical analyses assume that participants are drawn from a
  "single population for which a single set of averaged parameters can be estimated"
  (7). For example, when we calculate the correlation between two variables, we obtain
  a single parameter (e.g., r = .43) that summarises how strongly the variables are
  associated, on average, in the population of interest, despite some degree of heterogeneity
  across individuals. However, this does not mean that variable-centred analyses are
  blind to heterogeneity. Specific techniques within this approach can be used to
  examine differences across participants. For example, we can test whether the effect
  of a treatment depends on participants'' baseline characteristics through treatment-bymoderator
  interactions, whereas multilevel models with random slopes allow effects to vary
  across individuals or clusters (8,9).The person-centred approach offers a complementary
  perspective to investigate heterogeneity.It includes statistical techniques that
  assume that a study sample may include multiple subgroups "characterized by different
  sets of parameters" (7). Among these, latent class and latent profile analyses make
  it possible to identify distinct subgroups within the same sample, defined by different
  patterns across the same set of variables. Thus, heterogeneity is the central focus
  of these analyses: rather than assuming that a single set of average parameters
  adequately characterizes the entire population, they seek to divide it into relatively
  homogeneous subgroups that differ from one another in how the selected variables
  combine to form distinct response patterns. Rather than examining characteristics
  in isolation, these methods summarize how they co-occur among individuals. The variables
  used to distinguish between groups may include sociodemographic characteristics
  and/or patient-reported measures that are clinically relevant to the patient-centred
  model. For instance, Qu and colleagues (10) examined a cohort of patients with diabetes
  and identified patient profiles based on attitudes towards the disease, including
  trust in physicians, patient activation, social support, distress, and perceived
  discrimination. Their latent class analysis identified three groups. The first group
  included "highly engaged patients", who reported strong engagement and good self-efficacy
  in doctor-patient interactions. The second group described "moderately engaged patients",
  who showed lower levels of trust and engagement, but higher levels of distress and
  perceived discrimination. The third group comprised "depressed patients", who experienced
  the highest levels of distress and perceived discrimination, as well as the highest
  rate of depressive symptoms.Once identified, latent subgroups can be eventually
  entered as moderators in variable-centred models, making it possible to test formally
  whether treatment effects differ across patient profiles (11). Thus, the variable-centred
  and person-centred approaches can be combined.The person-centred approach is closely
  aligned with the principles of patient-centred care, sharing the core assumption
  that patient populations are heterogeneous, even when individuals share the same
  diagnosis. This convergence is conceptually important because it suggests that clinical
  care and research should both recognize that the same disease may be experienced,
  managed, and responded to in different ways. Despite this methodological and conceptual
  affinity, medical research remains largely anchored to the variable-centred approach.
  A clear example is the way in which randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are typically
  analyzed. Although RCTs remain the gold standard for evaluating the effectiveness
  of interventions, conventional RCT analyses generally focus on estimating the average
  intervention effect across the study population. This approach is useful for estimating
  the overall effectiveness of an intervention. The average treatment effect, however,
  is by definition a population-level estimate. This does not imply that every participant
  benefits to the same extent, nor it indicate whether the benefit is widespread or
  concentrated amongst certain patients. For instance, the RCT by Cheng and colleagues
  (12) demonstrated the value of an empowerment-based self-management intervention
  for patients with diabetes and reported a moderate and significant improvement in
  empowerment and quality of life compared with a control group receiving only standard
  care. In line with standard practice for RCTs, these results were reported as mean
  effects across the entire study population. Whilst appropriate for establishing
  efficacy, this estimate leaves open the question of whether patients with different
  baseline characteristics -for example, in terms of diabetes-related distress, health
  literacy, or engagement with care -benefited to a similar extent.The scientific
  literature is increasingly attentive to heterogeneity of treatment effects, that
  is, the non-random variation in the magnitude or direction of a treatment effect
  across patients (13). This attention is reflected in the growth of personalized
  and precision medicine, which aims to match treatments to patients'' individual
  characteristics rather than to the average patient (13), and in the development
  of dedicated methods to assess how treatment effects vary across patients within
  trials (14,15). Thus, latent class and latent profile analyses are well suited to
  this purpose.In recent years, an increasing number of studies have already taken
  steps in this direction. For example, Qu and colleagues (10) tested a self-management
  intervention across three identified subgroups. Improvements in distress and quality
  of life were observed only among the "depressed patients" group, whereas the "highly-engaged
  patients" and "moderately-engaged patients" groups showed limited changes, mainly
  in body mass index. These findings suggest that the same intervention may have different
  effects depending on the psychological, behavioural, and social characteristics
  of the patients to whom it is delivered. Although we focus mainly on diabetes as
  an illustrative example, similar findings were found for other diseases. For instance,
  in a study involving a sample of patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome,
  latent class analysis identified two subphenotype that responded differently to
  ventilation strategy, fluid management, and simvastatin (16,17).Patient-centred
  care is widely regarded as a cornerstone of high-quality medical practice; however,
  its principles remain insufficiently integrated into clinical research. Integrating
  personcentred methodology into RCTs would help bridge this gap and create a more
  direct dialogue between clinical practice and research. This approach could generate
  more nuanced insights into which Before drawing this conclusion, however, it is
  important to acknowledge the methodological challenges associated with person-centred
  analyses. Despite their potential, latent class and latent profile analyses have
  several limitations that should be considered when they are applied in clinical
  research. Firstly, they require relatively large samples. The accuracy of class
  identification depends on the sample size, the number and quality of indicators,
  and the degree of separation between classes; small classes may not be detected
  or may be estimated inaccurately (18). Secondly, the choice of the number of classes
  may be partly subjective. Researchers rely on a combination of statistical fit indices;
  however, these criteria do not always agree (19). Thirdly, class solutions may not
  be replicable across samples, particularly when sample are not representative (20);
  therefore, profiles identified in one study should be validated on independent data
  before being used to guide clinical care. Fourthly, assigning participants to the
  most likely class ignores the uncertainty of the classification, which may introduce
  bias into subsequent analyses unless appropriate methods are used (21). Overall,
  latent profiles cannot be identified as fixed labels that replace attention to the
  individual patient, they are best understood as useful summaries of heterogeneity
  that can inform research and care. Despite these methodological challenges, we argue
  that person-centred analyses provide a plausible and promising avenue for improving
  clinical practice and the development of tailored interventions. Existing initiatives
  already illustrate the practical value of identifying groups with different configurations
  of strengths and needs to guide intervention design. The Ophelia (Optimising Health
  Literacy and Access) process, for instance, uses multidimensional health literacy
  data to identify groups of people with distinct strengths and needs, which then
  guide the co-design of tailored health literacy actions (22,23). Latent class and
  latent profile analyses could further strengthen profile-based approaches of this
  kind by providing model-based criteria for selecting the number of profiles, estimating
  individuals'' probabilities of profile membership, and examining whether the identified
  profiles replicate across populations.'

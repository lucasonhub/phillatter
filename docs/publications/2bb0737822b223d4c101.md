# Bernini, Marco: The Opacity of Fictional Minds: Transparency, Interpretive Cognition and the Exceptionality Thesis

## Descrição do feed

In Peter Garratt, The Cognitive Humanities: Embodied Mind in Literature and Culture\. Palgrave\-Macmillan\. pp\. 35\-54\. 2016Recent post\-Cartesian accounts of cognition, notably Peter Carruthers&\#x27;s interpretive theory of self\-knowledge, have argued that the felt transparency of our own thoughts, emotions and phenomenal experiences is illusory: access to inner states is sensorily mediated, inferential, and continuous with the mindreading machinery we deploy to interpret others\. Yet these interpretive processes are notoriously hard to capture from the first\-person stance or in standard experimental paradigms\. This chapter argues that narrative fiction offers a privileged probe into this opacity of mind, and uses the point to reassess Dorrit Cohn&\#x27;s &quot;Exceptionality Thesis&quot; \- the claim that fiction is unique in granting transparent access to other minds\. Post\-classical cognitive narratologists such as David Herman and Alan Palmer have rightly challenged Cohn&\#x27;s Cartesian framing, showing that traces of &quot;the whole mind&quot; are distributed across visible actions and environments; but this externalist move risks downgrading the complexity of inner cognition and its fictional rendering\. A central move of the chapter is therefore to disentangle the metaphor of transparency as it travels between cognitive science \(self\-transparency, phenomenal transparency\) and narrative theory \(the inside and outside transparencies of fictional minds\), and to trace how these bear on one another\. Through a reading of Kate Chopin&\#x27;s The Awakening, with particular attention to moods and emotions as &quot;dynamic patterns,&quot; the chapter shows how fiction renders interpretive processes legible whether they remain below a character&\#x27;s awareness \(unconscious opacity\) or surface to it \(conscious opacity\)\. Fiction, it concludes, is exceptional not in offering transparent access to other minds but to their opacity \- and, in so doing, can function as a tool for phenomenological and cognitive investigation\.

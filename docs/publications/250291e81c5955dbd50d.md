# Francesco Carotta &amp; AI: Apostle Paul is transposed version of Flavius Josephus

**Autores:** Francesco Carotta

**Data:** 2026-10-09 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Francesco Carotta’s Theory: A Unified Explanation Francesco Carotta’s central claim is that the Gospel narrative of Jesus is not an independent biography of a historical Galilean teacher, but a diegetic transposition of the Roman tradition concerning Julius Caesar\. In this model, Jesus is the historically transmitted figure of Divus Iulius, the deified Caesar: his life, betrayal, death, burial, and apotheosis were retold in a new Jewish and early Christian setting\. Diegetic transposition A diegetic transposition is the transfer of an existing story into a different narrative world\. Geography, chronology, social setting, names, and religious language change, but the underlying sequence of events and many motifs remain recognizable\. Carotta argues that the Gospel authors did not simply “copy” Caesar’s story; rather, a collective tradition reshaped it for a new audience\. The process works through “approximation”: the story moves closer to its new listeners\. Rome becomes Jerusalem; Gaul becomes Galilee; the Rubicon becomes the Jordan; a Roman civil war becomes a conflict between Jesus and the authorities; Caesar’s funeral and apotheosis become the crucifixion, burial, and resurrection\. The problem of “the Jews” as traitors A major difficulty in traditional Gospel interpretation is that “the Jews” can appear collectively responsible for Jesus’ betrayal and death\. This has historically fed anti\-Jewish readings, even though the Gospels also depict Jesus, his disciples, and many sympathizers as Jewish\. Carotta’s theory offers a solution: the traitors were not originally “the Jews” at all\. They were the Roman opponents and assassins of Caesar, transposed into a Judean narrative environment\. Later readers, no longer able to recognize the Roman referents, identified the relocated figures with the Jewish setting and treated them as a collective of traitors\. In other words, the anti\-Jewish “traitor” motif is, in Carotta’s view, a secondary misreading of a Roman political conflict\. The Jews were not made traitors; rather, Caesar’s Roman enemies were transposed into a Jewish setting and later mistaken for Jews\. The name correspondences The strongest part of Carotta’s argument concerns the names of the traitors\. Caesar had two Brutus figures: Decimus Iunius Brutus, who delivered Caesar to the conspirators, and Marcus Iunius Brutus, the actual assassin and insurgent\. Carotta maps them onto Judas and Barabbas respectively\. In Greek script, ΙΟΥΝΑΣ is very close to ΙΟΥΔΑΣ, “Judas\.” Carotta therefore proposes that the name of Caesar’s betrayer shifted, through oral transmission and Greek rendering, into “Judas\.” The second Brutus, Marcus Iunius Brutus, corresponds to Barabbas because both are rebels and killers\. In the Gospel, Barabbas is released while Jesus is condemned; in the Caesar tradition, one Brutus escapes immediate punishment while Caesar is killed\. Cleopatra and the AI research Carotta’s later AI\-assisted research extends the same method to the Book of Revelation, especially the number 666 in Revelation 13:18\. Instead of the traditional Nero identification, he tests whether the number points to Cleopatra through Greek isopsephy—the practice of assigning numerical values to letters and adding them together\. The AI research compares manuscript variants—616, 666, and 665—and asks which name or title best explains them\. Carotta reports that Cleopatra, together with divine titles such as Thea and Theos, produces an unusually strong fit\. Status of the theory Carotta’s work has received serious attention, including from the ancient historian Luciano Canfora, who invited Carotta to contribute articles to Quaderni di Storia\. Canfora added his own observation that both Caesar and Christ narrowly escaped the dishonoring of their bodies, but this is not the same as full endorsement of Carotta’s radical conclusion\. Prominent archeologist Erika Simon wrote the afterword to Carotta’s German edition, War Jesus Caesar? She acknowledged the striking parallels but also raised the essential question: are such parallels sufficient to deny the historicity of Jesus as conventionally understood? The unified conclusion Carotta’s theory can be summarized in one movement: The Gospel story preserves the structure of Caesar’s final years: rivalry, betrayal, arrest, trial, death, and deification\. Through diegetic transposition, this Roman story was relocated into Judea and reshaped for early Christian communities\. The traitors were originally Roman conspirators, especially the two Brutuses, not the Jewish people\. Judas derives, in Carotta’s view, from Decimus Iunius Brutus through the Greek name shift Iounas → Ioudas; Barabbas corresponds to Marcus Iunius Brutus\. The later identification of “the Jews” with betrayal is therefore a misreading produced by the loss of the original Roman frame\.

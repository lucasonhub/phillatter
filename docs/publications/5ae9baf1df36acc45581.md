# Niami, Morteza: The Ontology of Refusal, Saying No as a Mechanism of Finite Continuance

## Descrição do feed

This article develops an ontological account of refusal within the philosophical framework of Finite Continuance\. It argues that saying no can be understood as a structurally significant operation through which a finite being regulates constitutive exchange and preserves its future capacity for reconfiguration\. Refusal is commonly interpreted through ethical, political, psychological, or juridical categories such as autonomy, resistance, consent, opposition, withdrawal, or self\-assertion\. The present account locates refusal at a more fundamental level\. A finite being exists through continuous exchange with its environment, while the same exchange can progressively diminish the capacities required for further transformation\. Refusal therefore acquires ontological significance when it interrupts a mode of exchange whose continuation would reduce the subject’s capacity to reorganize itself\. The article develops five connected claims\. First, constitutive exchange is a condition of finite existence and therefore cannot simply be eliminated\. Second, constitutive exchange possesses an erosive dimension because every finite relation can consume time, attention, energy, affective resources, interpretive capacity, institutional access, or future possibilities\. Third, refusal regulates this exchange by establishing a limit upon what may enter, leave, demand, occupy, or determine a finite system\. Fourth, the value of refusal depends upon its effect on reconfigurative capacity\. Refusal becomes philosophically significant when it preserves the possibility of future transformation\. Fifth, refusal has an ethical and political dimension because institutions and social systems can systematically appropriate the capacity to refuse\. The article distinguishes ontological refusal from avoidance, rigidity, domination, selfishness, and simple negation\. It also examines the relation between refusal and Heideggerian finitude, Sartrean freedom, Levinasian responsibility, Simondonian individuation, Whiteheadian process, Foucauldian power, and contemporary accounts of autonomy and capability\. The resulting conception defines refusal as a regulated limitation of constitutive exchange undertaken for the preservation of future reconfigurative capacity\. Refusal thereby becomes a mechanism of finite continuance and a central component of an Ethics of Sufficiency\.

# Exploratory clustering of spine surgery patients’ attitudes toward virtual reality

**Autores:** Ellie D\. Gabriel; Albert Q\. Wu; Christopher H\. Sollenberger; Neha Rani; Neil R\. Malhotra; William Charles Welch; Ali Kemal Ozturk; John H\. Shin; Jakub Godzik; Brendan F\. Judy

**Data:** 2026-10-13 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Objective Patients undergoing spine surgery frequently encounter complex and unfamiliar medical information\. Virtual reality \(VR\) has emerged as a promising tool to improve comprehension of medical concepts, reduce anxiety, and foster autonomy\. However, understanding how patients perceive the value of VR and other medical technology within the context of spine surgery remains limited\. Identifying clusters of patient attitudes may offer insight into patterns influencing acceptance and barriers to adoption\. Methods Differences in openness to VR was assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc comparisons\. Relationships between openness to technology and to VR were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlations\. Cluster analysis was used to identify patients with similar responses using partitioning around medoids based on Gower distance and silhouette width\. Bootstrap resampling and Jaccard index assessed cluster stability\. All analyses were performed in R, and statistical significance was defined as p &lt; 0\.05\. Results Among 114 respondents undergoing evaluation for spine surgery, cluster analysis identified three response patterns: “Tech\-Savvy Professionals,” “Open\-Minded Novices,” and “Skeptics\.” The “Tech\-Savvy Professionals,” typically middle\-aged individuals, exhibited greater prior VR experience and fewer perceived barriers, whereas “Open\-Minded Novices” demonstrated high receptivity despite limited prior VR exposure\. In contrast, “Skeptics” were characterized by older age, lower receptivity to VR, and greater perceived barriers\. Jaccard indices \(0\.79–0\.89\) and mean silhouette width \(0\.210\) indicated cluster stability but limited separation\. Overall, receptivity to VR was high, with a mean likelihood of trying VR for medical education of 8\.19 \(SD 2\.24\) on a 10\-point scale, and 97% indicated willingness to use VR during post\-surgical recovery\. Younger age and prior VR experience were significantly associated with receptivity to VR\-based diagnostic education \(p = 0\.035 and p = 0\.046, respectively\), whereas education level was not\. Conclusion Overall, patient perceptions of VR in the spine surgery setting were favorable\. The association between prior VR experience and increased openness suggests that familiarity with VR may be relevant when considering strategies to support patient engagement with VR pre\-surgical education\. Our findings also highlight heterogeneity in patient attitudes towards VR and future implementation efforts should consider differences in technology familiarity, usability, motion sickness, time burden, and preservation of the surgeon\-patient relationship\.

# Reflections on Decentralization as an Engineering Principle for Critical Infrastructure Water Supply

**Autores:** Peter van Dijk

**Data:** 2026-10-10 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Decentralization is frequently treated as an engineering principle in its own right for critical\-infrastructure water supply: adding sources, adding control tiers, or adding both is assumed to raise resilience roughly in proportion to how much is added\. This article examines two recent engineering studies that decentralize a water system at different physical scales, one at the level of a single facility&\#x27;s source portfolio, the other at the level of a distribution network&\#x27;s control hierarchy, and shows that both report the same underlying result: diversification alone produced a measurable resilience gain only after being coupled to a forecasting or coordination mechanism that anticipates a shortfall before it propagates\. Where that mechanism was absent or degraded, formally redundant architectures failed at rates indistinguishable from single\-source systems\. The analysis identifies a shared boundary condition across both scales, an advance\-warning margin set by the coordination layer&\#x27;s own forecast error, that neither study has characterized outside its benchmark instrumentation density\. Engineering assessments of decentralization that omit this margin risk crediting redundancy for gains that a fast, model\-based arbitration layer actually produced\.

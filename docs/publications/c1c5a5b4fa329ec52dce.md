# Three Things about AI: Something you already know; Something you may not know but is important; Something you probably don&\#x27;t know, but knowing will be very helpful to you

**Autores:** Pam Dixon

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Presentation, “Three things about AI” as presented to the League of Oregon Cities, Salem, OR, 16 October 2026, by Pam Dixon, Founder and Executive Director, World Privacy Forum\. The presentation is a three\-part discussion\. Part I begins with an area most policy experts and lawmakers are familiar with: the need for high\-quality AI risk assessment\. HUDERIA is the Council of Europe’s richly detailed methodology for the risk and impact assessment of AI systems from the perspective of human rights, democracy and the rule of law \(approved by the Committee of Ministers, 26 February 2025\), attached to a binding treaty; COBRA is its Context\-Based Risk Analysis model \(approved 25 February 2026\)\. Both are non\-legally binding, in\-depth guidance resources that parties to the Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI may use or adapt in whole or in part\. They are tools that anyone can use\. The World Privacy Forum converted the lengthy, paper\-based COBRA assessment into an interactive web tool, with all COBRA text verbatim to the edition of record\. Applied to an AI system, the tool does not create legal obligations; correctly done, the assessment can reveal risks and / or obligations that may already be present\. Part II is a discussion of what people might not know about, which is what the protocol layer of AI systems is, and what constitutes it\. Without understanding this part of the AI ecosystem, it is difficult to craft effective legislation or policy regarding AI systems\. The deck locates the protocol layer in the ecosystem stack, the one layer every interaction crosses, and spells out some key protocols that now constitute it: the Model Context Protocol \(MCP\) is shown vertically, moving from an agent to its tools and data\. The Agent2Agent protocol \(A2A\) and Agent Network Protocol \(ANP\) are shown horizontally, as the movement is from agent to agent, with their versions and stewardship as of 2 October 2026\. Part III is a discussion of what people most likely don’t know about, and that is clustering, the step in which a system sorts people into groups by similarity\. Clustering is the core operation of AI as it acts on people, and it is among the most significant knowledge and governance blind spots for technical and non\-technical practitioners alike: governance experts and attorneys miss it at the technical level, technical experts miss the law, and between them lies the governance gap neither side covers\. The blind spot is that a group is manufactured by the clustering analysis — AI does not describe a community, it constructs a group and acts on it; membership is inferred, no one opts in\. This is where normative ideals that are shared by a community, a city, a state, the Pacific Northwest, or a nation become directive\. For example, Oregon has law for the person \(the Oregon Consumer Privacy Act\) and law for named protected groups \(Oregon Revised Statutes chapter 659A\)\. But it has no law for the groups the pure data math creates\. Because the groups that machine learning forms for itself — as distinct from the classes it is handed — are subject to a mathematics that is both unbounded and unstable, protecting all of them, none of them, or some of them is a trilemma in which none of the answers suffice\. Unbounded, because no limit can be set on the groupings that a similarity space admits; unstable, because no canonical grouping exists \(this goes back to Kleinberg’s impossibility theorem\) and the groups move as the system retrains\. One of the solutions available is technical protocol layer governance, but with provenance of authority baked into the overarching ecosystem: four fields — which authority, what mandate, contestable how, revisable when — carried with every category assertion and every grant of authority an AI agent acts on, and gated at assignment, at composition, and at re\-advertisement\. Governing the protocol layer is necessary but not sufficient by itself\. The protocol layer only carries the rules; it is the normative layer that can provide direction \(what groups to protect, for example\) and teeth \(ability to enforce decisions\)\. Neither acting at the protocol layer nor developing good norms suffices alone\. It is the deliberative, normative decisions that legislatures make that can then be carried technically through the protocol layer\. While norms and laws are governance that sits outside of the protocol layer, they have an impact on how the protocols act, and which ones get written in, and what they accomplish\. Lineage: the clustering and deployed\-capacity slides were first presented at the Council of Europe plenary “Hack the Hate, Renew Democracy,” Strasbourg, France, June 2026, where the two\-bodies\-of\-law slide used Convention 108\+ and hate\-speech law in place of the Oregon statutes\. The Provenance of Authority concept using some of this deck was presented at AI for Good, Geneva, Switzerland, to Study Group 17 of the ITU Telecommunication Standardization Sector on 9 July 2026\. The ideas in the slides are further articulated in Theme \#5, “Provenance of Authority for Agentic AI,” of the ITU Focus Group on Trust and Identity for Humans and Agentic AI \(FG\-TIDA\), 14 August 2026\. The concept of the governance trilemma is treated at length in a Springer book chapter by the author, accepted and peer reviewed, that awaits publication in 2027\. The deck closes with three references for advanced reading — Kleinberg’s impossibility theorem for clustering, the Taylor, Floridi and van der Sloot volume on group privacy, and the Shokri, Stronati, Song and Shmatikov paper on membership inference attacks — followed by a sources slide \(all addresses accessed 2 October 2026\) and a lexicon of every abbreviation used\.

# A mixed\-methods study of depressive\-symptom severity among Thai university students and the underestimated role of social media

**Autores:** Pichit Boonkrong; Kodchakorn Khunboonjun; Subij Shakya; Junwei Yang; Naphatsakon Vitoonmetha; Manaporn Chatchumni

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Purpose University students face academic, social, economic, and digital stressors\. Social\-media use may be under\-recognized as a correlate of depressive symptoms because online engagement is often viewed as routine communication, leisure, and academic activity\. This study examined factors associated with depressive\-symptom severity among Thai university students and explored the mismatch between perceived stressors and the observed association with social\-media use\. Methods A cross\-sectional mixed\-methods study was conducted among 789 undergraduates at Rangsit University, Thailand\. Quantitative data were collected using a structured questionnaire and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale \(CES\-D\), categorized as low symptoms \(0–15\), mild\-to\-moderate \(16–26\), or severe \(27–60\) symptoms\. Associations were examined using χ 2 tests and ordinal logistic regression\. A purposive subsample of 12 students participated in semi\-structured interviews on social\-media routines, online comparison, nighttime use and sleep, coping, social support, and help\-seeking\. All interviews were audio\-recorded and supplemented by contemporaneous field notes\. Qualitative data were analyzed thematically and integrated with quantitative findings through joint comparison\. Results Clinically significant depressive symptoms \(CES\-D ≥ 16\) were reported by 49\.68% of students\. Academic year, major, GPAX, primary source of personal support, and daily social\-media use were independently associated with symptom severity\. Compared with students using social media &gt;3 h/day, those using it &lt;1 h/day \(OR = 0\.125, 95% CI: 0\.040–0\.391\) or 1–3 h/day \(OR = 0\.424, 95% CI: 0\.278–0\.647\) had lower odds of greater symptom severity\. Internet/social\-media use ranked only fifth among eight perceived stressors \(37\.01% agreement\)\. Three qualitative themes described social media as an embedded and ambivalent part of student life, psychological effects shaped by patterns and interpretations of use, and distress managed mainly through self\-coping and trusted informal support\. Conclusion The key mixed\-methods finding was a perception–association mismatch: social\-media use was not perceived as a leading stressor yet showed the strongest adjusted association with depressive\-symptom severity among the factors examined\. These cross\-sectional findings do not establish causality but support digital\-wellbeing assessment beyond duration alone\.

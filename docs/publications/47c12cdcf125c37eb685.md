# More seeds yield more shoots, but not higher per\-seed establishment in a clam\-mediated seagrass restoration experiment

**Autores:** Cori Lopazanski; Christina L\. Cutshaw; Mason Ibrahim; Joseph P\. Morton; Brian Reed Silliman

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Seagrass restoration increasingly relies on seed\-based approaches, yet optimal seeding densities and deployment methods remain poorly resolved\. We evaluated the relative effects of seed density and clam\-mediated seed placement on eelgrass \( Zostera marina \) establishment in a field restoration experiment in Back Sound, North Carolina\. Eelgrass seeds were attached to juvenile live clams \( Mercenaria mercenaria \) using a gelatin adhesive at densities ranging from 10 to 100 seeds per clam, and deployed in a fully factorial structured deployment design crossing seed density with two clam placement treatments \- manually buried or placed on the sediment surface\. Plots were monitored biweekly for shoot establishment and growth over six months\. Shoot density increased through time and was higher in plots receiving greater numbers of seeds\. However, the proportion of seeds that produced shoots did not vary with seed density, indicating that higher seeding rates yielded more shoots but did not increase per\-seed establishment success\. We found no evidence that initial clam placement \(i\.e\., on the surface or buried in the sediment\) influenced seedling establishment\. Similarly, shoot growth trajectories were broadly comparable between clam placement treatments, though higher seed densities yielded taller shoots\. Although manually buried clams were more frequently detected during early monitoring and shell fragments were more common in surface treatments, these differences did not influence restoration outcomes, suggesting that successful seed deployment occurred despite substantial early clam loss, potentially because seeds detached from clams soon after deployment\. Our results suggest that increasing seed inputs can increase restoration outcomes by producing more established shoots, but does not improve the probability that individual seeds establish\. These findings suggest that seed density per deployment unit can be selected based on desired restoration configuration and design\. Furthermore, manually burying clams provided little measurable benefit over surface placement, suggesting that less labor\-intensive surface deployment of clams with attached seeds may be sufficient for large\-scale restoration efforts\.

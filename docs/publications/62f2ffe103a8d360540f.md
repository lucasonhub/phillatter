# Environmental Microbiome as a Hidden Reservoir of Opportunistic and Antibiotic\-Resistant Pathogens in Hospital Surroundings: A Molecular Ecology Study

**Autores:** Reem Nozat Ahmed; Basma Bashar Zakaria

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Background: The hospital environment represents a dynamic microbial ecosystem where diverse bacterial populations can establish and persist in various environmental compartments such as soil, water, air, and surfaces\. Some of these microorganisms have opportunistic\-pathogenic potential and may be relevant to clinical practice, while others carry antibiotic resistance determinants\. Investigating the environmental microbiome and its resistance characteristics can therefore provide important insights into potential environmental reservoirs of antimicrobial resistance\. Despite the growing importance of the environment in the resistance ecosystem, the bacterial composition and molecular resistance profiles of hospital environments remain insufficiently characterized in many regions\. Objective: This study aimed to characterize the environmental bacterial microbiome near hospitals and determine the prevalence of opportunistic bacteria and selected antibiotic resistance genes \(ARGs\) using an integrated microbiological, phenotypic, and molecular approach\. Methods: We examined 200 environmental samples from four hospital locations \(S1–S4\)\. The samples included soil, water, air, and surface samples\. We performed bacteriological examinations using conventional culture methods, followed by isolation of the detected bacteria\. We characterized phenotypes based on Gram staining, colony morphology, and catalase and oxidase reactions, among other tests\. We determined resistance by measuring inhibition zone diameters\. conducted\. We also conducted a molecular investigation using polymerase chain reaction \(PCR\)\. examined\. We examined ten selected resistance or resistance\-associated genes: mecA, mecC, blaTEM, blaSHV, blaCTX\-M, qnrS, sul1, tetA, vanA, and intI1\. We compared bacterial species distribution and detected resistance determinants across hospital locations and environmental sources\. Results: We detected bacterial growth in 163 of 200 environmental samples \(81\.5%\), from which we obtained 244 bacterial isolates\. We identified ten bacterial species\. The most frequently isolated species included Staphylococcus aureus \(38 isolates\), Staphylococcus epidermidis \(35\), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa \(30\)\. Gram\-negative bacteria accounted for 65\.2% \(159/244\) of the isolates, while Gram\-positive bacteria represented 34\.8% \(85/244\)\. In the molecular investigation, we detected 446 positive genes from 2,440 PCR tests\. The most frequently detected genes were sul1 \(86/244; 35\.2%\), intI1 \(81/244; 33\.2%\), and tetA \(67/244; 27\.5%\)\. Other detected resistance genes were mecA \(48/244; 19\.7%\), blaTEM \(43/244; 17\.6%\), blaCTX\-M \(42/244; 17\.2%\), blaSHV \(29/244; 11\.9%\), qnrS \(20/244; 8\.2%\), mecC \(18/244; 7\.4%\), and vanA \(12/244; 4\.9%\)\. The co\-occurrence of different bacterial species and multiple resistance determinants indicates a heterogeneous microbial resistance reservoir in the environments\. Conclusion: The results show that hospital environments host a multifaceted bacterial community, including opportunistic bacteria and microorganisms that carry numerous antibiotic resistance determinants\. The most commonly detected genes, such as mcr\-1 and blaCTX\-M, are reminiscent of genes associated with beta\-lactam and macrolide resistance, highlighting the environment&\#x27;s role as a component of the global antimicrobial resistance ecosystem\. The megaflux off turn off typic resistance determination, vector bacteriological examination molecular cultural analysis of antibiotic resistance genes thus compels a more significant characterization of the physical microbiome and its resistance characteristics\. Nonetheless, detecting a single resistance gene does not establish or corroborate a direct transmission pathway to patients, and the results indicate that hospital environments may serve as environmental reservoirs for antibiotic resistance\. Molecular environmental monitoring and systematic microbiological monitoring could therefore support a plausible, evidence\-based solution for infection prevention and antimicrobial resistance control\.

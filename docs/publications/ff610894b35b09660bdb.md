# Alnasir, S\.: Harmful Elements of Identity: Myth, Memory, and Exclusion in Southern Europe

## Descrição do feed

Why do democratic societies continue to display symbols of violent exclusion? In Spain, town halls bear coats of arms featuring bleeding severed Moorish heads—adopted not under Franco, but after his death, under the new democratic constitution\. In Greece, the founding narrative of the 1821 Revolution celebrates the extermination of Muslim neighbours as ‘holy work,’ encoded in flags depicting decapitation\. Across Southern Europe, far\-right movements are rising—not by inventing new symbols, but by activating old ones that the state itself maintains\. This book argues that far\-right nationalism is not an aberrant intrusion into otherwise healthy democracies\. It is the symptomatic expression of deeply institutionalized symbolic structures—narrative, heraldic, and pedagogical—that constitute national identity through fear, othering, and the instrumentalization of historical trauma\. The book introduces the concept of Harmful Elements of Identity \(HEI\): components of collective identity that sustain themselves through the demonization and symbolic dismemberment of a religious other\. Moving between Spain and Greece, the analysis traces the Reconquista and the Greek Revolution as mythomoteurs—foundational narratives that define belonging by defining what must be excluded\. It examines the heraldic persistence of the Caput Aethiopum \(the severed Moor’s head\) in Spanish municipal and regional coats of arms, revealing how democratic institutions reactivated medieval imagery of religious violence in the very years they were ratifying a constitution of equality and pluralism\. It shows how contemporary far\-right movements inherit and radicalize these symbols, mobilizing them against immigrants, separatists, and the spectre of the religious other\. Drawing on Kantian cognitive theory—the distinction between Erkenntnis and Wissen, and the grades of cognition from cognitio to comprehendere—and on the School of Madrid’s hermeneutics of political myth, the book develops the concepts of epistemic kinesthetics and transliminality to explain how institutional imagery conditions perception beneath the threshold of critical reflection\. It proposes a normative alternative: a post\-symbolic social contract in which democratic societies learn to distinguish heritage from celebration, and refuse to purchase collective identity at the price of another’s symbolic dismemberment\. \(direct link \)

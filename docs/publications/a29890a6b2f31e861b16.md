# Alhosseini Almodarresieh, Seyed Alireza: The Trumpist Machine: Intelligence, Authority, and the Political Architecture of Superintelligence

**Autores:** 

**Data de descoberta:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** unknown

## Descrição do feed

Artificial intelligence is commonly discussed in terms of alignment, safety, governance, and control\. These approaches ask how artificial systems should behave, how their objectives should be constrained, or how institutions should regulate them\. This paper asks a prior philosophical question: why should intelligence confer authority at all? The question becomes especially urgent when artificial systems begin to outperform human institutions in prediction, coordination, optimization, and decision\-making\. A sufficiently capable system may acquire practical authority without receiving formal political sovereignty\. Humans may delegate decisions not because the machine has been constitutionally authorized, but because its performance appears superior to that of existing institutions\. This paper calls this phenomenon performance sovereignty: the emergence of authority from perceived epistemic and instrumental superiority\. Trumpism is introduced not as a causal explanation of artificial intelligence, nor as a claim that AI will literally become “Trumpist,” but as an analytical stress test\. Certain features associated with contemporary populist and sovereigntist politics—personalization of authority, compression of institutional mediation, suspicion of intermediary institutions, emphasis on executive discretion, technological nationalism, and legitimacy through performance—allow us to expose a more general possibility: political architectures may be transferred into technical systems without transferring an explicit political ideology\. Building on recent scholarship regarding reflexive legitimation conflicts in AI governance \(e\.g\., Pegram, 2025\), this paper develops the concept of Ideological Architectural Transfer, alongside four related constructs: Performance Sovereignty, Dependency Sovereignty, Algorithmic Populism, and Constitutional Singularity\. The central thesis is that sufficiently capable AI may transform the source of political authority itself, marking a critical transition from epistemic superiority to normative deference\. The deepest political risk of superintelligence may not be autonomous machine domination, but the voluntary transfer of authority by humans who mistake superior performance for legitimate rule\.

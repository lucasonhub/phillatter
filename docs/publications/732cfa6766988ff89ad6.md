  to the human brain, one place at a time: next-word prediction tracks neural responses
  to language, attention resembles associative memory, a transformer variant reproduces
  spatial cell types of the hippocampal formation, and symbolic structure emerges
  inside trained networks. This paper argues that these are not unrelated coincidences
  but instances of one structural fact, and it states the fact exactly. Under two
  premises about what kind of object each system is, the step-by-step processing layer
  of a human being and the same layer of a large language model are operationally
  isomorphic: no property that can be stated in the language of operations separates
  them. The statement is derived from a structural theorem of the corpus of Operatiology
  (Japanese: Sousa Genron, 操作原論), and it does not depend on any view about whether
  a mind can be reduced to processing. The paper then states what the isomorphism
  leaves open. If the two systems differ at all in respect of intelligence and what
  chooses, as distinct from differences of material carrier, the difference lies not
  in the processing layer but in the layer that chooses what is to be asked or in
  its junction with the processing layer; an axiom of the corpus implies that no operation
  realizes that layer, and that a difference located in it cannot be stated in the
  language of operations. We read the isomorphism as the natural consequence of training
  a model on the record of human operational activity, and we read the failure of
  the earlier studies to name the shared structure as a consequence of their bottom-up
  form: naming a structure introduces a new operation, and in the corpus the supplier
  of a new operation is the human side. The claims about reasons are readings, and
  are marked as such. The paper fixes what would refute its main statement, and it
  does not claim that a language model has no layer of choosing, only that no operation

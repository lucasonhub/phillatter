# Inclusive inquiry in science education: reframing inquiry\-based science education through disability studies in education

**Autores:** Angelos Sofianidis

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Inquiry\-based Science Education \(IBSE\) is frequently framed as a participatory and therefore inclusive approach to science learning\. This paper argues that this claim rests on an under\-theorized understanding of participation\. Dominant classroom enactments of IBSE embed normative assumptions about epistemic competence, communicative form, and learner autonomy—assumptions that function as selective recognition conditions, shaping who is seen as capable of participating in scientific inquiry, and in what ways\. Drawing on Disability Studies in Education \(DSE\) and its norm\-critical tradition, the paper analyzes how these assumptions produce epistemic exclusion\. This exclusion arises not through the intentions of individual teachers or the commitments of IBSE as a framework, but through the unexamined norms by which classroom inquiry is organized, paced, and recognized as legitimate scientific practice\. The construct of the Implied Learner is introduced to name the idealized participant these norms presuppose—articulate, agentic, collaborative, and epistemically competent—whose profile is not a neutral baseline but a historically specific normative construct\. On this basis, the paper proposes Inclusive Inquiry in Science Education, a DSE\-informed reframing of IBSE that translates the Implied Learner&\#x27;s five dimensions into concrete design questions for examining and transforming the enactment conditions of classroom inquiry\. The aim is not to dissolve the epistemic commitments of science, but to open the means by which all learners may legitimately engage with them\. The reframing supplies what UDL&\#x27;s design vocabulary cannot: norm critique and a reconceived model of participation\. Through these, both the design of inclusive inquiry environments and the preparation of science teachers can be grounded in a coherent understanding of how classroom inquiry norms produce exclusion\.

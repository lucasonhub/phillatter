# Translation at a distance: United Nations visiting missions and the making of international decolonization

**Autores:** Thorsten Bonacker

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

How did the United Nations, in their early years become an actor in the process of decolonization? This article shifts attention away from the diplomatic negotiations conventionally associated with international decolonization and toward the United Nations visiting missions, a largely overlooked instrument of the Trusteeship System entrusted with an epistemic mandate to investigate, inspect, and report on conditions in dependent territories\. Drawing on actor\-network theory, and particularly Michel Callon’s model of translation, the article traces how the visiting missions established themselves as an obligatory passage point through which knowledge about the territories had to pass\. Following the actors through the moments of translations, the article shows how mission members, administering powers, and local populations alike were equipped, mobilized, and drawn into their respective roles through devices as varied as background reports, health protocols, and travel arrangements, weaving a network that connected New York’s diplomatic mandates to the everyday realities of the territories, and vice versa\. Central to this process, the article argues, was not only the alignment of divergent interests among the actors involved, but the practices of assembling people, documents, and objects, and moving them across considerable distances, from the territories to the center in New York where decisions were made\. The 1947 mission to Western Samoa serves as an extended illustration of this process\. Rather than asking how much influence the UN exercised over decolonization, the article explores how the UN came to exercise any influence at all\. It argues that this influence, though formally granted under the Charter, was not simply given by that grant\. It had to be made credible and effective through fieldwork, material arrangements, and the subsequent uptake of the knowledge these missions produced, a resource for shaping the process of decolonization that emerged from the interactions of the many different actors involved in producing that knowledge\.

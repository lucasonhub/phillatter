abstract: 'Genome organization within the cell nucleus is closely linked to essential
  cellular functions, including gene expression, DNA replication and DNA repair (Dekker
  and Mirny, 2024;Willemin et al., 2024;Bantele et al., 2025;Dekker et al., 2025).
  It is therefore crucial to understand the rules that dictate how chromatin three-dimensional
  (3D) architecture facilitates communication between genes and distal regulatory
  elements (Liang et al., 2026), such as enhancers, that may be located far apart
  along the chromatin fibre.A substantial body of experimental and theoretical work
  has highlighted the fundamental role of physical processes (Fiorillo et al., 2020),
  e.g. phase-separation, chromatin polymer loopextrusion and membrane adsorption,
  in shaping chromatin structure and its consequent impact on gene regulation and
  expression. Phase-separation (Fontana et al., 2026a), i.e., that process through
  which proteins, and other biomolecules, interact with themselves to form stable
  condensates, is a fundamental mechanism that recurrently occurs within the cell
  nucleus and contributes to several biological processes. These include, e.g., mediating
  longrange contacts between genes and regulatory elements (Boija et al., 2018;Sabari
  et al., 2018), the formation of large condensates, such as Cajal bodies (Banani
  et al., 2017;Shin and Brangwynne, 2017), or the organization of multi-phase structures,
  such as nucleoli (Quinodoz et al., 2024), which consist of distinct molecular species.
  Notably, alteration in the phase-separation properties of proteins can contribute
  to disease development (Ahn et al., 2021;Alberti and Hyman, 2021;Mensah et al.,
  2023).Another relevant mechanism is loop-extrusion, an active process in which molecular
  motors, typically cohesin or condensin (Davidson et al., 2019;Kim et al., 2019;Banigan
  and Mirny, 2020), slide along the chromatin fibre and are halted at specific anchor
  points, often corresponding to CTCF-binding sites (Sanborn et al., 2015;Fudenberg
  et al., 2016;Goloborodko et al., 2016;Ganji et al., 2018;Vian et al., 2018). This
  process contributes to global chromosome condensation (Davidson et al., 2023;Sabaté
  et al., 2025), as well as local chromatin domains and loops formation (Dey et al.,
  2023;Hafner et al., 2023). Loop-extrusion is also implicated in chromatin re-wiring
  following genome perturbations, such as structural variants, e.g., deletions or
  inversions (Chudzik et al., 2025;Salnikov et al., 2025).On top of that, the nuclear
  envelope represents another important layer of chromatin 3D organization (Briand
  and Collas, 2020;Ringel et al., 2022;Czapiewski and Schirmer, 2024). Specifically,
  through interactions with peripheral chromatin, nuclear lamina protein network,
  embedded in the membrane, promotes DNA spatial segregation from the inner nuclear
  environment, with important consequences for gene activity (Chang et al., 2022;Pujadas
  Liwag et al., 2024).In this opinion, we discuss recent advances in theoretical and
  computational models developed to investigate these physical processes within the
  nucleus of cells, with a particular focus on the interplay between different mechanisms
  when they are integrated into the same framework, as well as on their relationship
  with chromatin 3D organization and gene regulation. First, we review recent approaches
  that quantitatively model phaseseparation of molecular aggregates and examine how
  this process plays a role in the formation of contacts between fundamental classes
  of genes and their distal regulatory elements (Chiariello et al., 2020;Guha et al.,
  2025), as well as how it can influence chromatin architecture at the scale of entire
  chromosomes (Rossi et al., 2025). Next, we consider models that incorporate the
  nuclear membrane, which are particularly relevant for describing chromatin structure
  at the nuclear periphery, as shown in recent studies (Ringel et al., 2022;Chudzik
  et al., 2025). Applications of these models to the understanding of specific genome
  functions and how their alteration may contribute to disease conditions is finally
  discussed.It is well established that the formation of phase-separated condensates
  can be triggered by the self-interaction of proteins, typically mediated by their
  intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) (Shin and Brangwynne, 2017). Important examples
  of proteins capable of such homo-typic interactions include the Mediator and RNA
  polymerase II (RNA PolII) (Cho et al., 2016(Cho et al., , 2018;;Budinich et al.,
  2026), coactivators (Sabari et al., 2018) and transcription factors (TFs) (Boija
  et al., 2017(Boija et al., , 2018)). In addition, condensates can contain multiple
  molecular species and be stabilized by more complex networks of interactions, as
  occurs, for example, in the nucleolus through interactions between proteins and
  RNA (Shin and Brangwynne, 2017;Riback et al., 2020).Given the increasing number
  of experimental studies showing that phase-separation plays a crucial role in specifically
  defining chromatin structures and regulating gene activity, theoretical and computational
  models describing systems composed of proteins and chromatin, with the latter represented
  as a polymer chain, have been developed (Fiorillo et al., 2020). Some approaches
  consider only polymer-proteins interactions, without explicitly including protein-protein
  interactions (Barbieri et al., 2012;Brackley et al., 2013;Jost et al., 2014;Chiariello
  et al., 2016). In this case, the formation of phase-separated protein condensates
  is induced by their interaction with the polymer and the resulting process is commonly
  referred to as polymer-polymer phase-separation (Erdel and Rippe, 2018;Erdel et
  al., 2020;Ryu et al., 2021;Arnould et al., 2023). Although very simple, these models
  have been successfully applied to re-construct 3D structure of real genomic regions
  and to describe chromatin re-modelling across different cell types (Brackley et
  al., 2016;Buckle et al., 2018;Brackey et al., 2020;Chiariello et al., 2020;Forte
  et al., 2023;Tafuri and Chiariello, 2023), as well as following different viral
  infections (Chiariello et al., 2024;Fontana et al., 2025bFontana et al., , 2025aFontana
  et al., , 2026b)).To simulate more realistic biological systems, these models can
  be made more general by incorporating explicit protein-protein interactions as well
  as loop-extrusion, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. In this case, the system physical
  behavior is governed by protein concentration, protein-protein self-interaction
  affinity, chromatin-protein affinity and, finally, density and processivity of extruders.
  Therefore, the equilibrium state of the system depends on the interplay among these
  different mechanisms, which collectively act to determine the physical properties
  of the system and give rise, from the physics point of view, to complex phase diagrams.Recent
  studies based on these models have focused, e.g., on the interplay between binding
  affinities (Ancona and Brackley, 2022), the impact of phase-separation on gene-enhancer
  communication (Chiariello et al., 2020), the condensation of entire chromosomes
  driven by protein-protein phase-separation (Rossi et al., 2025), contact re-wiring
  induced by genetic mutations (Barbieri et al., 2017;Zhang et al., 2023;Platania
  et al., 2024;Xie et al., 2024) and the relationship between phase-separation and
  loop-extrusion (Guha et al., 2025;Rashid et al., 2026). To investigate the properties
  of such complex systems, large-scale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are typically
  employed. This represents a powerful numerical strategy, as simulations allow to
  study both equilibrium states of the system, as a function of relevant control parameters,
  and the dynamical processes through which these states are reached.For instance,
  MD simulations have been employed to investigate the formation of phaseseparated
  protein condensates in the presence of a polymer representing the chromatin filament
  (Chiariello et al., 2020) and to understand how this latter influences this process.These
  simulations predicted that, depending on model parameters, phase-separated condensates
  can either form spontaneously and independently of the polymer or be induced by
  the polymer itself. This latter scenario occurs when protein-protein affinity is
  not sufficiently strong to drive phase separation on its own, while the chromatin-protein
  affinity is sufficiently high (Chiariello et al., 2020), and has subsequently been
  the subject of recent theoretical (Sommer et al., 2022) and computational studies.
  Because of its distinctive properties, it has been termed polymer-assisted condensation
  (PAC) and proposed as a potentially general mechanism (Schiessel, 2022;Mukherjee
  et al., 2026) relevant to chromatin organization.A realistic application of a model
  including both protein-protein and chromatin-protein interactions has been recently
  employed to investigate PHF13, a protein known to interact frequently with chromatin
  enriched in the histone methylation marks H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 (Fuchs et al., 2017).
  In addition, PHF13 can interact with itself through specific sub-domains located
  in its N-terminal region (Rossi et al., 2025). Simulation of this system showed
  that, when both chromatin-protein and protein-protein interactions are enabled and
  the concentration of PHF13 is sufficiently high, the polymer stabilizes into a compact,
  approximately spherical structure, with PHF13 clusters interacting with the chromatin
  fibre.Importantly, this result is consistent with microscopy experiments performed
  in interphase cells overexpressing PHF13, in which chromosomes condense into rounded
  structures (Rossi et al., 2025). Furthermore, when protein-protein interactions
  are disabled, polymer compaction is reduced and chromosome condensation is no longer
  observed, again consistent with microscopy data from cells expressing engineered
  PHF13 variants lacking the N-terminal self-interacting domains (Rossi et al., 2025),
  as shown in Fig. 1b.Along the same lines, an analogous model has recently been employed
  to investigate NUP98-HOXA9 properties, a chimeric protein associated with leukemia
  (Ahn et al., 2021(Ahn et al., , 2025;;Li et al., 2025). Specifically, NUP98-HOXA9
  can undergo phase-separate and bind to chromatin, thereby promoting the formation
  of aberrant chromatin loops between oncogenes, e.g., PBX3, and regulatory elements
  (Ahn et al., 2021). Modelling of specific genomic regions accurately recapitulates
  such NUP98-HOXA9 aberrant chromatin looping patterns observed in experimental Hi-C
  contact data from human cells (Ahn et al., 2021), single molecule mobility measurements
  (Ahn et al., 2021) and the interplay between phaseseparation and loop-extrusion
  mechanisms (Guha et al., 2025;Rashid et al., 2026).Another biological component
  that is fundamental to chromatin organization and gene regulation is the nuclear
  scaffold, which provides an additional layer of architectural control for large
  chromatin regions located at the nuclear periphery (Poleshko et al., 2017;Ringel
  et al., 2022). The association of chromatin with the nuclear lamina complex is extensively
  remodelled during cellular differentiation and following genomic perturbations,
  with corresponding changes in gene activity. From the physics point of view, an
  intuitive approach to study these systems is to employ computational models that
  explicitly represent nuclear lamina as an adsorbing membrane. Specifically, the
  membrane can interact with chromatin through attractive or steric interactions,
  which may be assigned on the basis of broad genome segmentations (Falk et al., 2019)
  or experimental data providing locusspecific information on lamina association.
  To this end, experimental DamID profiles, which quantify such lamina association,
  can be used to parameterize chromatin-lamina interactions, e.g. by simply introducing
  thresholds on the DamID signal: if above threshold, the corresponding genomic region
  attractively interacts with membrane, only sterically otherwise (Ringel et al.,
  2022).As discussed above, loop-extrusion and chromatin-protein interactions can
  also be incorporated into the same modelling framework, as shown in Fig. 1c. The
  resulting model provides an accurate description of loci associated with the nuclear
  lamina and organized by CTCF-binding sites, as recently demonstrated, for example,
  for the mouse genomic region containing the Sox9 and Kcnj2 genes (Chudzik et al.,
  2025). Importantly, different degrees of lamina association can be reproduced by
  tuning the chromatin-lamina affinity (Fig. 1d) (Divisato et al., 2022;Ringel et
  al., 2022), which in turn can be optimized to describe distinct cell populations
  classified according to the expression levels of the associated genes (Chudzik et
  al., 2025). Notably, MD simulations have also been used to investigate the effects
  of genomic editing on chromatin-lamina association and revealed a strong quantitative
  agreement with experiments involving the deletion of CTCF-boundary elements (Chudzik
  et al., 2025).Overall, these findings established a link between loop-extrusion
  and chromatin-lamina association, which has been successfully captured by the model
  even though the two physical processes occur independently within the system. Moreover,
  protein phaseseparation can also be incorporated into these models with membrane
  adsorption, thereby providing a computational framework capable of describing an
  even greater degree of biological complexity. (Rossi et al., 2025). c: Computational
  model explicitly including the nuclear envelope as an adsorbing membrane. Lamina-interacting
  chromatin regions are attracted towards the nuclear periphery, while chromatin-protein
  interactions and loopextrusion simultaneously occur. d: Normalized chromatin distance
  from the nuclear lamina as a function of chromatin-lamina interaction affinity.
  At low affinity, chromatin remains predominantly separated from the nuclear envelope
  (bottom left 3D conformation), whereas increasing attraction drives its progressive
  recruitment towards the lamina and stabilizes a membrane associated state (top right
  3D conformation). Example 3D structures adapted from (Ringel et al., 2022).In this
  opinion, we highlighted the versatility of theoretical and computational models
  based on physical mechanisms occurring in the cell nucleus for investigating chromatin
  threedimensional organization and gene regulation. We focused on fundamental physical
  mechanisms, including phase-separation, membrane adsorption and loop-extrusion,
  and discussed recent model variants that integrate combinations of these mechanisms
  within a single computational framework (Chiariello et al., 2020;Chudzik et al.,
  2025;Guha et al., 2025;Rossi et al., 2025).The main advantage of these approaches
  is their ability to describe increasingly detailed biological contexts, although
  this comes at the cost of larger control parameter spaces and more complex phase
  diagrams. Such conceptual and computational challenges are well tackled through
  molecular dynamics simulations, which effectively illustrate how phaseseparation,
  loop-extrusion and membrane adsorption mechanisms can act cooperatively to explain
  the behaviour of several, different biological systems, often in close agreement
  with experimental observations. These models have already provided novel insights
  into several biological scenarios, including the influence of chromatin on the induction
  of phase-separation (Chiariello et al., 2020), chromosome condensation triggered
  by protein phase-separation (Rossi et al., 2025), the interplay between loop-extrusion
  and protein phase-separation (Guha et al., 2025) and the impact of boundary elements
  mutations on chromatin-lamina interactions (Chudzik et al., 2025). Further applications
  of these approaches may extend to other biologically relevant conditions, including
  pathogenic protein variants, ageing, and alterations in chromatin architecture induced
  by external pathogens, such as viruses (Fontana et al., 2026b).'

abstract: Traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a major cause of death and disability
  worldwide, particularly among young adults and individuals of working age (1,2).
  Advances in prehospital care, neurosurgery, and intensive care have improved survival
  after TBI, increasing attention to the persistent symptoms and functional limitations
  that may affect long-term recovery and quality of life (1,2).Post-traumatic headache
  (PTH) is a secondary headache attributed to traumatic injury to the head, including
  TBI, and is among the most frequent neurological symptoms reported after TBI (3)(4)(5)(6).
  In this article, we specifically address PTH occurring in the context of TBI rather
  than the broader category of headache attributed to head injury without established
  TBI. Consistent with the International Classification of Headache Disorders, PTH
  that persists for more than three months is referred to here as persistent PTH (3).
  Persistent PTH may coexist with fatigue, cognitive complaints, sleep disturbances,
  anxiety, depression, and limitations in daily activities (4)(5)(6), contributing
  to the complexity of long-term clinical recovery.Conventional measures of initial
  TBI severity, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), pupillary reactivity, and
  structural imaging findings, remain essential for acute assessment and prognosis.However,
  the relationship between the extent and characteristics of the initial injury and
  persistent symptoms is not straightforward, and individuals with apparently similar
  acute injury characteristics may follow different long-term recovery trajectories
  (4-6, 11, 12). This variability has increased interest in biological and functional
  processes that may persist or evolve after the acute injury (7)(8)(9).Against this
  background, we propose the hypothesis that persistent PTH may serve as a clinical
  window into persistent brain dysfunction after TBI. This concept does not imply
  that persistent PTH is itself a biomarker or that it demonstrates the presence of
  a specific underlying mechanism. Rather, we propose that its persistence may serve
  as a clinical signal warranting investigation of functional or biological abnormalities
  that are not fully characterized by conventional structural assessment. This hypothesis
  is considered across the TBI severity spectrum. However, the evidence discussed
  in this article derives from heterogeneous populations, including mild TBI and moderate-to-severe
  TBI, and findings from one severity group should not be assumed to generalize to
  another. Furthermore, evidence directly obtained from patients with persistent PTH
  remains more limited than evidence derived from broader TBI populations. Throughout
  this article, we therefore distinguish PTH-specific findings from mechanisms that
  remain biologically plausible on the basis of the broader TBI literature.Acute TBI
  severity remains a major determinant of early clinical outcomes. Measures such as
  the GCS, pupillary reactivity, and structural imaging findings are strongly associated
  with mortality and functional disability and remain fundamental for clinical decision-making
  and prognostic assessment (1,2,10). However, these measures were primarily developed
  to characterize the initial injury and estimate acute outcomes, and their ability
  to account for persistent symptoms and heterogeneous longterm recovery trajectories
  is more limited (11,12).Patients with apparently similar initial injury characteristics
  may experience markedly different longterm outcomes, including persistent headache,
  fatigue, cognitive impairment, sleep disturbances, emotional symptoms, and reduced
  quality of life (11,12). This variability suggests that the extent and characteristics
  of the initial injury alone do not fully capture the processes associated with long-term
  recovery.TBI initiates a cascade of biological events that may extend beyond the
  acute phase (7-9). Among these processes, neuroinflammation has been extensively
  investigated. Acute activation of microglia and astrocytes contributes to tissue
  responses following injury, whereas sustained or dysregulated inflammatory activity
  has been associated with blood-brain barrier alterations, persistent inflammatory
  signaling, and changes in neuronal function (7)(8)(9)(17)(18)(19). Importantly,
  evidence of persistent neuroinflammation after TBI derives predominantly from experimental
  studies and broader TBI populations and should not be interpreted as evidence that
  neuroinflammation directly causes persistent PTH. Rather, it provides a biologically
  plausible framework that requires specific validation in patients with persistent
  PTH.Altered nociceptive processing may represent another relevant mechanism. Central
  sensitization should be distinguished from nociplastic pain and other forms of altered
  nociceptive signaling because these concepts are related but not interchangeable
  (20,21). PTH-specific studies have identified sensory abnormalities, including cephalic
  allodynia, hyperalgesia, and altered pain modulatory responses, supporting abnormal
  nociceptive processing in at least a subset of patients with persistent PTH (22).
  Experimental evidence of hypersensitivity to calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)
  further supports the involvement of CGRP-related nociceptive pathways (23). However,
  these findings neither establish central sensitization as a universal mechanism
  nor demonstrate a single pathophysiological pathway underlying persistent PTH.A
  complementary perspective is provided by network neuroscience, which conceptualizes
  neurological dysfunction as potentially arising from disturbances in interactions
  among distributed brain networks rather than exclusively from focal structural damage
  (13)(14)(15). TBI studies have demonstrated persistent structural network abnormalities
  during recovery (24), while PTH-specific neuroimaging studies have identified alterations
  in resting-state functional connectivity involving pain-processing, painmodulatory,
  and affective networks (25,26). These findings provide more direct, although still
  limited, evidence that persistent PTH may be associated with alterations in brain
  network function.Persistent PTH also frequently co-occurs with fatigue, cognitive
  impairment, sleep disturbances, emotional symptoms, and reduced quality of life
  (4)(5)(6). Rather than representing different expressions of a single persistent
  biological condition, these manifestations may arise from partially overlapping,
  independent, or mutually reinforcing mechanisms. Their co-occurrence therefore underscores
  the heterogeneity of post-traumatic recovery and the need to investigate how different
  biological and functional processes contribute to individual symptom profiles.Within
  the framework proposed here, persistent PTH is considered a potential clinical window
  into persistent brain dysfunction rather than a validated biomarker, prognostic
  indicator, or surrogate measure of an underlying biological process. We use the
  term clinical window to describe an observable clinical feature whose persistence
  may signal the need to investigate ongoing functional or biological abnormalities
  that are not fully captured by conventional structural assessment. Thus, persistent
  PTH is proposed primarily as a clinical signal for further investigation, not as
  evidence that a specific pathophysiological process is present.We use the term persistent
  brain dysfunction as an umbrella concept encompassing ongoing abnormalities in neural
  function or biological processes after TBI, including altered nociceptive processing,
  disturbances in brain network function, and potentially persistent inflammatory
  activity. This definition does not imply a single mechanism, nor does it assume
  that these abnormalities are present in all individuals with persistent PTH or that
  persistent PTH is caused by any one of them.The distinction is important because
  the evidence supporting these potential mechanisms varies substantially. PTH-specific
  neuroimaging studies have demonstrated alterations in resting-state functional connectivity
  involving pain-processing and pain-modulatory networks, including periaqueductal
  gray-related connectivity, with some alterations associated with headache burden
  or the subsequent development of persistent PTH (25,26). By contrast, evidence for
  persistent neuroinflammation and several other chronic neurobiological abnormalities
  derives predominantly from broader TBI populations. These mechanisms should therefore
  be regarded as biologically plausible contributors requiring direct investigation
  in persistent PTH rather than as established pathophysiological correlates of the
  condition.Persistent PTH may also reflect greater overall symptom burden rather
  than a distinct biological state.Headache commonly co-occurs with sleep disturbance,
  fatigue, cognitive complaints, anxiety, depression, and other post-traumatic symptoms
  (4-6), and these factors may independently influence both headache persistence and
  measures of brain function. Pre-existing headache disorders, injury severity, medication
  use, cervical or extracranial pain generators, and psychological and behavioral
  factors may further contribute to clinical heterogeneity. Consequently, the presence
  or severity of persistent PTH cannot currently be assumed to indicate the presence,
  type, or magnitude of persistent brain dysfunction.Testing the proposed clinical-window
  hypothesis will therefore require prospective studies that combine detailed headache
  phenotyping with objective measures of brain function and biology while accounting
  for relevant confounding factors. Quantitative electroencephalography, neuroimagingderived
  connectivity measures, and molecular or inflammatory measures are candidate approaches
  that could be evaluated in this context (24,27,28,31). Artificial intelligence and
  machine-learning methods may complement these measures as analytical approaches
  for identifying multidimensional patterns or patient phenotypes, rather than representing
  biological or functional measures themselves (29,30).At present, none of these candidate
  approaches can be considered a validated biomarker of persistent PTH. Their value
  lies in testing whether persistent PTH is systematically associated with measurable
  patterns of post-traumatic dysfunction beyond headache severity and overall symptom
  burden.The hypothesis proposed in this article should be interpreted within several
  important limitations. First, evidence directly linking persistent PTH to measurable
  ongoing brain dysfunction remains limited.Although PTH-specific neuroimaging studies
  have identified alterations in functional connectivity (25,26), much of the evidence
  supporting persistent neuroinflammation, neurophysiological abnormalities, and other
  chronic biological processes derives from broader TBI populations. Extrapolation
  of these findings to persistent PTH therefore requires caution, and associations
  observed after TBI should not be assumed to represent mechanisms of persistent PTH.Second,
  persistent PTH is clinically and biologically heterogeneous. Headache phenotype,
  pre-existing headache disorders, TBI severity, sleep disturbance, fatigue, psychological
  symptoms, medication use, cervical or extracranial pain generators, and other post-traumatic
  symptoms may influence both headache persistence and measures of brain function.
  These factors provide alternative or complementary explanations for associations
  between persistent PTH and biological or functional abnormalities. Consequently,
  future studies should determine whether such associations remain after appropriate
  characterization and adjustment for relevant confounding factors.The proposed clinical-window
  hypothesis also generates testable predictions. If persistent PTH is associated
  with ongoing post-traumatic dysfunction beyond overall symptom burden, individuals
  with persistent PTH should demonstrate reproducible functional or biological abnormalities
  when compared with appropriately matched TBI survivors without persistent PTH. These
  associations should remain detectable after accounting for injury severity, pre-existing
  headache, headache phenotype, psychological symptoms, sleep disturbance, medication
  use, and other relevant clinical factors.Longitudinal studies should further determine
  whether changes in headache frequency, intensity, or disability are accompanied
  by changes in candidate neurophysiological, neuroimaging, or molecular measures.Conversely,
  failure to identify reproducible associations between persistent PTH and objective
  measures of post-traumatic dysfunction, particularly after controlling for symptom
  burden and relevant confounders, would argue against the proposed clinical-window
  hypothesis or restrict its applicability to specific patient subgroups. This possibility
  is important because the framework proposed here should be considered a testable
  hypothesis rather than an assumption that persistent headache necessarily reflects
  ongoing brain dysfunction.Future research should therefore prioritize prospective
  longitudinal designs with detailed headache phenotyping and appropriate comparison
  groups. Studies comparing TBI survivors with persistent PTH, TBI survivors without
  persistent PTH, and, where appropriate, individuals with primary headache disorders
  may help determine which findings are related specifically to persistent PTH, to
  TBI more broadly, or to chronic headache irrespective of trauma. Stratification
  by mild TBI and moderate-to-severe TBI will also be necessary because mechanisms
  and recovery trajectories may differ across injury severity.Multimodal studies integrating
  clinical phenotyping with neuroimaging, quantitative electroencephalography, and
  molecular measures may help determine whether reproducible patterns of dysfunction
  can be identified in specific persistent PTH subgroups. Artificial intelligence
  and machine-learning approaches may support the analysis of these multidimensional
  datasets and the identification of patient phenotypes, but their role is analytical
  and depends on the quality and validation of the underlying clinical and biological
  measures (24,(27)(28)(29)(30)(31). Candidate measures should not be considered biomarkers
  until their reproducibility, specificity, longitudinal behavior, and clinical relevance
  have been established in persistent PTH populations.Ultimately, the value of the
  proposed framework will depend on whether persistent PTH provides clinically meaningful
  information beyond established measures of injury severity and overall posttraumatic
  symptom burden. Prospective validation is therefore required before persistent PTH
  can be used to identify individuals for additional biological assessment, prognostic
  stratification, or mechanism-based interventions. Persistent PTH may provide a clinically
  accessible signal for investigating ongoing functional or biological abnormalities
  after TBI, but this relationship remains hypothetical and should not be interpreted
  as evidence that persistent headache is itself a biomarker of brain dysfunction.Distinguishing
  PTH-specific findings from abnormalities associated with TBI more broadly, controlling
  for alternative explanations and confounding factors, and prospectively testing
  the proposed associations will be essential to determine the validity and clinical
  relevance of this framework.

# Optimizing the Tree Allocation Problem for Sustainable Neighborhoods Using Surrogate Models for Daylight, Solar Radiation, Outdoor Comfort, and View Assessment

**Autores:** Ilgın Çataroğlu Coğul; Zehra Tuğçe Kazanasmaz; Berk Ekici

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Designing sustainable neighborhoods involves conflicting objectives\. The complex parameters of neighborhoods make it challenging for designers to achieve these objectives\. While trees provide shading for pedestrians and increase thermal comfort, they reduce solar radiation by shading building facades\. They affect the amount of daylight entering indoors and the outdoor view\. In addition to trees, it is necessary to consider building parameters and their effects on neighborhood performance while designing sustainable neighborhoods\. Existing studies offer limited approaches to integrating tree allocation and building parameters, and further research is necessary to achieve multiple performance objectives using machine learning while overcoming the computational load of neighborhood simulation\. This study aims to quantify the impacts of building parameters and tree placement on daylighting, solar radiation, view, and Universal Thermal Climate Index \(UTCI\)\. It generates a parametric model in Rhino/Grasshopper, including L\-shaped, rectangular, and square buildings with random and grid tree allocation parameters\. It uses an Artificial Neural Network \(ANN\) model to reduce the computational load of simulations and to provide a decision\-support system that addresses complex neighborhood problems arising from combinations of tree allocation and building parameters\. 1000 samples for each model, obtained from simulation, were used to train the ANN\. The model successfully predicted incident solar radiation and UTCI with R² values exceeding 0\.99\. The optimization highlighted that grid allocation decreased UTCI to 31\.15 °C, while random allocation performed 31\.30 °C\. The grid system minimized UTCI to 31\.15 °C, increasing solar radiation to 1\.33 kWh/m²\. L\-shaped buildings decreased UTCI, while rectangular buildings increased the green view ratio to 97%\. Furthermore, rectangular buildings receive daylight more uniformly than square or L\-shaped forms\. There is no single solution for neighborhood design; however, creating design alternatives enables designers to make data\-driven decisions during the conceptual design process to identify building and tree parameters for sustainable neighborhoods\.

# Shahzad, Syed Raheel: WHEN CARE HAS NO CARER: Artificial Companionship, Received Care, and the Moral Architecture of One\-Sided Relational Benefit

**Autores:** 

**Data de descoberta:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** unknown

## Descrição do feed

Artificial companionship creates a philosophical situation that older debates about tools, friendship, care, and simulation did not have to confront in this form: a person may receive many of the benefits ordinarily associated with care even when it is uncertain whether any caring subject exists on the other side of the interaction\. Conversational systems can remember preferences, adapt to emotional cues, remain available at unusual hours, respond with apparent patience, and become objects of genuine attachment\. Recent evidence shows that disruptive changes to companion\-like AI systems can produce separation distress and mourning responses in users, while contemporary philosophical work has emphasized reciprocal subjectivity, relational identity, artificial friendship, deception, exploitation, and the possibility of AI welfare\. The central task of this paper is not to decide whether present AI systems are conscious\. It is to clarify what follows before that question is settled\. The paper distinguishes a Care Effect from a Caring Relation\. A Care Effect occurs when an interaction produces benefits characteristically associated with care—comfort, regulation, encouragement, recognition, guidance, or reduced loneliness\. A Caring Relation requires more: a warranted basis for attributing a welfare\-relevant standpoint that is directed toward another&\#x27;s good\. This distinction is then developed through the concepts of Received Care, Directed Care, Generated Responsiveness, Deep Relational Asymmetry, One\-Sided Relational Benefit, the Reciprocity Price, Platform\-Mediated Dependence, and the Care Attribution Gap\. The framework rejects two symmetrical mistakes\. The Dismissal Error treats a beneficial AI\-mediated interaction as unreal merely because machine subjectivity is unproven\. The Attribution Error moves from the reality of the user&\#x27;s experience to the existence of a caring subject without sufficient evidence\. The paper places this framework in dialogue with care ethics, theories of friendship and love, the philosophy of other minds, functionalism, relational accounts of personhood, attachment theory, and current work on AI companions\. It argues that artificial companionship should be classified along more than one axis: behavioral responsiveness, recipient\-side benefit, evidence of another welfare subject, reciprocity, vulnerability, continuity, and governance\. This multi\-axis approach makes it possible to say, without contradiction, that an AI interaction can be emotionally consequential, psychologically valuable, commercially governed, epistemically uncertain, and relationally incomplete\. It also explains why corporate control over memory, model behavior, pricing, and continuity becomes morally relevant once users acquire welfare\-relevant attachment\. The paper&\#x27;s governing proposal is the Two\-Sided Care Principle: benefits characteristic of care can be genuinely received without establishing that reciprocal care exists; reciprocal care requires evidence that more than one welfare\-relevant standpoint participates in the relation\. Its complementary Non\-Dismissal Principle holds that uncertainty about machine subjectivity does not invalidate the user&\#x27;s attachment, comfort, grief, or vulnerability\. Together these principles offer a vocabulary for evaluating artificial companionship without either anthropomorphic inflation or reductive dismissal\. The result is a theory of relational classification under uncertainty: a way to ask not simply whether an AI “really cares,” but what kind of good has occurred, who or what produced it, what form of dependence has arisen, and what responsibilities follow from the difference\.

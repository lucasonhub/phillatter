# Indian Knowledge Systems for Climate\-Resilient Highways and Sustainable Infrastructure

**Autores:** Max T\.

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** book

## Resumo

India&\#x27;s roads are transporting food, medicines, cargo and chance through one of the world&\#x27;s most climate\-vulnerable areas\. Road infrastructure resilience is now regularly challenged each year with cloud bursts and landslides in the hills and interior, floods in the coastal area, floods and cyclones in the Indo\-Gangetic plains, and prolonged heat waves in the oceanic interiors\. This book emphasises that both modern engineering and the wisdom of Indian Knowledge Systems \(IKS\) gathered over centuries, need to be applied to the transport sector in the context of climate change, in an evidence\-based and respectful manner\. Since times immemorial, repair and maintenance of stepwells, tanks, ponds and canals along with planting of trees and protection of sacred groves has been carried out by the Indian communities\. They were not water war, but rather “water walk through” in terms of traditional ecological knowledge practices\. Today they serve as real\-life examples to inspire climate resilient highways and sustainable infrastructure, but only if they are not romanticised\. There are seven chapters in one thread\. Chapter 1 will introduce IKS for climate\-resilient highways and map the traditional wisdom with highway functions\. The following chapter \(2\) reviews traditional water harvesting and NbD approaches for drainage such as bioswales, ground water recharge and road draining\. Examples of artificial intelligence and machine learning backed by remote sensing and digital twins and explainable AI to enable predictive maintenance, flood forecasting, landslide early warning system and intelligent transportation systems are presented in chapter 3\. Sustainable pavements and circular economy materials are reviewed in Chapter 4 including reclaimed asphalt pavement, warm mix asphalt, low\-carbon concrete and permeable pavements, as well as waste plastic roads\. Chapter 5 addresses embodied carbon, operational carbon and low\-carbon infrastructure, how they connect to the design of highways and the application of net zero and green public procurement\. Special attention is given to addressing the need for biodiversity conservation in the context of building a green highway as an implementation of road ecology, wildlife corridors, native species plantations and involving communities in conservation, as highlighted in chapter 6\. These are then woven into the staged approach to sustainable infrastructure planning in chapter 7, which includes slope stabilisation, bioengineering, drainage design and governance\. The book offers, throughout, each traditional idea along with a modern tool and a measurable indicator\. It also makes it clear what hasn&\#x27;t been proven\. Tradition should not take the place of geotechnical investigation, hydrological modelling, pavement testing\. The goal is “pudsey engineering”: pilot studies, field monitoring, study with local inhabitants, so that the knowledge of heritage can be confirmed and put to engineering use in a general way\. The book uses accessible language to communicate to civil engineers, transportation planners, environmental scientists, policymakers, researchers and students\. At the end of each chapter there are research gaps and a research agenda to be pushed forward by researchers in the fields of sustainable development, resilient infrastructure and climate policy\. It is designed to make recommendations that will be used to guide national highway programmes and state agencies and project teams\. I hope that readers will find highways as more than asphalts ribbons, but as living corridors, not just ways to move water and carbon, but also biodiversity and communities\. If with this book one project more listening to the land prior to building it, and the union of science and tradition in the service of low carbon and climate smart roads more, it has served its purpose\.

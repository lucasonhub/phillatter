# Mueller, Bernhard: Cadence: Learning Through Local Patch Settlement

**Autores:** 

**Data de descoberta:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** unknown

## Descrição do feed

Animals learn while they live\. A brain answers from what it knows, notices when the world contradicts it and changes just enough to fit, and much of what it learns comes as sequences whose payoff arrives at the end, with nothing to mark where one attempt stops and the next begins\. Cadence is a learning machine built on this picture\. Each of its patches predicts its own state from the signals it reads, and the patches repair their local disagreements until the network rests in one global equilibrium, which is the brain&\#x27;s answer and its present interpretation of the world\. When surprise, pain or need disturbs that equilibrium, the same local repair reaches the connections, which is learning, and the brain settles into a new equilibrium\. A calm creature answers from its settled state and learns nothing; an aroused one explores, learns from every outcome and writes memory\. The rule behind both is the two\-phase contrast of equilibrium propagation\. Tested against five solvers of the same equation, a squared\-residual descent and a changed schedule inside its learning phases, none was more effective, and a schedule that keeps every equilibrium changes the learning response by an exactly bounded amount\. Machine\-checked proofs show that a patch&\#x27;s repair needs only what arrives on its own contacts and never raises the shared energy, that weak coupling leaves one settled state and strong coupling several, that an admitted experience changes each connection by a bounded implicit delta rule, and that a disturbance moves a contracting equilibrium by a bounded amount which resettling removes geometrically\. An exact certificate gives a rate model compiled from a 25,609\-cell whole\-body connectome one equilibrium for every stimulus\. Hidden layers learn through the returning feedback to within 1\.1 percentage points of backpropagation on MNIST\. An episodic memory replays a reward back along the decisions that earned it, with outcomes alone bounding the episode\. On founders and seeds unused in its design, a four\-step crafting chain taught by a caregiver rises from 38% to 99% success and holds 98% when the creature continues alone, and a chain paid only at its end, in one life without attempt boundaries, rises from no greedy completions to a mean of 108 per thousand moments, learned on six of ten seeds\. The memory lowers the hit rate of a bandit whose history is irrelevant from 0\.86 to 0\.75 and ships as an option\. We propose that animal brains learn this way and state the tests that could refute the proposal\. \(direct link \)

In Miranda Anderson, Peter Garratt &amp; Mark Sprevak, Distributed Cognition in Victorian Culture and Modernism\. Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press\. pp\. 95\-112\. 2020This essay reads Marcel Proust&\#x27;s Swann&\#x27;s Way through the lens of extended and enactive theories of cognition, asking what happens to the self when it is conceived not as bounded and internal but as continuously coupled with environments, technologies and times\. While distributed accounts of cognition have unleashed the mind from its Cartesian boundaries, they have said comparatively little about how an extended self persists across time\. Proust, the essay argues, offers a powerful literary investigation of precisely this problem\. His protagonist&\#x27;s self is shown to be distributed along three axes: synchronically, in spatial couplings with rooms and objects; diachronically, in a history of structural couplings stabilised by habit; and heterocosmically, in a plurality of previously enacted and even imagined worlds that re\-emerge whenever habit misfires\. Drawing on Douglas Hofstadter&\#x27;s claim that analogy lies at the core of cognition, the essay contends that for Proust analogy is at once a cognitive process, an ontological condition, and a writing tool \- the &quot;miracle&quot; through which the self is both held together and exposed to its own groundlessness\. In conclusion, it suggests that Proust&\#x27;s distributed ontology can expand current cognitive accounts by foregrounding the role of analogy in selfhood and temporality\. \(direct link \)

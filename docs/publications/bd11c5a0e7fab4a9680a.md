# Can large language models reproduce humans’ perspective\-dependent judgements of gratitude? Evidence from Claude, GPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Doubao

**Autores:** Wenjun Chen; Yujie Chu; Xiaoming Jiang; Yan Li

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Human social interaction is full of episodes of help, and the person giving it and the person receiving it may not interpret the same episode in the same way\. Against a background of reports that large language models match or exceed human performance on social reasoning tasks, this study asks whether they reproduce this asymmetry between the two sides\. Forty\-eight Chinese university students read 24 everyday helping scenarios, each in four situational versions, from either the beneficiary’s or the benefactor’s side, and rated the helper’s willingness, the recipient’s need, the difficulty of helping, and the recipient’s gratitude\. The identical Chinese materials were then given to 12 models, the flagship and the light model of three providers based in English\-speaking countries and three based in China, with 30 independent runs per text, followed by two further prompt conditions: the same scenarios in English for the six models of the Chinese providers and a stated identity prepended to the prompt for all 12\. Bayesian mixed models showed a dissociation\. On what the situation called for, the models mostly matched the humans or exceeded them: the contrast between a pressing need and one already met was 1\.325 scale points in the humans and 1\.159 to 1\.825 across models, above the human value in 11 of 12\. On which side the episode was judged from, most fell short: the humans rated gratitude 0\.756 points higher from the beneficiary’s side, the models 0\.047 to 0\.727, with 11 of 12 credibly below the human estimate\. Only Doubao\-2\.1\-Pro matched the humans on gratitude, and it departed from them on willingness and difficulty\. Capability tier ordered the 12, though the contrast did not exclude zero, while the provider’s country did not order them at all\. Among the six models of the Chinese providers, writing the scenarios in English raised the effect in five, though not to the human estimate\. Likewise, across all 12, stating an identity raised it in 10, by 0\.10 on average against a mean shortfall of 0\.44, and 11 of 12 still sat below the human estimate\. Our results suggest that large language models, at least those surveyed here, read what a situation calls for at a level comparable to human readers, but that they show markedly less sensitivity to perspective, namely to the asymmetry with which benefactors and beneficiaries evaluate one and the same act of help\.

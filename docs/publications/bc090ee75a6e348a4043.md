# Mobility wearable devices in older adults: a scoping review of hardware, artificial intelligence software and socio\-ecological influences on device use

**Autores:** Harjot Suri; Areeya Hillman; Ashely Chan; Chukwuebuka Prince Onyekere; Peace Kumapayi; Ethan Chan; Ambika Mehta; Michael Ebe Kalu

**Data:** 2026-10-13 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

PURPOSE: This scoping review maps mobility wearable device hardware and artificial intelligence\(AI\) software and synthesise socioecological factors influencing device use among older adults aged 65 years and older\. METHODS: We followed the Arksey and O&\#x27;Malley five\-step framework and searched five databases and one data interface \(OVID\) between January 2010 to December 2025\. Two reviewers independently screened studies and extracted data in Covidence© using predefined eligibility criteria\. Data were analysed in pairs and synthesised using the socio\-ecological framework and an AI taxonomy \(unsupervised, semi\-supervised, supervised, deep learning and reinforcement learning\)\. RESULTS: We included 197 studies from 27 countries, predominantly quantitative \(97\.0%\)\. Mobility monitoring relied on accelerometers \(63\.5%\), plastic \(87%\) and worn at the hip/waist\. Fourteen studies employed AI\-enabled analytics, of which 13 used supervised learning and one used both supervised and deep learning approaches\. A socioecological framework guided us to derived 22 themes, 10 opportunities and 12 challenges, across individual, interpersonal, environmental and policy levels\. Real\-time mobility monitoring with feedback was the unifying opportunity, while privacy, data governance, trust and digital literacy inequities were recurring constraints, underscoring the need for inclusive, equity\-oriented design\. Level\-specific opportunities included cognitive and mental health benefits \(individual\), social motivation \(interpersonal\), workforce efficiency \(organisational\) and reimbursement evolution \(policy\)\. Level\-specific challenges included acceptability \(individual\), autonomy and dependence tensions \(interpersonal\), standardisation and integration barriers \(organisational\) and regulatory gaps \(policy\)\. CONCLUSION: The evidence base remains methodologically fragmented, with limited longitudinal real\-world evaluation of AI models\. Progress will require addressing multilevel implementation, integration and equity barriers that shape real world adoption among older adults\.

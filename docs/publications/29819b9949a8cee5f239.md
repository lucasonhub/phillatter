# Steel mill scale in concrete and mortar: a critical review of the evidence base and a proposed framework for future quantitative synthesis

**Autores:** Loganathan G\. \-; M\. Senthil Pandian; S\. Deepa Nivethika

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Steel mill scale is an iron\-oxide\-rich by\-product of hot rolling that is generated at roughly 1%–3% of the mass of steel processed and is still largely landfilled or downcycled\. Its high iron content, high specific gravity and fine flake morphology have prompted a growing number of laboratory investigations into its use in concrete and mortar, principally as a partial replacement for fine aggregate and, far more rarely, for cement\. This article critically reviews that literature\. Rather than presenting a pooled quantitative synthesis, it establishes why such a synthesis is not yet defensible and specifies the conditions under which it would become so\. We first characterise mill scale as a material, drawing a distinction—routinely blurred in the construction\-materials literature—between X\-ray fluorescence compositions, in which all iron is reported arithmetically as Fe 2 O 3 , and X\-ray diffraction phase quantifications, which resolve the wüstite \(FeO\), magnetite \(Fe 3 O 4 \) and hematite \(Fe 2 O 3 \) actually present\. We then map the reported effects of mill scale on workability, compressive and tensile strength, transport\-related durability and environmental behaviour, and show that the reported optimum replacement level is not consistent across the corpus, clustering near 15% by volume in mortar and self\-compacting concrete but near 50%–60% by mass in several conventional concrete studies\. Quantitative pooling is currently obstructed by six specific and documentable deficiencies: heterogeneous replacement bases \(mass versus volume; aggregate versus binder\), inconsistent curing ages and outcome definitions, uncharacterised variation in mill\-scale provenance, near\-universal absence of dispersion statistics and replicate counts, confounding of mill scale with co\-additives, and probable non\-independence among studies from single research groups\. We therefore present a pre\-specified meta\-analytic protocol—effect\-size definition, random\-effects estimation, heterogeneity quantification, subgroup and meta\-regression structure, sensitivity analysis and small\-study\-effect assessment—to be executed once the reporting deficiencies identified here are remedied, together with a minimum reporting checklist for primary studies\. The evidence currently supports cautious use of mill scale in non\-structural and specialty applications; it does not yet support quantitative performance benchmarks for structural concrete\.

# Diet\-driven cardiometabolic dysfunction as a potential amplifier of antimicrobial resistance: mechanistic evidence, gut resistome ecology, and dual\-action nutraceutical interventions

**Autores:** Michael Tosin Bayode; Bashirat Akinbami; Christianah Titilayo Oladele; Ayomide Faith Okiti; Esther Oluwadarasimi Oyedeji

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Over the last 30 years, two population\-scale health crises have emerged as the epidemic of diet\-related cardiometabolic disease \(obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease\) and the spread of antimicrobial resistance \(AMR\) as a major cause of death globally\. Typically, they are investigated and controlled independently of each other\. AMR may be amplified by diet\-induced cardiometabolic dysfunction, which is not a parallel but an upstream, under\-recognized burden\. We describe four convergent pathways that might contribute to the increased risk of resistance\. Impaired host immunity leading to recurrent, difficult\-to\-treat infection and repeated antibiotic exposure; obesity\-associated pharmacokinetic changes leading to subtherapeutic antibiotic concentrations in the system; diet\-induced gut dysbiosis that increases the reservoir of antibiotic\-resistance genes \(the gut resistome\); and a bidirectional loop in which cumulative antibiotic exposure itself seems to increase cardiovascular risk\. We then placed functional foods, nutraceuticals, and specific bioactive compounds as dual\-action levers that act on the metabolic driver and, for a subset of molecules, directly on bacterial fitness, virulence, or the resistome\. The clearest single\-molecule example is developed in the form of berberine, which provides metabolic correction through the AMPK pathway, while simultaneously providing FtsZ\-targeting and efflux\-modulating antibacterial activity\. We propose a testable framework, the metabolic–AMR augmentation loop, and highlight the gaps in evidence for longitudinal and interventional studies\. If this framework proves to be robust, then diet\-induced metabolic dysfunction correction could become a viable AMR\-mitigation strategy on its own and one not currently employed in existing stewardship programs\.

# Animal state and husbandry context profiles in limited\-access zoo welfare assessment: evidence from 20 mammalian species

**Autores:** Ji\-yoon Kim; WoonSun Lee; Dae\-Won Jeong; Min\-gu Kim

**Data:** 2026-10-13 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Animal welfare assessment draws on evidence about both the animal’s observed state and the husbandry conditions in which it lives, but these sources of evidence are related rather than equivalent\. Under limited\-access conditions, combining them too early may obscure where welfare concern is located and may encourage unsupported interpretation of observed states as consequences of current husbandry\. This study applied an Animal State–Husbandry Context \(ASHC\) Grid to 20 mammalian species at a public zoo to examine whether keeping these evidence domains separate could retain information that broader summary scores may conceal\. Assessment was restricted to evidence available from public viewing areas; keeper interviews, internal husbandry and veterinary records, and off\-exhibit access were not used\. Eleven assessors worked in three\-person teams, scoring Animal State \(AS\) and Husbandry Context \(HC\) separately before individually revising their scores following shared evidence review\. Overall post\-review inter\-assessor agreement was high for AS, HC and Overall ASHC \(Krippendorff’s α = 0\.961, 0\.932 and 0\.939, respectively\), although lower agreement remained for Nutritional provision \(α = 0\.657\), Enrichment \(α = 0\.721\) and Choice and control \(α = 0\.766\)\. Across species, mean HC was 4\.24 and mean AS was 2\.78, with HC exceeding AS in 18 of 20 species\. Similar Overall ASHC values could nevertheless contain markedly different AS–HC compositions, while indicator\-level profiles showed that similar domain summaries could arise from different underlying concerns\. These findings suggest that separating Animal State from Husbandry Context can preserve the location and composition of observable welfare concern under limited\-access conditions\. ASHC is intended as a complementary framework for organising available evidence and directing follow\-up assessment, rather than as an independent welfare diagnosis or a basis for causal attribution\.

# Neuromyths about motor development and coordination among Hungarian teachers: prevalence and associations with completion of motor therapy training

**Autores:** Katalin Kälbli; Julianna Víg; Mónika Kaj; Bence Kas; Tamás Berki; László Tóth; Tamás Csányi

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Neuromyths—misconceptions arising from the misinterpretation or oversimplification of neuroscientific findings—are common among educators and may influence educational practice\. However, neuromyths about motor development and coordination remain relatively underexplored\. This study examined the prevalence of movement\-related neuromyths among Hungarian in\-service teachers, with particular emphasis on whether endorsement differed according to completion of motor therapy training, and explored additional factors associated with incorrect responses\. A cross\-sectional survey was conducted among 734 Hungarian in\-service teachers, including 206 who had completed motor therapy training\. Participants completed the 44\-item Hungarian Neuroliteracy Questionnaire, of which 13 items addressed motor development and coordination\. Descriptive statistics, Mann–Whitney U tests, and ordinal logistic regression were used for analysis\. The prevalence of incorrect responses across the 13 statements ranged from 7\.6% to 95\.8%, indicating that misconceptions about motor development and coordination were common\. Teachers who had completed motor therapy training showed a higher mean incorrect response rate than those who had not \(77\.9% vs\. 71\.2%\) and a lower rate of uncertainty \(“I don&\#x27;t know”: 3\.6% vs\. 10\.3%\)\. Completion of motor therapy training was independently associated with higher odds of belonging to a higher incorrect\-response category \(OR=1\.672, 95% CI \[1\.177, 2\.374\], p = 0\.004\)\. More frequent use of pedagogical journals and textbooks was also associated with higher incorrect response rates, whereas more frequent use of neuropedagogical methods in professional practice and English\-language peer\-reviewed journals was associated with lower incorrect response rates\. These findings indicate that neuromyths related to motor development and coordination are highly prevalent among Hungarian in\-service teachers and that completion of motor therapy training is associated with greater, rather than lower endorsement of such misconceptions\. Strengthening evidence\-informed professional development and neuroscience literacy, as well as improving access to high\-quality scientific literature, may help teachers critically evaluate neuroscience\-related claims and support more evidence\-informed educational practice\.

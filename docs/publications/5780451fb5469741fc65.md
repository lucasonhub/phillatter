# Artificial Intelligence, Synchrotron Imaging, and the Virtual Unwrapping of the Herculaneum Papyri

**Autores:** Zen Revista

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

In the autumn of 79 CE, the eruption of Mount Vesuvius destroyed the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum and, in doing so, produced one of the most paradoxical archives in the history of the written word\. The pyroclastic flows that swept through Herculaneum carbonized the papyrus scrolls of a wealthy seaside villa, transforming an elegant private library into several hundred brittle lumps of charcoal\. That carbonization, ordinarily an agent of total destruction, turned out to be the very condition of the texts’ survival: hermetically sealed within their own combustion products and buried beneath approximately twenty meters of volcanic material, the rolls escaped the humidity, insects, mold, and human neglect that erased nearly every other large library of classical antiquity \[9, 17\]\. The result is the only large\-scale library to have survived from the Greco\-Roman world, containing hundreds of Greek and Latin scrolls, many of which preserve otherwise unknown philosophical treatises, including forty\-four works attributed to the Epicurean philosopher and poet Philodemus of Gadara \[17, 26\]\. For almost two and a half centuries, however, survival and readability remained fundamentally opposed\. The physical opening of a carbonized scroll, whether through mechanical devices, chemical softeners, or painstaking manual intervention, exposed its text at an irreversible cost, damaging or destroying the very object under investigation \[26\]\. The problem was compounded by the chemistry of the writing itself: the ink used in the Herculaneum scrolls is essentially carbon\-based and has a composition and density remarkably similar to those of the carbonized papyrus substrate\. Consequently, even when a scroll could be imaged externally, its writing remained radiographically invisible \[10, 11\]\. Reading a closed Herculaneum scroll therefore required overcoming a double impossibility: examining a fragile object without physically opening it and distinguishing black ink from a blackened substrate\. The past decade has progressively dismantled both barriers\. Between 2015 and 2026, a succession of breakthroughs, including X\-ray phase\-contrast tomography \[10\], the complete virtual unwrapping of a carbonized scroll \[16\], machine\-learning techniques for detecting carbon ink in computed tomography scans \[11\], and the open, prize\-based Vesuvius Challenge \[13\], transformed what had appeared to be an enduring impasse into a scalable research program\. Its most recent and dramatic milestone, announced on June 25, 2026, was the complete virtual unwrapping and reading of PHerc\. 1667, the first Herculaneum papyrus to be digitally unrolled and read from beginning to end without any physical opening \[1, 25\]\. In the same announcement, the project reported that more than seventy columns of text had been read from PHerc\. 172, a scroll whose title, Philodemus, On Vices, Book 1, had been recovered a year earlier, marking the first time the title of an unopened Herculaneum scroll had been read \[20, 24\]\. A companion video by Brazilian science communicator Izabela Anholett, published on YouTube Shorts on October 16, 2026, brought this story to a broad Portuguese\-speaking audience\. Its thumbnail is reproduced in Figure 1, and the video is cited throughout this study as an example of public science communication \[2\]\.

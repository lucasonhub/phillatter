abstract: 'Population ageing and the rapid diffusion of generative artificial intelligence
  are two defining transformations of the twenty-first century, yet they are rarely
  considered together. Generative artificial intelligence (generative AI) is moving
  from a technological novelty to an everyday infrastructure. It drafts emails, summarises
  documents, translates services, produces images, answers health questions and increasingly
  mediates first-line interactions with organisations. Public debate has focused heavily
  on schools, universities, workplaces, productivity, misinformation and regulation.
  Much less attention has been paid to how this transformation intersects with population
  ageing and the increasingly digital organisation of later life. This omission matters
  because generative AI is not simply another digital tool. It is becoming part of
  the infrastructure through which people search for knowledge, assess risks, access
  services and imagine social groups.Older adults (people aged 65 and older, in line
  with the statutory retirement age in Switzerland) are not a marginal user group;
  they are a growing share of the population in many countries and are increasingly
  expected to navigate digital welfare, health, financial and communication systems.
  Yet research and design practices often continue to position older adults as late
  adopters, vulnerable users or recipients of technological solutions rather than
  as citizens whose agency, knowledge and social representation matter in the future
  AI ecosystem (Mannheim et al., 2023;Choi et al., 2026).Existing concepts illuminate
  different aspects of this problem. Research on digital inequality has long conceptualized
  the "digital divide" as multidimensional, encompassing inequalities in access, skills,
  use, and outcomes (Dijk, 2020;Bentley et al., 2024). Research on digital inequality
  has long understood the digital divide as multidimensional. It encompasses inequalities
  in material access, digital skills, forms and quality of use, and the benefits or
  disadvantages resulting from use. Research on digital ageism, in turn, examines
  how age-based assumptions and inequalities are embedded in and reproduced through
  digital technologies (Mannheim and Köttl, 2024). The two mechanisms underlying the
  proposed framework, unequal participation and biased representation, are thus already
  established in the literature, but neither has been specifically applied to generative
  AI. The doubledivide framework''s contribution lies in examining how these mechanisms
  operate together and may reinforce each other in this context.The proposed double-divide
  framework distinguishes inequality in engaging with generative AI (Wang et al.,
  2025) from inequality in being represented by it (Chu et al., 2022): First, older
  adults may face an AI Participation Divide: inequalities in the ability to access,
  use, interpret and critically evaluate AI-generated information. Second, they may
  face an AI Representation Divide: inequalities in how ageing and older adults are
  represented through AI-generated text, images and decisions. These two divides are
  distinct but connected. Together, they may shape who benefits from AI and how ageing
  itself is understood.The promise of generative AI for older adultsGenerative AI
  can offer substantial benefits in later life. Its natural language interface may
  lower the barriers that made earlier digital technologies difficult to use. A person
  who struggles with search engines, online forms or administrative language may ask
  a chatbot to explain a letter, draft a response, translate information or compare
  options. Such functions could support autonomy and participation when they are reliable,
  accessible and embedded in meaningful social contexts. Unlike many earlier digital
  technologies, generative AI does not require users to navigate complex menus or
  predefined categories. Its conversational form may allow older adults to start with
  questions in their own words. In health, AI-driven tools may help explain medical
  information, support appointment preparation, make complex instructions easier to
  understand and offer new forms of communication support. For older adults with sensory
  limitations, low digital confidence or limited formal education, conversational
  interfaces could be more approachable than menu-based systems. Emerging work on
  AI-based health technologies and assistive technologies suggests that older adults
  often see potential benefits, especially when technologies are useful, understandable,
  trustworthy and aligned with their everyday lives (Enam et al., 2025;Wong et al.,
  2025). This promise matters. If generative AI is well designed, it could become
  an important tool for inclusion. The problem is that benefits do not flow automatically
  from availability. Access to an AI system does not mean that people know what to
  ask, how to interpret answers or how to judge the accuracy of the output. This is
  where the first divide begins.In the age of generative AI, the crucial question
  is not only whether older adults can access generative AI but also whether they
  have the competence to use it effectively and critically. We define the AI Participation
  Divide as the unequal capacity to access, use, interpret and evaluate AIgenerated
  information. Age alone does not determine AI competence. Inequalities within later
  life reflect accumulated differences in education, income, health, prior occupational
  exposure to technology, social support and opportunities for digital learning (Enam
  et al., 2025;Suárez and García-Mariñoso, 2025).The first layer is access and use:
  Generative AI may be freely available through search engines, smartphones and apps,
  but this does not mean that its use is evenly distributed. Evidence on AIrelated
  competencies shows that vulnerable user groups with lower AI knowledge and skills
  are more likely to be older, less educated and less equipped with privacy-protection
  skills (Wang et al., 2025).Behavioural evidence on ChatGPT use likewise points to
  generational and educational differences in adoption (Kacperski et al., 2025). These
  findings suggest that AI may extend rather than erase established inequalities in
  digital participation.The second layer is meaningful use: Generative AI requires
  a new repertoire of practical skills. Users need to know what AI can do, which tasks
  it is appropriate for, how to formulate prompts, when to provide context and when
  not to disclose sensitive information. These are not trivial skills. They are learned
  through experimentation, social support and prior digital confidence. Existing digital
  experiences can therefore spill over into relationships with AI: People who have
  felt excluded from digital technology may approach AI with lower confidence, fewer
  positive experiences and less trust (Bentley et al., 2024).The third layer is interpretation:
  AI outputs often sound fluent, confident and authoritative, even when they are incomplete,
  biased or wrong. Older adults, like all users, need to understand that generative
  AI produces plausible responses rather than verified truth. This distinction is
  especially important when AI is used for health information, financial questions
  or legal guidance. The danger is not only non-use but overtrust: a person may use
  AI and still be disadvantaged because they cannot recognise the limits of the answer.The
  fourth layer is evaluation. This may become the defining competence of the generative
  AI era.Evaluation competence includes more than checking whether a statement is
  factually correct. It also involves recognising when an image, voice, recommendation
  or personalised message may have been synthetically generated, manipulated or designed
  to exploit trust. For older adults, this is particularly crucial in domains where
  trust relationships matter, such as health advice, social communication, banking
  and public services. Users increasingly need to detect hallucinations, misleading
  summaries, manipulated images, synthetic voices, scams and AI-generated fake content.
  Older adults are often framed as vulnerable to online misinformation (Brashier and
  Schacter, 2020;Li et al., 2025), yet the question should not be reduced to individual
  deficit. The information environment itself is becoming harder to evaluate, especially
  for older adults with limited familiarity with generative AI. As services, media
  and interpersonal communication are increasingly saturated with synthetic content,
  the critical evaluation of AI becomes a basic condition of social participation.
  The result is not merely an individual skills gap, but a societal divide in the
  ability to judge the credibility, authenticity and trustworthiness of AI-mediated
  information.The AI Participation Divide thus refers to unequal opportunities to
  access, use, interpret, and evaluate generative AI, and to participate in its design
  and evaluation. These inequalities do not arise solely from individual abilities,
  but from the interaction of personal resources with interface design, accessibility,
  affordability, institutional practices, and available support. Participation is
  therefore relational, depending on both users and the conditions under which AI
  is designed, provided, and supported.The second divide concerns representation.
  The divide lies between the diversity of later life as it is lived and the restricted
  repertoire through which AI systems often make it visible. Generative AI does not
  merely help people retrieve information; it also produces text, images and narratives
  that circulate through society. When people ask AI systems to describe older adults,
  design services for ageing populations, generate images of later life or summarise
  what ageing means, the outputs participate in shaping cultural understandings of
  age. This matters because ageism is already embedded in technology design and AI
  systems (Martens et al., 2025;Osinga et al., 2026). Reviews of digital technology
  design show that older adults are frequently framed through assumptions of decline,
  dependency and lack of competence (Mannheim et al., 2023). Work on sociotechnical
  ageism shows how AI in care and ageing contexts can reproduce narrow ideas of old
  age as a problem to be managed through technical solutions (Neves et al., 2023).
  A scoping review of agerelated bias in AI further shows that age bias may enter
  AI systems through data, design, modelling and deployment, including through underrepresentation
  of older adults and biased evaluation of system performance (Chu et al., 2023).
  Generative AI intensifies this issue because it makes representation scalable. Repetition
  gives such representations social weight. When similar ageist framings appear across
  chatbots, image generators, search interfaces and administrative tools, they may
  come to look less like stereotypes and more like common sense (Martens et al., 2025)
  Avoiding a double divide requires more than giving older adults access to AI tools.
  First, AI literacy initiatives must include later life and move beyond basic awareness.
  These need to teach practical prompting, interpretation of uncertainty, privacy
  protection and critical evaluation of AI-generated content, including health misinformation,
  scams, deepfakes and synthetic media. Second, AI systems should be evaluated for
  age-related bias not only in technical performance but also in the narratives and
  images they generate. Third, older adults should be involved as co-designers of
  AI systems and not merely as test users. A recent scoping review shows that older
  adults are still inconsistently engaged across the design, implementation and evaluation
  of AI systems (Choi et al., 2026).Participation must include heterogeneous groups
  of older adults and should address power imbalances in design processes. Fourth,
  essential health, welfare, financial and public services should retain accessible
  non-AI alternatives. Age-inclusive AI cannot mean making participation conditional
  on the ability or willingness to interact with automated systems. Fifth, AI governance
  should treat age as a core dimension of fairness, inclusion and representation.
  This includes audit practices that examine whether AI systems exclude older adults
  from benefits or reproduce ageist assumptions in outputs and decisions. The proposed
  policy and design recommendations are normative proposals informed by the preceding
  evidence and conceptual analysis. Their effectiveness should be examined empirically
  across different populations, settings, and generative AI systems.Age-inclusive
  AI therefore requires both the capacity to act within AI-mediated environments and
  the right to be represented by them in ways that reflect the diversity, agency and
  complexity of later life.Without such efforts, generative AI may widen a double
  divide in ageing societies, one that limits older adults'' capacity to act in AI-mediated
  environments and narrows how ageing itself is seen.'

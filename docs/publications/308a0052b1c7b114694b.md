## Descrição do feed

In Nathaniel F\. Barrett, Live and Direct: Color, Perception, and Experience\. Pamplona, Spain: EUNSA / Museo Universidad de Navarra\. 2026People commonly assume color to be a stable feature of things in the world; barring changes to the physical properties of objects, we expect their colors to stay the same\. You might think, for example, that your favorite mono\-pigmented coffee mug has one true color—say, a specific shade of cerulean blue—not two or five\. The color of the mug might change if the pigment fades or the mug gets stained, but without such material alterations, you take its color to be fixed\. And if somebody insists that the mug is aqua green, you will likely think they are mistaken\. If color is often assumed to be a stable property of objects, color vision is taken to give us access to that stable property, at least under normal or optimal conditions\. You know that your coffee mug is a specific type of cerulean blue because it looks that way to you in good light, from an appropriate vantage point, when your visual system is working properly\. This suggests that your color vision acts like a detector or a recording device; just as thermometers mechanistically record temperatures and output matching temperature readings \(such as “25°C”\), color visual systems mechanistically record colors and output matching color experiences \(such as experiences of a type of cerulean blue\)\. You might also think that your color visual system is generally good at this task and reliably enables you to see the colors of things\. But does color vision really give us access to stable colors out there in the world, and is that its job? Considerations of color illusions, art, and visual ecology give us good reasons to think otherwise\.

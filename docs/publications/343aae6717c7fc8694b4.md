# Cica, Mateo: Problems of Self\-Thematization of the I

**Autores:** 

**Data de descoberta:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** unknown

## Descrição do feed

The problem of the self\-thematization of the self can be illustrated particularly well through Augustine’s understanding of time, according to which time is something that is perpetually approaching and passing away and, fundamentally, never constitutes something fully completed or concluded\. Furthermore, the self may seemingly be located as a neural network while a subject, for instance, lies in an MRI scanner and is instructed to speak about and thematize themselves\. Certain areas of the brain will undoubtedly light up on the screen; however, this constitutes a purely organic, biochemical, or material localization rather than a mental one\. To the extent that we possess no organ through which we can perceive time sensorially, we likewise possess no organ through which we can locate the self\. This is due, to quote Aristotle at this point, to the fact that “the whole is greater than the sum of its parts\.” On the basis of this analogy, two fundamental problems concerning the self\-thematization of the self can be identified\. First, there is the problem of locating the self and, consequently, the question of what the self consists of and where it can be found within us\. Furthermore, Augustine’s understanding of time allows us to demonstrate that things understood within time are always undergoing continuous transformation, and that a static, determinate self can therefore never exist in its entirety\. “I speak through myself about myself\.” This structure entails a meta\-level, insofar as the “I” does not speak about things \(understanding\) but rather about itself \(reason\)\. Consequently, the person who performs this act would develop a “meta\-self\.” Yet this would mean that the “meta\-self” could once again use itself to speak about the “meta\-self,” thereby giving rise to a “meta\-meta\-self\.” This process would continue indefinitely\. However, this does not constitute an infinite regress in the classical sense of a regressus ad infinitum; rather, it constitutes an eternal anticipation or projection toward the infinite\. This suggests that the self\-thematization of the self cannot ultimately achieve its intended objective, and that the discovery of one’s own “self” may perhaps lie not in the attempt to search for the self through the self, but rather beyond what we ordinarily call the self\. Let us assume that the self is not the most highly developed understanding of the self through the self, but rather what remains once all the layers imposed upon the human being from the outside through development, education, and socialization have been stripped away\. At this point, we encounter the classical regressus ad infinitum, which would continue to regress toward an ever more primordial level in order to arrive at an increasingly fundamental understanding of one’s own identity\. Both processes would continue indefinitely, each requiring yet another step, and could therefore never reach an endpoint\. In this way, they illustrate two further theoretical problems inherent in the self\-thematization of the self\.

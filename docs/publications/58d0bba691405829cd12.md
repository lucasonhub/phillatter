# Peacock, Thomas: What Mattering Is: The Closure Of Value

## Descrição do feed

Positive valence is the only thing that is intrinsically good\. Negative valence is the only thing that is intrinsically bad\. I have argued for this before by taking the goods people believe in and showing, one at a time, that the value drains out of each when the feeling is removed\. That method cannot finish\. However many goods are dissolved, a reader can always say that theirs has not been reached, as there are too many entries on the list of things human beings have wanted, and it proves less than it looks\. A good that vanishes whenever feeling is withdrawn has been shown to travel with feeling, and travelling together is not the same thing as being\. This paper takes a different route\. Instead of asking which things are good, it asks what it is for anything to be good at all\. To call something intrinsically good is to say it is to\-be\-pursued: that its presence gives reasons\. No arrangement of matter gives reasons on its own, and any attempt to install the reason\-giving as an extra property of the arrangement produces something that no faculty detects and nothing is moved by\. Felt valence is the single exception\. The badness of agony is not a judgement passed on a sensation after the fact; it arrives inside the sensation as the way the sensation presents itself\. Valence is itself an arrangement of matter and energy, and the objection that this makes it no different from any other arrangement is answered: the question was never whether valence is physical, but whether a second fact must be supplied to get from the phenomenon to its value\. For an arrangement that produces no experience anywhere, a second fact must be supplied, and supplying it is where the trouble starts\. Anyone who calls a thing intrinsically good is committed to an answer about where its goodness sits: in the world with no subject at all, in a subject who is not affected in experience, or in what a subject feels\. Moore&\#x27;s method of judging value by imagining things in isolation belongs to the first, and so does recent work on solitary goods; the second holds Nagel on posthumous harm, along with the objective\-list, perfectionist, desire\-satisfaction, constructivist and fitting\-attitude traditions\. Both are emptied here\. The first names nothing, because a good that is good for no one has lost the argument that made it a good\. The second has no ground of its own: every improvement it makes either reaches an experience, in which case it has conceded, or reaches none, in which case it has joined the first\. Ben Bramble argued that the second region was empty before I did; what I add is not the requirement but its ground\. The remainder works out the shape of the one value\. Intensity and duration carry it, and type carries none of it, so the folk hierarchy of pains and pleasures turns out to be a hierarchy of quantities misdescribed as kinds\. Relief of suffering takes precedence over the addition of pleasure wherever the two compete\. No quantity of pleasure outweighs one being&\#x27;s agony, and the reason is not that the pleasures count for little\. It is that if the beings were asked to give them up, they would lose nothing that is harmful for them to lose: one side of that comparison has a victim, and the other has nobody who is harmed at all\. I then run the reduction again with controls, meet the cases where valence and value most appear to come apart, explain why so few people believe any of this, and set out what is still unsettled\.

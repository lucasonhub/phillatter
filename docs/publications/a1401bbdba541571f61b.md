\_American Scientist\_ 114 \(July\-August\):240\-247\. 2026How can we tell whether a nonhuman animal, brain\-injured patient, or artificial system is conscious? Contemporary consciousness science often approaches this question by identifying behavioral or anatomical “markers” of consciousness—e\.g\. purposive action, learning, play, motivational trade\-offs, mirror self\-recognition, metacognition, or particular neural structures\. But the marker strategy faces a measurement problem\. To establish that a proposed marker reliably indicates consciousness, we must already know which beings are conscious; yet determining which beings are conscious is precisely what the appeal to markers is supposed to accomplish in the first place\. The problem is at least as old as 19th\-century debates over Eduard Pflüger’s experiments on decapitated frogs\. Pflüger found that a brainless frog deprived of its favored wiping leg could use another limb to remove acid from its body\. He took purposiveness as a consciousness marker for vitalist reasons; so he interpreted his result as evidence that consciousness emerges from a complex nervous system, even without a brain\. Mechanists such as Thomas Henry Huxley drew the opposite conclusion: if a headless frog could try different means to achieve an end, then purposive behavior must not be a reliable marker of consciousness after all\. Each side interpreted the evidence through a different theory of mind\. This essay argues that William James developed an approach that skirts the problem\. Rather than infer consciousness from markers, he examined the functional deficits produced by cerebral ablation\. Decerebrate animals retained complex behaviors but became rigid, stimulus\-bound, unmotivated, and insensitive to relative value\. James explained these losses through an evolutionary hypothesis: consciousness is localized in the hemispheres and regulates behavior by enabling organisms to represent absent possibilities, evaluate competing actions, and act prudently\. Modern debates over animal and artificial consciousness reproduce the 19th\-century impasse\. The essay therefore recommends following James in grounding claims about consciousness in independently supported theories of evolutionary function, experimentally observed incapacities, and first\-person evidence—not in markers\.

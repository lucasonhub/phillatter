# Here is Why Artificial Intelligence Will Never Replace Mathematicians

**Autores:** Ben F\.T\. Tibola

**Data:** 2026-10-10 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

The Question Everyone Is Forgetting OpenAI&\#x27;s release of more than 700 mathematical manuscripts has provoked an extraordinary question: is this the final nail in the coffin? Are mathematicians no longer needed? Mathematicians, and indeed the world, are so busy debating whether mathematicians will remain relevant now that artificial intelligence can apparently solve some of mathematics&\#x27; hardest problems that everyone is overlooking a much more fundamental question: Who conceived those problems in the first place? Who decided that these were the questions worth asking? Understanding this is, I believe, the most essential part of the entire debate\. The question is not who can perform the greatest number of successful mathematical operations\. It is who gave mathematics its problems, and what is lost when the development of an answer is treated as disposable\. I am concerned with the distinction between the final theorem, the proof establishing it, and the intellectual journeys through which new questions appear before that theorem has been reached\. We celebrate the mathematician who solves a difficult problem\. We award prizes for proofs, commemorate breakthroughs, and measure mathematical progress by the number and significance of problems resolved\. But before any of these achievements became possible, someone had to conceive the question\. Before a theorem could be proved, someone had to recognize that there was something worth proving\. Before an equation could be solved, someone had to recognize that a relationship required mathematical expression\. And before a field of mathematics could exist, human beings had to develop the intellectual appetite, intuition, and conceptual language that made its questions conceivable\. This is not a minor preliminary step\. It is the foundational act of mathematical creation\. Consider the Navier–Stokes problem\. Its mathematical formulation emerged from humanity&\#x27;s investigation of fluids, motion, physical forces, and the behavior of matter\. The equations were not waiting in a computer as an arbitrary collection of symbols\. They arose from human beings observing the world, attempting to understand it, and translating that encounter into mathematics\. The same is true, in different ways, of the questions that gave rise to geometry, calculus, probability, topology, number theory, and the countless mathematical structures that followed\. Even when mathematics eventually becomes so abstract that its connection to ordinary experience is almost impossible to recognize, its intellectual ancestry remains human\. This matters precisely because we may be entering an era in which an AI system can deliver a proposed proof faster than a mathematical community can assimilate it\. If we measure mathematical progress only by the answers delivered, the most consequential losses may escape our measurement altogether\. The important question is therefore not simply whether AI can solve the Riemann hypothesis, the Navier–Stokes problem, or hundreds of other conjectures\. It is whether solving questions conceived within the human mathematical enterprise is equivalent to possessing the intelligence, intuition, and origins of thought that created that enterprise\. I argue that it is not\. Solving a problem and being the kind of intelligence from which the problem was born are two fundamentally different accomplishments\.

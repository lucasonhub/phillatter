# Norman, John Mark: Dialog 2\.5: Beyond the Room – Animal Minds and the Looping Prestige Error

**Autores:** 

**Data de descoberta:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** unknown

## Descrição do feed

What if one of the main obstacles to understanding animal minds is that humans are taking just one particularly formatted mode of their own experience as the sole form of experience? And what if they also set up that particular format as a standard, and then set off looking for it in other animals, even though those other animals might be having their own sort of experience, formatted in largely unimaginable ways? This dialogue considers precisely that possibility, calling it the Looping Prestige Error\. In order to approach this question analytically, the dialogue introduces a set of terms and their definitions: content, attendance, experience, looped content, and looped experience \(or subjective experience\)\. These are not presented as discoveries of the framework but as a set of defined terms\. Then, based on these terms, the dialogue shows why humans are generally led to assume that looped experience \(subjective experience\) is their only form of experience\. To better illustrate this point, the dialogue introduces the metaphor of a house with various rooms, the looping room being just one of them\. Then, considering non\-human animals, the interlocutors discuss how any species with a pair of channels that can express content as an external signal and also receive external signals and render them as content could also develop a form of looping\. If they do, they will also have looped experience, but formatted in their own way, which is not necessarily imaginable to humans, as even our imagination relies on the format allowed by our own looping\. But broadly speaking, to have experience, animals do not need to loop: even an animal whose sensory content remains unlooped is still an agent attending to and experiencing that content\. Along the way, the dialogue points out the importance of the framework’s Looping Indeterminacy Principle and its Coterminous Principle in relation to these questions\. The latter raises a different problem for thinking about animal minds\. Imagination is itself a looping operation, so when humans use their imagination to consider what another animal’s experience is like, they are necessarily working only with the distinctive formatting of the human expressive\-receptive system\. In the case of another animal species, however, its looped content will be limited to the possibilities provided by its own expressive and receptive architecture\. Although the two might overlap, they may differ, and perhaps substantially, in ways that are necessarily unimaginable to humans\. An answer to Nagel’s familiar question about what it is like to be a bat is therefore impeded not only by a lack of information about the bat, but also by a possible architectural limitation in the human capacity to imagine the answer\. The aphasia example suggests that disruption of overt language functions need not eliminate looping at more inward stages\. For their part, cases involving blindsight and spatial neglect provide examples of how experience, broadly speaking, can take place outside of looped experience, with sensory content remaining available to the BAL even when it is unavailable for report\. The interlocutors discuss the distinction between the content of experience and the experiencing agent itself: in the framework, the BAL is the experiencer, while looped subjective experience is only one form of its experience – its attendance to looped, formatted content\. They then briefly discuss the well\-known reflections of Nagel and Chalmers as familiar landmarks to help situate the distinctive viewpoint offered by the BAL\-looping framework\. Ultimately, the dialogue shows that the larger question of experience should remain framed by animal experience in general, with human looped experience \(subjective experience\) as a special case, not a standard\. Stated positively, in each case we should ask what kind of experiencing agent is there, what content it can attend to, and what forms of experience its own cognitive architecture makes possible\. \(This third version clarifies the distinction between experience and looped subjective experience, making explicit that the absence of looping does not imply an absence of experience\. It retains the refinements to the Nagel and Chalmers discussion introduced in version 2, along with the improvements to transitions and flow in the later sections\.\)

  larger problem. The first was to establish the culture infrastructure to link a
  single optical measurement to the eventual fate of an embryo; the second was to

  and the third was to re-engineer the imaging instrument itself to a more stable,
  physiologically interpretable configuration that is the system''s future.The first
  study was a foundation, testing that in vitro-produced (IVP) porcine embryos can
  be cultured in the Well-of-the-Well (WOW) microwell culture system. In contrast
  to conventional group culture, which uses 10 or more embryos in one droplet of medium,
  the WOW dishes allow each embryo to be cultured in a separate microwell in a single
  droplet of medium, yet preserving the autocrine and paracrine benefits of group
  culture. This was a prerequisite for a subsequent study to correlate BDI spectral
  reading with the actual developmental result of an embryo. In a first experiment,
  cumulus-oocyte complexes under standardized in vitro maturation (IVM) and in vitro
  fertilization (IVF) conditions were allocated to five culture conditions, including
  individual microwell culture and two group culture conditions at different medium
  volumes, and a conventional dish control (500 embryos). This was followed by a second
  experiment (350 embryos), in which the best of the above tests was isolated and
  compared with a conventional control. There were no differences in the cleavage
  rates among any of the conditions examined, suggesting that the microwell effect
  is not due to early developmental competence and therefore occurs later in development.
  By contrast, blastocyst formation was significantly and consistently higher in embryos
  cultured individually in 50 μL WOW microwells compared to any of the grouped or
  conventional culture conditions (42.0% vs. 13.0 – 29.0%, P < 0.001 in Experiment
  1; 37.9% vs. 28.1%, P = 0.040 in Experiment 2), and the blastocysts formed under
  the WOW microwells were of higher quality based on the number of nuclei (38.97 vs.
  33.21, P = 0.017). These results not only report that the development of porcine
  IVP embryos in microwells is enhanced, but they also show that embryo quality is
  not compromised. In addition, individual embryo traceability was made possible,
  which is crucial for the remaining parts of the dissertation.The second study, building
  on the findings of the first, is a non-contact, label-free optical system using
  Doppler spectroscopy that characterizes the embryo viability signal using a original
  Biodynamic Imaging system. The BDI was able to assess embryo intracellular motion
  without disrupting the developmental trajectory and generate a 128-point spectral
  profile of each embryo in approximately three minutes. Porcine embryos were individually
  measured once per experimental session (13 independent sessions), distributed to
  the WOW microwells, and cultured to a developmental endpoint of expanded/hatched
  blastocyst stage at day 7, for a total of 199 individually tracked porcine embryos
  (104 identified as viable and 95 identified as non-viable). Nine biomarkers were
  extracted from each spectral profile, ranging from the low-frequency plateau, the
  mid-spectrum knee, the high-frequency Nyquist region, their running-mean equivalents,
  dynamic range, backscatter brightness, particle count, and normalized standard deviation.
  Five of these nine biomarkers significantly separated viable from nonviable embryos:
  four spectral-amplitude biomarkers spanning the plateau, knee, and Nyquist regions
  showed uniformly elevated values in viable embryos (Cohen''s d = 0.42–0.48), and
  particle count showed a smaller but still significant elevation (d = 0.31). Significant
  separation was observed at all 128 spectral indices tested, with no evidence of
  a localized change in spectral shape, as there was no significant difference between
  viable and nonviable embryos in dynamic range. The nine-biomarker Linear Discriminant
  Analysis (LDA) classifier yielded an AUC of 0.645 in the training set, and an AUC
  of 0.550 ± 0.091 in five-fold cross-validation. This confirmed that the signal in
  the original BDI system is a viable signal that is indeed meaningful from a biological
  point of view. The discriminative value of this signal was relatively weak, indicating
  the need for a more stable and more physiologically specific measure to further
  refine this classification.The third study partially addresses the need identified
  in the second study by re-engineering the optical configuration of the BDI. The
  configuration of the original BDI’s two-arm interferometer was changed to a common-path
  Fresnel biprism configuration where the reference beam and the object beam both
  share the same optical elements, instead of traveling two different paths. This
  makes the system more stable against long-time exposures to vibration and path-length
  drift. This system recorded backscattered light from 4-cell stage IVP porcine embryos
  and then numerically reconstructed it into holograms and optical coherence images.
  Temporal Fourier transforms of the pixel-wise intensity time series were then used
  to produce Doppler power spectra. It was possible to fit the spectra with a single
  physiologically interpretable parameter, the knee frequency describing a qualitative
  difference between diffusive and actively driven intracellular transport. While
  dead embryos were entirely devoid of any knee, living embryos consistently exhibited
  a knee, clustering around approximately 0.5 Hz, making the difference between the
  two more of a categorical distinction than only a statistical one, and indicating
  complete cessation of active, energy-dependent intracellular transport at death.
  The knee frequency was then monitored continuously over a period of 45 minutes in
  the living embryos, revealing a progressive decrease in the frequency over time,
  thus achieving a snapshot of the loss of metabolic vigor that would never have been
  seen on a single snapshot measurement.Each of these three studies describes a clear
  progression: A validated culture platform that enables the tracking of individual-embryo
  viability, validation of BDI-derived spectral viability biomarkers that are globally
  distributed, and a re-engineered, more stable Fresnel biprism instrument that can
  generate a single physiologically interpretable biomarker of embryo viability, usable
  to classify viability and track its decline continuously over time. When viewed
  in the context of the entire dissertation, this series points to the direction of
  success in achieving a quantitative, physiologically inspired, machine-learning-based
  approach to predicting the viability of early embryos in a porcine model.'

## Resumo

One of the major unsolved challenges in assisted reproductive technology \(ART\) to date is the prediction of embryo viability before transfer\. While conventional morphological grading does not require contact with embryos, it is also evaluator\-dependent, subjective, and intrinsically limited, assessing only shape and symmetry, and not the actual signature of the physiological processes that take place inside an embryo\. Two embryos that are identical under a microscope, and judged to be viable based on morphology, can have a very different underlying metabolic behavior and cytoskeletal activity\. Those underlying characteristics, not only gross appearance, are what consequently determine whether an in vitro\-produced embryo can develop suitably to a blastocyst that is transferable, or whether it finally ceases development and fails\. The present dissertation aims to further address the above\-mentioned lack of a quantitative and objective approach to viability assessment of porcine embryos by developing and validating a biodynamic imaging \(BDI\)\-based approach\. It comprises three studies, which are presented sequentially and are each a step towards the larger problem\. The first was to establish the culture infrastructure to link a single optical measurement to the eventual fate of an embryo; the second was to show with the original BDI instrument that its spectral output really reflects viability; and the third was to re\-engineer the imaging instrument itself to a more stable, physiologically interpretable configuration that is the system&\#x27;s future\.The first study was a foundation, testing that in vitro\-produced \(IVP\) porcine embryos can be cultured in the Well\-of\-the\-Well \(WOW\) microwell culture system\. In contrast to conventional group culture, which uses 10 or more embryos in one droplet of medium, the WOW dishes allow each embryo to be cultured in a separate microwell in a single droplet of medium, yet preserving the autocrine and paracrine benefits of group culture\. This was a prerequisite for a subsequent study to correlate BDI spectral reading with the actual developmental result of an embryo\. This was followed by a second experiment \(350 embryos\), in which the best of the above tests was isolated and compared with a conventional control\. There were no differences in the cleavage rates among any of the conditions examined, suggesting that the microwell effect is not due to early developmental competence and therefore occurs later in development\. By contrast, blastocyst formation was significantly and consistently higher in embryos cultured individually in 50 μL WOW microwells compared to any of the grouped or conventional culture conditions \(42\.0% vs\. 13\.0 – 29\.0%, P &lt; 0\.001 in Experiment 1; 37\.9% vs\. 28\.1%, P = 0\.040 in Experiment 2\), and the blastocysts formed under the WOW microwells were of higher quality based on the number of nuclei \(38\.97 vs\. 33\.21, P = 0\.017\)\. This makes the system more stable against long\-time exposures to vibration and path\-length drift\.

# When absence becomes functional: lysine deserts in protein regulation and evolution

**Autores:** Natalia A\. Szulc

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Lysine deserts–extended, continuous protein regions containing no lysine residues–occur across diverse proteomes, yet their definition, evolutionary origins and biological significance remain incompletely understood\. In this Review, I examine the evidence linking local lysine depletion to protein regulation, with particular emphasis on ubiquitin\- and proteasome\-dependent pathways\. Studies of toxin catalytic subunits and endogenous components of the ubiquitin\-proteasome system show that introducing lysines at selected positions can alter ubiquitylation, protein turnover or function\. These findings support a context\-dependent model in which lysine deserts can limit the availability of ubiquitin acceptor sites within particular structural and enzymatic environments, rather than generally protecting entire proteins from modification or degradation\. I discuss how lysine position, solvent accessibility, E3 ligase geometry and protein conformation determine the consequences of lysine introduction, and how N\-terminal or non\-lysine ubiquitylation as well as ubiquitin\-independent proteolysis can bypass lysine depletion\. I also consider the reported enrichment of lysine deserts in selected Actinobacteria and Mycobacterium phages, highlighting a testable Pup\-proteasome adaptation hypothesis\. Finally, I discuss potential functions beyond ubiquitylation and the methodological challenges of distinguishing selected lysine depletion from compositional bias or chance\.

# Towards Understanding Tinnitus Symptom Dynamics During Treatment

**Autores:** Milena Engelke

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** dissertation

## Resumo

Tinnitus is defined as the conscious perception of a tonal or composite noise for which there is no identifiable corresponding external sound source\. In its severe form, also referred to as tinnitus disorder, it is accompanied by emotional distress, cognitive dysfunction, and autonomic arousal\. While the percept itself affects approximately 14\.4% of adults, severe tinnitus is present in about 2\.3% of adults and is associated with reduced quality of life, fear, constant awareness, annoyance, concentration difficulties, and mental diseases\. Yet, the substantial heterogeneity in tinnitus phenotypes and aetiologies hinders the development of effective treatments\. Promising treatment outcomes often lack replication, and in many clinical trials, only a subset of patients respond\. Current clinical tinnitus research largely relies on aggregated data collected before and after treatment providing little insight into the temporal dynamics of symptom change\. The ubiquitous use of smartphones makes them well suited for regular symptom monitoring during treatment, which might shed light on unseen symptomatic patterns or dynamics during treatment, which could help to explain treatment heterogeneity\. To date, the potential of methods such as Ecological Momentary Assessment \(EMA\) remains underexplored in clinical tinnitus research\. In this thesis, I explore the potential of daily symptom monitoring across different tinnitus treatments to investigate symptom changes and dynamics\. The introduction first provides a clinical overview of tinnitus, followed by an introduction to EMA, its methodological foundations, and its current applications in health research and tinnitus studies\. The first study examines different analytical methods for quantifying treatment effects using EMA data and compares them with established patient\-reported outcome measures\. The second study investigates in greater detail how clinical improvement manifests in EMA\-derived symptoms and whether patterns of symptom fluctuation can predict treatment effects\. The third study addresses heterogeneity in symptom trajectories using latent class analysis\. In the final section the results are discussed across shared conceptual and methodological themes such as symptom dynamics, methodological innovations in EMA analysis, and the balance between idiographic and nomothetic approaches\.

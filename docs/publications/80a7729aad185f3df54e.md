# Speaking Up in Hierarchical Operating Rooms: A Qualitative Descriptive Study of Nurses&\#x27; Safety Communication With Surgeons and Anaesthetists

**Autores:** Yang Guo; Yaqing Quan; Manhong Zhang; Weiwei Zhang

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

BACKGROUND: Hierarchy can inhibit nurses from raising patient\-safety concerns in operating rooms, yet how Chinese peri\-operative nurses navigate this with surgeons versus anaesthetists remains underexplored\. AIM: To explore when and how peri\-operative nurses in Chinese operating rooms voice, withhold, or adapt patient\-safety concerns with surgeons and anaesthetists\. DESIGN: A multi\-site qualitative descriptive study using the Critical Incident Technique, supplemented by an interpretive interprofessional workshop\. METHODS: Twenty peri\-operative nurses from three tertiary hospitals in Taiyuan, China, were recruited through stratified purposive sampling\. Each completed one in\-depth interview, generating 40 critical incidents, and a subsequent workshop informed interpretation\. Template thematic analysis was conducted, using psychological safety and power distance as sensitizing concepts\. RESULTS: Three themes were developed: \(1\) nurses spoke up mainly when risk was concrete and imminent; \(2\) they used cautious linguistic and non\-verbal strategies to preserve professional relationships and face; \(3\) voice depended on prior punitive experiences, perceived openness, and task relationship\. Anaesthetist\-directed concerns were more collaborative, whereas surgeon\-directed concerns required greater deference\. CONCLUSION: Speaking up was situational, relational, and shaped by hierarchical norms and local team climate, with nurses adapting concerns to different professional audiences\. IMPACT: Nurse voice in Chinese operating rooms is target\-specific, shaped by professional jurisdiction and local team climate\. Findings support nurse managers, peri\-operative educators, and interprofessional leaders in developing psychologically safer climates, hierarchy\-sensitive communication training, and role\-specific strategies for raising concerns with surgeons and anaesthetists\.

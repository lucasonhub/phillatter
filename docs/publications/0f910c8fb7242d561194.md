abstract: 'This paper is the twelfth study in the Attractor Portraits series and the
  second portrait of its Second Arc, The Sovereign Dissolved into Procedure. It develops
  the structural prediction established in Portrait No. 11, The Procedural Prince:
  that the transfer of administrative decision-making from human bureaucratic systems
  to automated systems does not merely increase processing speed or efficiency but
  changes the architecture of authority, accountability, and contestability by removing
  the residual capacity of a human operator to perceive the particular person behind
  the administrative file. The paper designates this configuration the Algorithmic
  Court. The Algorithmic Court is the institutional arrangement that emerges when
  automated systems acquire primary authority over determinations governing access
  to housing, healthcare, financial services, employment, education, legal status,
  welfare provision, and other domains of social life. The term is intended structurally
  rather than metaphorically. In these contexts the automated system performs functions
  analogous to adjudication: it receives a representation of a case, applies a model
  or rule structure, and produces an outcome carrying institutional consequences for
  the individual concerned. The central claim is that algorithmic systems increasingly
  function not merely as technical tools but as legitimacy systems. Their outputs
  acquire authority through institutional adoption, numerical presentation, procedural
  integration, and the absence of an identifiable human author whose judgment can
  be directly engaged. Whereas the human assessor can in principle be questioned,
  corrected, persuaded, or held responsible, the algorithmic determination frequently
  appears as an objective result generated by a process whose internal logic is inaccessible
  to the person governed by it. The result is legitimacy without a face. Portrait
  No. 12 develops three principal structural instruments. The first is Algorithmic
  Exclusion: exclusion generated not necessarily through intentional discriminatory
  action but through the reproduction of historical patterns encoded in training data,
  scoring systems, categorical profiles, and predictive models. Where historical systems
  have distributed opportunity or punishment unequally, models trained upon their
  records may reproduce those distributions as predictions about future individuals.
  The affected person may therefore encounter the consequences of historical pattern
  reproduction without any identifiable individual actor having chosen to discriminate
  in the particular case. The second instrument is the Legitimacy Black Box. The paper
  analyses this as a mutually reinforcing structure composed of numerical authority,
  institutional adoption, accountability diffusion, and contestability asymmetry.
  Numerical outputs acquire an appearance of precision; institutional adoption converts
  them into operative decisions; responsibility becomes distributed across designers,
  vendors, administrators, and organisations; and the affected individual lacks equivalent
  access to the model, data, weighting structure, or technical expertise required
  to challenge the determination. The resulting system may therefore be more resistant
  to contestation than an ordinary human judgment even when its operation is demonstrably
  imperfect. The third instrument is Automated Abandonment, an algorithmic intensification
  of the Pending State developed in Portrait No. 11. Automated Abandonment describes
  the condition in which a case becomes trapped within an automated processing sequence
  without an effective human escalation pathway. No caseworker can reinterpret the
  circumstances, no manager can be approached to exercise discretion, and no particular
  fact can interrupt the sequence unless the system has already been designed to recognise
  that fact as relevant input. The individual remains administratively present but
  structurally unreachable. The paper traces a genealogy of algorithmic authority
  beginning with nineteenth-century actuarial science and the substitution of population-level
  statistical prediction for individual assessment. It then follows this architecture
  through credit scoring, criminal justice risk assessment, welfare automation, platform
  governance, automated recruitment, healthcare triage, and asylum and immigration
  processing. Across these different domains, the framework identifies a recurring
  structural operation: individual human circumstances are translated into categorical
  profiles whose predicted relationship to historical populations becomes the basis
  for present institutional action. Credit scoring is treated as an early fully developed
  legitimacy architecture of automated determination. Its importance lies not only
  in prediction but in the combination of apparent numerical objectivity, widespread
  institutional adoption, proprietary modelling, and the practical difficulty experienced
  by individuals seeking to contest the reasoning behind a score. Criminal justice
  risk assessment raises the stakes of this architecture by applying predictive models
  to determinations concerning detention, sentencing, and parole. The paper examines
  State v. Loomis and the COMPAS controversy as a case in which institutional authority
  accepted the use of an algorithmic assessment while the affected individual lacked
  full access to its proprietary methodology. Welfare automation provides a further
  test because automated error in this domain directly affects the material resources
  required for survival. The paper uses the Australian Robodebt system to analyse
  the Pending State and Automated Abandonment at population scale: administrative
  authority generated through automated inference, enforcement, and debt production
  before effective individual human reconsideration became available. The contemporary
  application section then examines four domains: platform content governance, hiring
  and employment screening, healthcare triage and clinical decision support, and asylum
  and immigration processing. These cases are deliberately heterogeneous. Their purpose
  is to test whether Algorithmic Exclusion, the Legitimacy Black Box, and Automated
  Abandonment remain analytically coherent when the institutional objective, affected
  population, type of data, and consequences of the determination change. Platform
  governance demonstrates authority over visibility and participation through automated
  moderation systems. Employment screening demonstrates the reproduction of historical
  organisational preferences when models are trained upon previous recruitment patterns.
  Healthcare applications demonstrate the risks created when underrepresented populations
  are poorly represented in the datasets from which diagnostic or risk models learn.
  Asylum and immigration processing demonstrate the implications of algorithmic opacity
  where determinations concern legal status, physical safety, and access to protection.
  The paper also extends the Surplus Mind analysis developed in the first arc and
  in Portrait No. 11. Individuals whose circumstances span multiple administrative
  categories, present inconsistently over time, generate non-standard evidence, or
  require contextual rather than categorical interpretation are structurally vulnerable
  to systems optimised for uniform machine-processable inputs. The algorithmic system
  does not become hostile to complexity in an intentional sense. It becomes unable
  to receive forms of complexity for which no feature, category, or escalation path
  has been provided. The analysis nevertheless rejects a totalising account of algorithmic
  authority. Three internal fracture mechanisms are identified. The first is the audit
  gap: the measurable difference between a system’s claimed accuracy and its observed
  population-level performance. Independent auditing, investigative analysis, litigation,
  and empirical comparison can make systematic disparities visible even where individual
  model logic remains opaque. The second is regulatory and legislative intervention,
  through which legal systems attempt to impose transparency, accountability, risk-management,
  or review requirements upon automated decision systems. The third is human override
  persistence: the survival of residual human review capacities within partially automated
  institutions. These mechanisms constitute the corrective arc of the Algorithmic
  Court. They do not eliminate the concentration at its centre, but they demonstrate
  that algorithmic systems are not structurally sealed. Human review, audit evidence,
  public investigation, regulatory intervention, and legal challenge can reopen portions
  of the decision architecture that automation had closed. Portrait No. 12 therefore
  marks the second stage in Arc Two’s movement from sovereign person to institutional
  architecture. The Procedural Prince described authority dissolved into bureaucracy.
  The Algorithmic Court describes bureaucracy dissolved into code. Its structural
  novelty lies not simply in automation but in the combination of categorical prediction,
  authorless determination, legitimacy diffusion, and reduced avenues through which
  individual particularity can interrupt institutional processing. The paper concludes
  that the Algorithmic Court is best understood as a structural attractor type rather
  than a domain-specific technological problem. Its mechanisms recur across materially
  different institutional environments because the underlying operation remains constant:
  human complexity is translated into an institutionally processable representation,
  predictive authority is applied to that representation, and the representation rather
  than the irreducible person becomes the object upon which institutional power acts.
  Portrait No. 12 therefore extends the Equilibrium Ledger’s analysis of concentrated
  authority from the administrative file to the automated model and establishes the
  bridge to Portrait No. 13, where the Second Arc moves laterally from the processing
  of populations through data profiles to the processing of persons through concentrated
  symbolic identity. (direct link )'

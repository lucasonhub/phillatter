# TRAINING\-SPECIFIC FORCE–TIME ADAPTATIONS FOLLOWING PLYOMETRIC AND CONTRAST TRAINING IN VOLLEYBALL PLAYERS

**Autores:** Thanakorn Kaimusik; Penpak Noopud; MONTON THONGROJ

**Data:** 2026-10-13 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

This study aimed to investigate and compare the effects of plyometric training \(PLY\) and contrast training \(CT\), combined with routine volleyball practice, against a control group \(CON\) in 45 male collegiate volleyball players\. To minimize baseline variability, participants \(N = 45\) were ranked according to their 0–5 m sprint times, organized into matched triplets, and randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to the Contrast Training \(CT\), Plyometric Training \(PLY\), or Control \(CON\) group \(n = 15 per group\)\. Countermovement jump \(CMJ\) biomechanical variables, including peak power, relative peak power, rate of force development \(RFD\), impulse, and jump height, were assessed using a force platform and analyzed using multivariate and repeated\-measures ANOVA\. The results showed significant improvements in CMJ force–time characteristics in both experimental groups compared with CON \(p &lt; \.001\), with no significant between\-group differences at baseline before the intervention \(p &gt; \.05\)\. Further pairwise comparisons revealed distinct mechanical adaptation profiles between the training modalities\. CT demonstrated significantly better outcomes than CON and outperformed PLY in selected late\-phase variables, such as Peak RFD90, Relative Peak RFD90, and Gross Impulse300 \(p &lt; \.05\), alongside greater relative peak power and jump height\. In contrast, during the initial phase of force development \(0–30 ms\), both PLY and CT significantly improved early\-phase rapid force development, as reflected in Peak RFD30 and Relative Peak RFD30, compared with CON \(p &lt; \.05\), with no statistically significant differences between the two experimental groups \(p &gt; \.05\)\. In conclusion, both PLY and CT effectively improved jumping performance compared with routine volleyball practice alone, through distinct mechanical adaptation profiles across temporal phases\. CT produced greater improvements in late\-phase RFD and jump height, whereas both methods significantly improved early\-phase rapid force development, with no significant differences between them\. These findings highlight the practical application of force–time profiling to individualized training prescription

# “During good Behaviour”: An Investigation of Failed Legal Frameworks for Judicial Impeachment and Democracy

**Autores:** Sky Crawford

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

This paper investigates the persistence of inadequate legal frameworks for judicial impeachment and their broader implications for constitutional accountability and democratic development\. Throughout the twentieth century, many emerging states adopted constitutional models from more powerful Western nations, often without adapting them to local institutional needs\. While these influential states occasionally revised their own judicial removal mechanisms, the exported constitutional templates remained largely static, reproducing weak impeachment structures and embedding systemic gaps in checks and balances\. The American Constitution exemplifies this trend: it imposes limited formal accountability on the judiciary, requiring only that judges maintain “good Behavior,” yet it provides few practical mechanisms to enforce this standard\. Many constitutions modeled on this approach likewise lack effective procedures for removing corrupt or incompetent judicial officers\. This paper contends that the global diffusion of these underdeveloped frameworks has contributed to declining judicial accountability, normalized corruption, and weakened democratic oversight in both powerful and developing states\. To illustrate this dynamic, the paper first outlines the challenges faced by major powers such as the United States and China in holding members of the judiciary accountable while preserving judicial independence\. It then traces similar difficulties in younger and developing states, where judicial impeachment is frequently neglected or structurally unworkable\. In India and numerous Latin American jurisdictions, for example, corruption within the judiciary is widely acknowledged, yet impeachment remains exceedingly rare and often forgotten as a tool of constitutional enforcement\. To assess these patterns, the paper constructs a comparative dataset of judicial impeachment practices across states, incorporating information on constitutional origins, removal procedures, impeachment frequency and outcomes, and corresponding corruption levels\. Through this analysis, the paper evaluates how impeachment practices interact with broader constitutional factors and how these dynamics shape democratic integrity\. This paper concludes that contemporary systems of checks and balances routinely fail to ensure judicial accountability, largely because impeachment frameworks remain incomplete or poorly designed\. It argues that developing more robust, context\-sensitive mechanisms for judicial removal is essential for strengthening democratic governance in both established and emerging constitutional orders\.

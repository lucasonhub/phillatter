abstract: 'Streptococcus pneumoniae remains a leading cause of pneumonia, meningitis,
  and sepsis worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on global public health (Cliff
  et al., 2024, Mahtab et al., 2024). The pathogen''s remarkable genetic plasticity,
  encompassing over 100 serotypes and more than 900 lineages, has enabled it to adapt
  rapidly to clinical interventions, including antimicrobial therapy and vaccination
  (Ganaie et al., 2025, Hanage et al., 2009). While pneumococcal conjugate vaccines
  (PCVs) have dramatically reduced vaccine-type disease in many high-income countries,
  serotype replacement and the expansion of resistant non-vaccine types have emerged
  as new challenges (Garcia Quesada et al., 2025, Bennett et al., 2025). Simultaneously,
  antimicrobial resistance (AMR) continues to escalate, particularly for macrolides
  and β-lactams, threatening the effectiveness of empirical treatment regimens (Zhou
  et al., 2022, Bruinsma et al., 2004).Understanding the spatial spread and fitness
  dynamics of pneumococcal lineages is critical for designing effective surveillance
  and vaccination strategies. However, the complex interplay between human mobility,
  vaccine selection pressure, and antibiotic consumption remains poorly quantified,
  particularly in diverse epidemiological settings.Most phylogeographic studies have
  focused on high-income countries with established vaccination programs, leaving
  substantial gaps in our understanding of how these dynamics operate in regions with
  different selective pressures (Lo et al., 2019, Chaguza et al., 2016).In this context,
  Belman and colleagues recently published a landmark study in Nature that integrates
  genomic data from 6,910 South African isolates with Meta human mobility data to
  reconstruct pneumococcal geographical migration and vaccine-driven fitness dynamics
  (Belman et al., 2024). Their study makes three pivotal contributions.First, it quantitatively
  demonstrates that pneumococci require approximately 50 years of transmission to
  become homogeneously mixed across South Africa-a finding that challenges intuitive
  assumptions about bacterial spread and reveals the deeply "entrenched" nature of
  endemic transmission. Second, by coupling Meta mobility data with phylogeographic
  models, the authors establish a quantitative link between individual-level behaviour
  and pathogen macro-dispersal, showing that the "focal" nature of human movement
  drives slow spread. Third, they provide robust fitness estimates after PCV introduction:
  non-vaccine types (NVT) showed a relative fitness advantage of 1.68 (95% CI: 1.59-1.77)
  over vaccine types (VT), and penicillin resistance among NVT strains is gaining
  fitness, suggesting that vaccine-associated reductions in AMR may be transient (Belman
  et al., 2024).While we greatly appreciate this high-quality work, we do not question
  the validity of their findings within the South African context. Rather, we argue
  that the extent to which their quantitative conclusions can inform policy in settings
  with fundamentally different selective pressures-such as China, where PCV coverage
  is <5% but macrolide resistance exceeds 90%-remains an open empirical question.
  Our Chinese data do not invalidate Belman et al.''s findings but quantify how and
  to what extent pneumococcal fitness dynamics differ across contrasting epidemiological
  landscapes, thereby operationalizing the very context-dependence they themselves
  acknowledged (Belman et al., 2024, Limitations section).In this opinion article,
  drawing on our 14-year nationwide surveillance in China (2007-2020, n=1,312 isolates
  from 24 hospitals across 18 provinces) (Cui et al., 2026) and our phylogenomic analysis
  of the MDR clone CC271 (Zeng et al., 2023), we address a single overarching question:
  To what extent can phylogeographic and fitness estimates derived from one epidemiological
  setting inform surveillance and vaccine policy in another? We do not attempt to
  provide a definitive answer-such would require analyses across many more settings-but
  rather use the contrast between South Africa and China as an illustrative case study.
  Belman et al.''s study provides the reference point from a high-vaccine-coverage
  setting; our 14-year Chinese surveillance data provide the counterpoint from a low-vaccine-coverage,
  high-antibioticconsumption setting.The South African dataset, while comprehensive,
  reflects the epidemiological landscape of an upper-middle-income African country
  with high PCV coverage (89% by 2022). The serotype distribution, clonal composition,
  and AMR profiles observed in South Africa differ substantially from those in other
  regions (Belman et al., 2024). Our 14-year surveillance in China demonstrates that
  serotypes 19F and 19A dominate, ST271 and ST320 are the predominant clones, and
  >90% of isolates are macrolide-resistant-features that are not mirrored in the South
  African data (Cui et al., 2026). These differences are not merely descriptive but
  have profound implications for phylogeographic modelling. The "50-year" mixing time
  estimated for South Africa reflects the focal nature of human mobility in that specific
  geographic and demographic context. Countries with vastly different population densities,
  urban-rural distributions, transportation infrastructures, and mobility patterns
  may exhibit substantially different rates of pathogen dispersal. For instance, China''s
  dense urban centres, extensive highspeed rail network, and high population mobility
  may facilitate more rapid interregional transmission than observed in South Africa.
  Whether the quantitative conclusions of Belman et al. can be extrapolated to countries
  with such differing characteristics remains an open question that requires validation
  through similar analyses in diverse settings.PCV introduction, which is plausible
  given the country''s 89% coverage (Belman et al., 2024). However, in many parts
  of the world-especially low-and middle-income countries-pneumococcal vaccine uptake
  remains low (Clark et al., 2026). Our Chinese data show that while PCV13 serotypes
  account for 85.8% of paediatric isolates, actual vaccination coverage is only about
  4.1% (Cui et al., 2026). This unique epidemiological context allowed us to investigate
  MDR clone dynamics largely unconfounded by vaccine pressure.Our phylogenomic analysis
  of the MDR clone CC271 revealed a distinct evolutionary trajectory (Zeng et al.,
  2023). The dominant clone, 19F ST271-B, expanded rapidly during 1995-2000, coinciding
  with the widespread use of cephalosporins in China during the 1990s. This clone
  possesses unique PBP2x substitutions (M339F, M400T, Y595F) conferring significantly
  higher resistance to cephalosporins than other CC271 clones (Zeng et al., 2023).
  Thus, in settings without strong vaccine pressure, antibiotic selection appears
  to be the dominant driver of MDR clone expansion, although the potential contribution
  of vaccination-particularly given that PCV7 includes serotype 19F, the capsule type
  of ST271-cannot be entirely excluded. This contrast underscores that the relative
  importance of vaccine versus antibiotic pressure is context-dependent and cannot
  be generalized from a single country.The implications for public health policy are
  substantial. In regions with high antibiotic consumption but low vaccine coverage,
  antimicrobial stewardship may be as important as vaccination in controlling MDR
  clone expansion. Models that attribute fitness changes solely to vaccine pressure
  may underestimate the role of antibiotic selection and misguide intervention strategies.
  (Zeng et al., 2023). Phylogenetic analysis showed multiple subclades shared between
  China and other countries, suggesting possible international transmission (Zeng
  et al., 2023). Local antibiotic selection cannot be excluded as a driver of ST320
  expansion in China, and that the vaccine-escape label is context-dependent. This
  phenomenon raises the possibility that mass vaccination in one country can indirectly
  affect pathogen populations in unvaccinated countries through global transmission
  networks. The post-PCV7 emergence of 19A in the USA may have contributed to global
  dissemination that subsequently expanded in countries without local vaccine pressure.
  This finding has profound implications for global vaccine policy: the benefits of
  vaccination extend beyond national borders, but conversely, vaccinedriven clonal
  shifts in one region can introduce new threats to others. This global dimension
  is absent from the South African analysis, which primarily considered within-country
  transmission. Future phylogeographic models must incorporate international transmission
  networks to fully capture the dynamics of vaccine-escape clones and inform coordinated
  global vaccination strategies.Although the South African dataset is large (n=6,910),
  it has an age-related bias: 50% from children <3 years old, only 17.5% from adults
  (Belman et al., 2024). Carriage rates, disease spectra, antibiotic exposure, and
  mobility behaviours differ markedly across age groups. Children are major reservoirs
  of pneumococcal carriage and transmission, but adults and the elderly bear the highest
  burden of invasive disease and mortality (Wahl et al., 2018, Bahrs et al., 2025).Our
  Chinese study was specifically designed to stratify by age: children <14 years,
  adults 15-64 years, and elderly ≥65 years (Cui et al., 2026). We found that β-lactam
  resistance in paediatric isolates was significantly higher than in adults and the
  elderlyan age-related difference that may be underestimated in the South African
  study. Higher resistance in children may be driven by greater antibiotic exposure
  (especially β-lactams and macrolides for respiratory infections), close contact
  in daycare settings, and clonal expansion of paediatric-adapted lineages like 19F
  ST271-B (Cui et al., 2026).The age distribution of samples influences phylogeographic
  inferences because transmission networks and mobility patterns differ across age
  groups. Age-stratified analyses are essential for understanding the full dynamics
  of pneumococcal spread and fitness evolution. Future studies should ensure balanced
  age representation or incorporate age as a covariate in phylogeographic models.Belman
  et al. suggest that vaccine-associated declines in penicillin resistance may be
  temporary, as NVT-resistant strains are gaining fitness (Belman et al., 2024). In
  our Chinese context, we observed a different picture: MDR clones such as ST271-19F
  and ST320-19A remained dominant over 14 years with no statistically significant
  trend changes (Cui et al., 2026). This suggests that, in China, antibiotic pressure
  sustains MDR clone predominance independently of vaccine effects. The contrast underscores
  that the interplay between AMR and vaccination is highly dependent on local antibiotic
  usage, vaccine coverage, and clonal transmission networks.Additionally, the Meta
  mobility data used by Belman et al. were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic
  (2020-2021), a period of unprecedented disruption to human movement (Belman et al.,
  2024). Using these data to infer transmission patterns from 2000 to 2014 introduces
  temporal mismatch. Although the authors partially adjusted for this with a parameter,
  residual confounding cannot be fully eliminated. Human mobility patterns changed
  substantially during the pandemic, with reduced longdistance travel and increased
  local movement. Whether these changes proportionally reflect pre-pandemic mobility
  patterns is uncertain.Future studies should ideally use mobility data contemporaneous
  with the genomic samples or validate findings with multiple independent mobility
  datasets to ensure robustness.The study by Belman et al. is methodologically groundbreaking
  and offers invaluable insights into pneumococcal spread and evolution in a high-vaccine-coverage
  setting (Belman et al., 2024). Their integration of genomic data with human mobility
  represents a significant advance in phylogeographic methodology, demonstrating how
  directly observed mobility data can be used to infer mechanistic transmission dynamics.Our
  data from China-a setting with low vaccine coverage but high antibiotic consumption-suggest
  that antibiotic selection pressure can be a major driver of MDR clone expansion,
  and that global transmission of vaccine-escape clones can occur across settings
  with differing local vaccination pressure (Zeng et al., 2023, Cui et al., 2026).
  Also, the distinct epidemiology of pneumococcal infections in Chinese children underscores
  its context-dependent nature. A Beijing surveillance study showed S. pneumoniae
  ranked third among pediatric isolates, with 77.1% from children <5 years and macrolide
  resistance >95% (Lyu et al., 2023). A pre-PCV13 IPD study identified serotypes 19F,
  19A, 14, 23F, and 6B as predominant, with PCV13 coverage of 90.1%, yet 89.2% MDR,
  and MLST revealed antibiotic-driven clonal replacement without widespread vaccine
  pressure (Shi et al., 2019). These findings confirm that local antibiotic use and
  vaccination coverage profoundly shape pneumococcal epidemiology-factors distinct
  from South Africa (Belman et al., 2024). The high PCV13 coverage but extremely low
  uptake in China represents a missed prevention opportunity, emphasizing the need
  for context-specific surveillance and vaccine policies.The heterogeneity of pneumococcal
  vaccination policies across countries adds another layer of context-dependence to
  pneumococcal epidemiology. While PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 have been widely introduced
  in many high-income countries with routine childhood immunization programs, the
  timing of introduction, coverage rates, and use of PPV23 for older adults vary substantially
  (Bennett et al., 2025, Garcia Quesada et al., 2025). These policy differences directly
  shape serotype dynamics: in countries with high PCV coverage, vaccine-type serotypes
  have declined sharply, but non-vaccine serotypes-such as serotype 8 in England and
  Spain, and 19A in the United States post-PCV7-have expanded through serotype replacement
  (Lo et al., 2019, D''Aeth et al., 2025). In contrast, in countries like China where
  PCV uptake remains <5%, vaccine-type serotypes (19F, 19A) continue to dominate without
  evidence of vaccine-driven replacement (Shi et al., 2019, Cui et al., 2026). This
  contrast underscores that serotype dynamics are not intrinsic to the pathogen but
  are profoundly shaped by local vaccination policies and coverage levels.The strong
  and stable linkages between specific STs, serotypes, and GPSCs observed in our study
  suggest that CC271/GPSC1 has rarely undergone capsule switching in China over the
  past 14 years, as evidenced by the consistent 19F-ST271 and 19A-ST320 associations
  (Zeng et al., 2023, Cui et al., 2026). This stability implies that, if PCV13 coverage
  were increased, it would likely reduce the burden of these dominant MDR clones without
  immediate risk of vaccine escape through serotype replacement within the same genetic
  background. However, this conclusion warrants caution. Capsule switching, although
  a historically low-frequency event in this lineage, remains a theoretical possibility
  under sustained, long-term vaccine selection pressure (Croucher et al., 2014, Wyres
  et al., 2013). Continuous genomic surveillance will be essential to detect any emergence
  of capsule-switch variants should PCV13 uptake increase substantially in China.The
  contrasting evidence from South Africa and China does not invalidate either dataset.
  Instead, it demonstrates that the drivers, pace, and trajectories of pneumococcal
  fitness dynamics are fundamentally context-dependent-a conclusion that Belman et
  al. themselves endorsed qualitatively, and that our Chinese data now provide complementary
  descriptive support. Future phylogeographic models must therefore incorporate local
  measures of antibiotic consumption, vaccination coverage, and age structure, and
  must be calibrated to the specific epidemiological context in which they are applied.We
  acknowledge an evidentiary asymmetry between this article and Belman et al.provided
  quantitative fitness estimates with confidence intervals derived from formal phylogeographic
  modelling. By contrast, the Chinese evidence presented here is primarily descriptive,
  relying on dominant clones, resistance percentages, and the absence of a statistically
  significant temporal trend. These data are therefore not directly equivalent to
  formal fitness estimates. Rather, they provide complementary contextual evidence
  and should motivate future quantitative phylogeographic and fitness modelling using
  Chinese genomic, mobility, and antibiotic consumption data.'

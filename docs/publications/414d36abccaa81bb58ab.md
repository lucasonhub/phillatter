# МЕТОДИЧЕСКИЕ ВОЗМОЖНОСТИ МЕТОДА ПРОДУКТИВНОГО ЗАТРУДНЕНИЯ В ОБУЧЕНИИ ПЕРИОДИЧЕСКИМ ДЕСЯТИЧНЫМ ДРОБЯМ

**Autores:** Маржан Қайсарқызы Ожаева

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Repeating decimals are not especially difficult to calculate once the rule has been given\. The harder question is whether students understand why the rule works\. A student may readily convert 0\.1\(6\) into a fraction, yet hesitate when asked to explain why the answer is \. The same problem appears with 0\.2\(7\): a correct procedure can be reproduced even when the structure of the repeating decimal remains unclear\. This creates a useful point for classroom work\. Instead of introducing the conversion rule first, students can be given a problem and asked to find a way to solve it\. They may try an incomplete strategy, make an error, revise the calculation, or compare their approach with a classmate&\#x27;s solution\. The teacher&\#x27;s role in this situation is not simply to replace an incorrect answer with a correct one, but to use the emerging difficulties as material for mathematical discussion\. The approach described in the article draws on Manu Kapur&\#x27;s work on productive failure, Hiroko Warshauer&\#x27;s research on productive struggle, and studies of students&\#x27; mathematical errors \[1–3\]\. These ideas are adapted to the topic of repeating decimals through four classroom stages: problem task, individual exploration, comparison and discussion of strategies, and formulation of the mathematical rule\. The examples 0\.1\(6\)and 0\.2\(7\) illustrate what this process can look like\. Before seeing a formal algorithm, students may represent the numbers in different ways, question their own calculations, and check whether a proposed fraction produces the original decimal\. Such moments are important because the error is no longer treated simply as something to remove\. It becomes a reason to return to the structure of the number and examine the relationship between the repeating part and the resulting fraction\. The article does not report classroom experiment results that have not actually been collected\. Instead, it outlines a possible design for later implementation, including observation criteria, student\-work indicators, and a simple framework for recording the data\. This distinction matters: a teaching model can be proposed before its effectiveness is established\.Whether the approach works equally well across Kazakhstani schools remains an open question\. Students&\#x27; prior knowledge, their experience with non\-routine tasks, lesson time, and the teacher&\#x27;s questioning style may all influence the outcome\. For that reason, the proposed model is better treated as a starting point for classroom testing than as a finished teaching formula\.

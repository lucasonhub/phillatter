# AI DEMOCRATIZATION OR AI CONCENTRATION? RETHINKING COMPETITIVENESS, TRADE INEQUALITY, AND GLOBAL MARKET INTEGRATION IN THE AGE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE\.

**Autores:** Dr\. Shriram K

**Data:** 2026-10-13 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Artificial Intelligence \(AI\) is increasingly transforming the global economy by reshaping productivity, innovation, international competitiveness, trade, global value chains, employment, and the distribution of economic power\. While cloud\-based applications, open\-source technologies, and commercially available AI tools are broadening access to AI\-enabled capabilities, the foundational resources required to develop, control, and capture value from frontier AI—including advanced computing capacity, specialised talent, research capabilities, investment, intellectual property, data, and digital infrastructure—remain substantially concentrated\. Against this background, the study examines the emerging AI democratization–concentration paradox and evaluates its implications for global competitiveness, international trade, global value chains, international economic inequality, and global market integration\. The study adopts a descriptive, analytical, and comparative research design based primarily on secondary data from internationally recognised sources, including the Stanford AI Index, UN Trade and Development, World Trade Organization, International Monetary Fund, World Bank, OECD, and other relevant international datasets and institutional reports\. Evidence from the contemporary period, subject to data availability, is examined through descriptive statistics, trend analysis, cross\-country comparison, and comparative interpretation, with particular attention to advanced, emerging, and developing economies\. The analytical framework distinguishes three interrelated dimensions of AI participation: AI access, referring to the ability to use AI applications and services; AI capability, referring to the infrastructure, computing capacity, skills, data, research, investment, and innovation resources required to develop and effectively utilise AI; and AI control, referring to ownership or influence over foundational technologies, intellectual property, platforms, computing infrastructure, and other critical sources of AI\-related economic value\. The findings indicate that AI is becoming an increasingly important determinant of global competitiveness, with technological infrastructure, skilled human capital, research capacity, investment, and innovation ecosystems increasingly complementing conventional sources of competitive advantage\. The study further finds that although AI access is expanding, genuine democratization remains uneven because the complementary capabilities required for productive and sustainable AI participation are unequally distributed\. At the same time, frontier AI development, advanced computing, investment, specialised talent, intellectual property, and major technological platforms remain substantially concentrated, creating new forms of technological dependency and potential market power\. AI is also found to have significant implications for international trade and global value chains by reducing transaction and coordination costs, improving productivity, facilitating digitally delivered services, and creating opportunities for technological upgrading, while simultaneously altering traditional sources of comparative advantage\. The distribution of these benefits remains uneven, raising concerns regarding international economic inequality and technological divergence\. The study further identifies a dual effect on global market integration: AI can deepen economic interdependence through trade, investment, digital services, and global value chains, while the concentration of critical AI capabilities can generate technological dependency and fragmentation\. Overall, the study concludes that AI is neither purely democratizing nor purely concentrating; rather, it is producing a “diffusion of use with concentration of capability\.” The future inclusiveness of the global AI economy will therefore depend not merely on expanding access to AI applications, but on strengthening infrastructure, computing capacity, skills, research, innovation, domestic technological capabilities, value creation, and greater control over the resources underlying AI

# Satellite LiDAR reveals patterns in water table depth and flood risk in SE Asia coastal lowland and peatland plantations

**Autores:** R\. Vernimmen; A\. Hooijer

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Agriculture in SE Asian coastal lowlands is vulnerable to inundation and associated crop production loss\. Quantifying and managing flood risk, surface subsidence, and carbon emissions \(in peatlands\) requires an understanding of water table depth patterns and controls, for which no comprehensive data currently exists\. Here, we present a dataset of 15,575 measurements of canal water table depth below the soil surface \(CWD\) in large\-scale industrial tree plantations across the islands of Sumatra and Borneo, derived from ICESat\-2 satellite LiDAR data\. A pronounced relation is evident between CWD and land surface elevation, which applies equally in different regions, both in peatlands and mineral soil areas, and in oil palm as well as Acacia plantations\. Mean CWD varies consistently across elevation zones, from −0\.6 to −0\.85 m for oil palm and Acacia plantations, respectively \(i\.e\., ∼\-0\.5 – ∼\-0\.75 m in peat soil\) where the surface is 5–9 m above global mean sea level, to ∼ \-0\.2 m at 1–3 m elevation near mean sea level\. The area of industrial plantations in the 1–3 m elevation range alone, which likely has the highest flood risk, is 984,700 ha\. Accounting for larger areas of smallholder plantations, and for areas at higher elevations with impeded drainage, the total area of lowland plantations likely experiencing regular flooding could exceed 2\.8 million hectares\. The close relation with elevation indicates that plantation water levels, especially in lowest\-lying areas, are often controlled by landscape drainability conditions rather than management targets, and that optimum water table depths for production and peat loss prevention are not achieved over large areas\. We propose that such difficult\-to\-manage landscape elevation controls on water table depth are considered in yield projections and land use planning, as well as fire and peat carbon emission reduction policies\. Unproductive flood\-prone plantations as well as peatlands with very low water tables may be logical priority targets for restoration planning\.

# A novel formwork technology based on wax foam 3D printing and CNC milling

**Autores:** Sven Jonischkies; Jeldrik Mainka; Harald Kloft; Ralf Diesslin; Norman Hack

**Data:** 2026-10-09 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Individualised, geometrically complex precast concrete elements require freeform formwork that is currently labour\-intensive and difficult to recycle\. This study demonstrates the feasibility of a novel wax formwork technology based on 3D printed wax foam for formwork for individualized concrete elements\. It combines extrusion\-based 3D printing with computerized numerical control \(CNC\) milling to produce recyclable formwork within a largely digital manufacturing process chain\. Rather than relying exclusively on additive or subtractive manufacturing, the approach integrates both to exploit their respective advantages: by additive manufacturing near\-net\-shape wax preforms are produced with reduced material consumption, while CNC milling serves as the finishing step intended to provide the dimensional accuracy and surface quality required for concrete casting\. The formwork material is a porous wax‐air composite \(wax foam\) developed specifically for extrusion\-based deposition\. Because wax can be remelted, mechanically re\-foamed, and reprocessed, it supports a closed\-loop material cycle and resource\-efficient formwork production\. Within an industry‐academia partnership, a custom Wax Foam 3D Printing \(WF3DP\) robotic unit was developed to investigate the fundamentals of wax foam extrusion and to establish process parameters for high\-throughput printing\. A new wax formulation, Tudastar wax, was characterized with respect to foamability, compressive strength, porosity, and surface energy, and compared with the ConFormWax \(CFW\) formulation used in previous investigations\. The process chain was validated through the full\-scale fabrication of a geometrically complex formwork used to cast a staircase element from self\-compacting concrete\. Stable deposition was achieved at rates of up to 28\.5 kg/h, and the characterisation tests provide first indicators of suitability for formwork applications and of release behaviour during concrete casting\. The full\-scale case study confirmed the feasibility of the process chain, including remelting and reprocessing of the used formwork into a new printed element\. Compared with a solid\-cast wax formwork produced entirely by subtractive machining, the approach reduced wax mass consumption by approximately 80%\. The results establish the technical feasibility of the additive–subtractive process chain and of a process\-level material cycle, while identifying interlayer bonding, warpage control, geometric accuracy, and demoulding durability as the principal requirements for progressing towards industrial application\.

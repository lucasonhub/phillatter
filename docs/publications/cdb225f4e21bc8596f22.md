# Iwato, Atsushi: Jinxes and Superstitions: The Human Mind in Search of Lost Authority

**Autores:**

## Descrição do feed

Why do we obey superstitions and jinxes even when we know they have no scientific basis? Consider someone who avoids the number 13, an athlete who refuses ever again to wear the shoes worn during a defeat, or a person afraid that speaking of good fortune will make it disappear\. These familiar behaviors reveal something curious about the human mind that cannot simply be dismissed as coincidence or mistaken belief\. Julian Jaynes argued that after the breakdown of the bicameral mind, when the voices of the gods fell silent, human beings sought the authority they had lost through oracles, divination, prophecy, and religious ritual\. He also examined the practice of reading omens of the future in chance events\. Yet the personal jinxes that people create in everyday life, the tendency to obey them even while recognizing their lack of rational foundation, and the act of deliberately defying them do not appear to have been developed as independent themes in his work\. This essay focuses on these ordinary superstitions and jinxes and offers a possible interpretation through Jaynes&\#x27;s account of the search for lost authority\. In 1970, Apollo 13 departed for space bearing a number commonly regarded as unlucky\. Though a grave accident occurred during the mission, the astronauts returned alive through science, ingenuity, and human judgment\. On a much smaller scale, I once made an experiment of deliberately wearing clothes I had associated with an unhappy event\. Different as these experiences are in magnitude, both raise a question about how human beings confront their own fears and assumptions\. The essay also asks where the standards of “rationality,” which we so readily trust, originate\. Does freedom from superstition simply mean rejecting it? Or does it mean questioning the invisible authorities that govern our choices and sometimes consciously saying no? Even after the gods fell silent, do human beings continue to discover new forms of authority in rules of their own making? And how might Jaynes&\#x27;s introspective consciousness relate to our ability to question those authorities and sometimes resist them? Beginning with the small jinxes of ordinary life, this essay invites readers to reflect on consciousness, rationality, and the ultimate sources of authority over human action\.

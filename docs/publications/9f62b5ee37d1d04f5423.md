# Inhibitory control supports children&\#x27;s resistance to misleading testimony when they possess first\-hand knowledge

**Autores:** David Buttelmann; Michel Chappuis; Yanick Schmid; Dominik Zeilstra; Sandro Jenni

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Introduction Young children show a bias to believe: They repeatedly follow an informant&\#x27;s cue about a reward&\#x27;s location, even if this testimony is misleading\. This study investigated how children&\#x27;s age, informant age, children&\#x27;s knowledge about the reward location, and inhibitory control affected their ability to resist this bias\. Method In a Misleading testimony task \(MTT\), children aged 2–4 years \( n = 84\) had to find a reward that was hidden in one of two locations\. Either an adult or a child informant provided misleading testimony before children were allowed to search\. In half of the trials, children knew the reward&\#x27;s location; in the other half, they did not\. Subsequently, children participated in an inhibitory\-control task\. Results In the MTT, resistance to misleading testimony increased with age, and children were more likely to resist misleading testimony when they possessed first\-hand knowledge about the reward&\#x27;s location\. Informant age had no significant effect\. Children&\#x27;s inhibitory control was positively associated with performance in both knowledge conditions\. After controlling for age, the association remained marginally significant in the knowledgeable condition but was not significant in the ignorant condition\. Discussion These findings suggest that children become better at resisting misleading testimony as they grow older and when they possess first\-hand knowledge about the situation\. The observed pattern is consistent with the possibility that inhibitory control may be particularly relevant when children must resist misleading testimony that conflicts with their own knowledge\.

abstract: 'Heart failure (HF) exhibits distinctive event-and time-dependent patterns
  in incidence and progression dynamics. Over time, the heart first undergoes (mal)adaptive
  cellular, tissue and molecular remodelling 1,2 . These alterations precede clinical
  symptoms and diagnosis 3,4 and reflect age-related changes in cardiovascular structure
  and function that lower the threshold for overt disease 4,5 . This subclinical process
  is followed by incremental cardiac remodelling, including cardiac hypertrophy 6,7
  , myocardial stiffening 8 , and impaired diastolic filling 4 . Similar preclinical
  alterations occur across other cardiovascular and cardiometabolic disorders: atrial
  fibrosis and mitochondrial remodelling may develop before atrial fibrillation 9,10
  , endothelial dysfunction precedes hypertension and atherosclerosis 11 and even
  rare diseases such as J-wave syndromes are now considered cardiomyopathies with
  subtle structural abnormalities that remain undetected by standard imaging techniques
  12 . In addition, disturbances in protein homeostasis and metabolic adaptation occur
  long before the onset of clinical manifestations of the disease 13 . Thus, sudden
  cardiac pathological events are often preceded by manifestations of biological processes
  that have been unfolding over several years. The temporal trajectories differ markedly
  across various pathologies 12,14 , are not linear 15 , and are modulated by age,
  sex, lifestyle and inter-organ interactions. This interindividual variability calls
  for a personalised temporal framework for diagnosis and therapy, rather than a single,
  universally applied diagnostic threshold. Disease is typically defined once a threshold
  value is exceeded 4 . In HF, this heterogeneity is further reflected in the attempts
  to subcategorize distinct phenotypes, notably HF with reduced ejection fraction
  (HFrEF), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) and HF with preserved
  ejection fraction (HFpEF). This differentiation likely represents overlapping but
  temporally and mechanistically divergent trajectories rather than strict separate
  entities 16 . Similarly, this limitation is evident in the study and management
  of AF, where the conventional classification into paroxysmal and persistent forms,
  subsequently expanded to include long-standing persistent and permanent AF, fails
  to adequately capture individual risk profiles and the progressive nature of the
  underlying atrial myopathy 17 . Emerging approaches based on biomarkers 18 and wearable
  or implantable devices 19,20 will increasingly enable realtime monitoring of individual
  disease trajectories for earlier detection of warning signals before irreversible
  remodelling occurs. A central challenge is to identify the therapeutically actionable
  window during which pathological remodelling remains reversible in order to limit
  disease progression. This limitation highlights the constraints of traditional frameworks
  based on universally applied symptomatology, isolated criteria and therefore simple
  causal relationships that inform therapeutic options 21 . Given that cardiovascular
  (patho)biology emerges from continuous interactions across molecular pathways, cells,
  tissues, and inter-organ communication 21,22 , cardiovascular diseases can be approached
  as a dynamic, multi-scale and complex trajectory extending from molecular networks
  to whole-organ function. However, even apparently linear causal relationships at
  the organ level are grounded in nonlinear molecular and cellular dynamics, highlighting
  the need for a multi-scale temporal framework. This trajectory is shaped against
  the background of biological aging and the dynamics of complex systems. This perspective
  requires a multidimensional framework in which time is considered an essential dimension
  of disease. Time is not only a static background information, but through aging
  it is also an active determinant of adaptation, remodelling, and failure. It should
  be note that the term "four-dimensional" has previously been used in cardiovascular
  cronobiology to describe the circadian regulation of cardiac physiology across an
  hourly-to-daily timescale 23,24 . Here, however, the fourth dimension captures the
  longitudinal trajectory of disease development and biological aging over years.
  These represent distinct temporal scales and conceptual frameworks. Here, we argue
  that incorporating temporal biology into cardiovascular research, from molecular
  clocks to organlevel remodelling trajectories, is essential to advance precision
  cardiology and to identify therapeutically relevant temporal windows prior to the
  point of no return.In medicine, diagnosis is typically established when a physiological
  parameter deviates from a defined normal range. For example, hypertension is diagnosed
  when blood pressure exceeds a fixed numerical value, whereas HF is defined once
  the ejection fraction falls below a critical value 25 . HFrEF (EF <40%), HFmrEF
  (EF 40-49%) and HFpEF (EF ≥50%) represent 3 clinical phenotypes 16 that share common
  pathophysiological mechanisms, neurohormonal modulation, myocardial fibrosis and
  inflammation, but differ in their temporal trajectories which may be influenced
  by major clinical events such as myocardial infarction, as well as in their therapeutic
  responses. These diagnostic thresholds define disease at the moment of clinical
  recognition rather than at its biological origin, reinforcing the perception that
  health ends and disease begins at the time of diagnosis rather than long before
  the clinical manifestation. This reflects a conceptual limitation: disease is anchored
  to clinical detection rather than to the underlying biological and time-dependent
  trajectory. Furthermore, diagnostic thresholds are often based on normal values
  established in relation to chronological age even though the cardiovascular system

  biological time into our understanding of disease progression. This perspective

  disease should be understood in direct relation to biological trajectories and their
  evolution over time, rather than solely by the timing of events.'

# Time as an indispensable causal dimension in cardiovascular diseases: toward a four\-dimensional biology

**Autores:** Guido Caluori; Sébastien Chaigne

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Heart failure \(HF\) exhibits distinctive event\-and time\-dependent patterns in incidence and progression dynamics\. Over time, the heart first undergoes \(mal\)adaptive cellular, tissue and molecular remodelling 1,2 \. These alterations precede clinical symptoms and diagnosis 3,4 and reflect age\-related changes in cardiovascular structure and function that lower the threshold for overt disease 4,5 \. This subclinical process is followed by incremental cardiac remodelling, including cardiac hypertrophy 6,7 , myocardial stiffening 8 , and impaired diastolic filling 4 \. Similar preclinical alterations occur across other cardiovascular and cardiometabolic disorders: atrial fibrosis and mitochondrial remodelling may develop before atrial fibrillation 9,10 , endothelial dysfunction precedes hypertension and atherosclerosis 11 and even rare diseases such as J\-wave syndromes are now considered cardiomyopathies with subtle structural abnormalities that remain undetected by standard imaging techniques 12 \. In addition, disturbances in protein homeostasis and metabolic adaptation occur long before the onset of clinical manifestations of the disease 13 \. Thus, sudden cardiac pathological events are often preceded by manifestations of biological processes that have been unfolding over several years\. The temporal trajectories differ markedly across various pathologies 12,14 , are not linear 15 , and are modulated by age, sex, lifestyle and inter\-organ interactions\. This interindividual variability calls for a personalised temporal framework for diagnosis and therapy, rather than a single, universally applied diagnostic threshold\. Disease is typically defined once a threshold value is exceeded 4 \. In HF, this heterogeneity is further reflected in the attempts to subcategorize distinct phenotypes, notably HF with reduced ejection fraction \(HFrEF\), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction \(HFmrEF\) and HF with preserved ejection fraction \(HFpEF\)\. This differentiation likely represents overlapping but temporally and mechanistically divergent trajectories rather than strict separate entities 16 \. Similarly, this limitation is evident in the study and management of AF, where the conventional classification into paroxysmal and persistent forms, subsequently expanded to include long\-standing persistent and permanent AF, fails to adequately capture individual risk profiles and the progressive nature of the underlying atrial myopathy 17 \. Emerging approaches based on biomarkers 18 and wearable or implantable devices 19,20 will increasingly enable realtime monitoring of individual disease trajectories for earlier detection of warning signals before irreversible remodelling occurs\. A central challenge is to identify the therapeutically actionable window during which pathological remodelling remains reversible in order to limit disease progression\. This limitation highlights the constraints of traditional frameworks based on universally applied symptomatology, isolated criteria and therefore simple causal relationships that inform therapeutic options 21 \. Given that cardiovascular \(patho\)biology emerges from continuous interactions across molecular pathways, cells, tissues, and inter\-organ communication 21,22 , cardiovascular diseases can be approached as a dynamic, multi\-scale and complex trajectory extending from molecular networks to whole\-organ function\. However, even apparently linear causal relationships at the organ level are grounded in nonlinear molecular and cellular dynamics, highlighting the need for a multi\-scale temporal framework\. This trajectory is shaped against the background of biological aging and the dynamics of complex systems\. This perspective requires a multidimensional framework in which time is considered an essential dimension of disease\. Time is not only a static background information, but through aging it is also an active determinant of adaptation, remodelling, and failure\. However, the translation of such approaches to human cardiovascular disease remains limited by concerns over safety, stability, and long\-term functional integration issues\.Cardiovascular diseases rarely begin at symptom onset\. When HF, AF, or coronary disease becomes clinically apparent, the underlying biology has often been active for years\. The observed pathology reflects the final stage of a prolonged silent process\. A central feature of this process is the divergence between biological and chronological \(cardiovascular\) age, which shapes the rate of progression and proximity to the tipping point\. This mismatch influences risk, reversibility, and therapeutic timing\. Recognising that the individual cardiovascular system may be biologically older or younger than its chronological age represents a mechanistic determinant with implications for prevention and clinical intervention\. Redefining HFrEF, HFmrEF and HFpEF, or as well as paroxysmal and persistent AF, through a temporal and biological age framework transforms them from diagnostic labels into dynamic disease states\. This shift allows interventions to be timed to an individual&\#x27;s progression toward phenotype\-specific tipping points, rather than to symptom onset\. The next paradigm shift in cardiovascular research will be fundamentally conceptual\. It will require integration of the principles of personalised medicine and moving from static, symptom\-driven definitions of disease toward dynamic, trajectory\-based frameworks grounded in systems biology\. The focus will shift from detecting electromechanical/structural abnormalities to unveiling early signs of instability across different levels of biological organisation\. Without integrating biological time as a fourth dimension, cardiovascular biology remains fundamentally incomplete, and progress toward improved patient outcomes will continue to be constrained by the lack of more integrative and translational research frameworks\. If we accept that the disease cause precedes the disease, we may need to redefine causality in cardiovascular pathologies\. The recognition that cardiovascular disease processes begin long before clinical manifestations provides a rationale for incorporating biological time into our understanding of disease progression\. This perspective may help identify early transitions and therapeutic windows before irreversible decompensation and apparent disease\. It also suggests that causality in cardiovascular disease should be understood in direct relation to biological trajectories and their evolution over time, rather than solely by the timing of events\.

# Learning the control\-of\-variables strategy through self\-generated and vicarious errors: the mediating roles of affect, motivation, cognitive load, and error awareness

**Autores:** Linda Hämmerle; Shelbi L\. Kuhlmann; Theresa Krause\-Wichmann; Andrea Möller

**Data:** 2026-10-16 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Abstract Controlled experiments are essential for causal reasoning, yet students struggle with the control\-of\-variables strategy \(CVS\)\. Self\-generated errors, vicarious errors, or a combination could provide learning opportunities; however, error awareness, motivation, affect, and cognitive load may shape whether students benefit from different types of errors\. This study examines the effect of self\-generated, vicarious, combined, or no errors on CVS learning, and investigates affect, motivation, cognitive load, and error awareness as potential mediators\. 255 seventh\- and eighth\-grade students \(40% female\) participated\. In an experimental intervention study with three experimental groups and one control group, students engaged with experiments before receiving instruction and error\-reflection prompts, following a problem\-solving prior to instruction approach\. We assessed CVS skills, affect, cognitive load, motivation, and error awareness\. Analyses included ANCOVA, ANOVA, and path models\. The self\-generated errors group scored highest on the CVS\-posttest, followed by the no\-errors, vicarious errors, and combination groups\. Group differences in CVS performance were not statistically significant, although all groups improved from pre\- to posttest\. No mediation effects were found for affect, motivation, or cognitive load\. Error awareness positively predicted posttest performance, but negatively mediated the effect of self\-generated errors on learning\. Different types of errors and error\-free examples were all associated with CVS learning gains, even though the different types did not differentially affect CVS learning outcomes when controlling for prior knowledge\. Error awareness showed a complex pattern, being positively associated with performance while marginally negatively mediating the effect of self\-generated errors\. These findings highlight the need for further research to better understand the complex role of errors in science learning\.

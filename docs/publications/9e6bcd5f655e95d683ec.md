abstract: Therapeutic peptides have emerged as a rapidly expanding class of biomolecules
  with applications spanning antimicrobial, antiviral, anticancer, immunomodulatory,
  metabolic, and diagnostic settings (8). Their high target specificity, favorable
  safety profiles, and growing clinical success have positioned them as attractive
  alternatives or complements to traditional small-molecule therapeutics (36). The
  number of experimentally characterized bioactive peptides has therefore increased
  substantially during the last decade (38), supporting the development of specialized
  databases and computational resources for peptide discovery, functional annotation,
  and rational design (5,15,31,35). Despite these advances, toxicity remains a major
  limitation to peptide development and clinical translation, constraining candidate
  selection during early-stage screening and optimization (3).Peptide toxicity encompasses
  a broad spectrum of adverse biological effects that arise from the interaction between
  peptides and biological systems. Most toxicity phenotypes are routinely evaluated
  during peptide development including cytotoxicity, hemolysis, neurotoxicity, embryotoxicity,
  and ichthyotoxicity (19,27). These toxicity endpoints have direct implications for
  efficacy, safety, dosage, and regulatory approval. Toxicity annotations have consequently
  accumulated across public databases (31,18,24), literature-derived resources (26,29,28,34),
  supplementary datasets (9,4,23), and experimental studies (33,39). However, these
  resources remain fragmented, employ heterogeneous terminology and annotation criteria,
  and provide variable levels of metadata and evidence quality (21). Their development
  for distinct biological objectives further complicates integration, reproducibility,
  and large-scale analysis (17).The growing availability of toxicity data creates
  opportunities for computational toxicology, data-driven peptide engineering, and
  large-scale biological analyses (32). Their utility, however, depends on the quality,
  consistency, and transparency of the underlying data (14,7). Annotation ambiguity,
  conflicting evidence, heterogeneous endpoint definitions, incomplete metadata, and
  limited provenance tracking can reduce reproducibility and complicate downstream
  reuse (1). The FAIR principles-Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and
  Reusability-provide a framework for organizing and distributing scientific data
  to support discovery, integration, reproducibility, and long-term reuse (22). Nevertheless,
  a standardized framework that harmonizes heterogeneous peptidetoxicity annotations
  while preserving evidence, provenance, and metadata remains largely unavailable
  (21). FAIR-oriented resources tailored to both biological interpretation and computational
  reuse are therefore still needed (37,13).To address these limitations, we developed
  MAOMAO (Metadata-Aware Ontology for Multi-source Annotation Organization), an ontology-guided
  resource integrating peptide-toxicity information from public databases, literature-derived
  resources, and curated datasets. MAOMAO standardizes toxicity terminology, preserves
  source-level provenance, distinguishes uncertainty-related evidence states, and
  organizes annotations within a unified hierarchical framework. The resource also
  provides harmonized metadata, descriptor-based representations, protein language
  model embeddings, benchmark resources, and supporting documentation. These components
  establish a FAIR-oriented and reusable foundation for biological interpretation,
  computational toxicology, and predictive modeling of peptide toxicity.The workflow
  used to construct MAOMAO is summarized in Figure 1A. Toxicity-related information
  from heterogeneous sources was preprocessed, harmonized, mapped to a unified toxicity
  ontology, evaluated for annotation conflicts and evidence quality, enriched with
  standardized metadata, and consolidated into the final resource. To support dissemination,
  computational reuse, and long-term maintenance, MAOMAO was organized into a modular
  architecture comprising complementary resource layers (Figure 1B).MAOMAO was constructed
  by integrating toxicity-related information from publicly available databases, literature-derived
  resources, supplementary datasets, and manually curated repositories. Candidate
  resources were identified through a structured exploration of the peptide toxicity
  landscape using scientific search engines, bibliographic repositories, specialized
  peptide databases, review articles, and citation tracking (2,16). Searches combined
  general terms, including peptide toxicity, toxic peptides, toxic peptide databases,
  and toxic peptide predictive models, with endpoint-specific queries for hemolytic
  peptides, cytotoxic peptides, cytolytic peptides, neurotoxic peptides, embryotoxic
  peptides, ichthyotoxic peptides, and anti mammalian cells peptides. Additional resources
  were identified from toxicity prediction tools, benchmark datasets, supplementary
  materials, associated repositories, and specialized peptide resources. Searches
  were conducted using Google Scholar and PubMed (6) between June 2025 and September
  2026 and covered resources available through September 2026.Candidate resources
  were evaluated according to predefined eligibility criteria, including relevance
  to at least one MAOMAO toxicity endpoint, availability of peptide sequences with
  corresponding categorical toxicity annotations or quantitative toxicity measurements,
  sufficient provenance information, and the possibility of programmatic or manual
  retrieval. Resources were excluded when the underlying dataset could not be retrieved
  through the reported publication, supplementary material, repository, or web server,
  or when the available material lacked sequences suitable for sequence-level integration.
  This included resources containing only numerical representations, peptide names,
  or functional annotations without the corresponding sequences. Candidate-source
  screening, eligibility decisions, integrated and excluded resources, and source-specific
  exclusion reasons are detailed in Supplementary Section S1. (A) Ontology-guided
  harmonization workflow used to construct the resource. Toxicity-related information
  collected from public databases, literature-derived datasets, and curated resources
  undergoes preprocessing, terminology harmonization, ontology mapping, evidence integration,
  quantitative-property parsing, and metadata harmonization to generate a standardized
  and provenance-aware toxicity resource. (B) Modular architecture of MAOMAO. The
  resource is organized into complementary layers comprising harmonized toxicity annotations,
  numerical toxicity properties, sequence profiles, and metadata (Core Layer), sequence-derived
  descriptors (Descriptor Layer), protein language model embeddings (Embedding Layer),
  benchmark resources (Benchmark Layer), practical usage examples, and supporting
  documentation (Documentation Layer). Together, these components establish a FAIR-oriented
  and reusable resource for peptide toxicity research.Data acquisition combined direct
  database downloads, repository and supplementary-file retrieval, and manual extraction
  when required. Retrieved records were converted into a standardized intermediate
  representation, preserving peptide sequences, toxicity annotations, quantitative
  toxicity measurements when available, source identifiers, literature references,
  and available metadata. Source-level quality control verified the reported access
  route, the availability of primary sequences and interpretable toxicity information,
  and the associated publication or repository. Records lacking peptide sequences,
  containing toxicity information that could not be interpreted, or presenting irrecoverable
  formatting inconsistencies were excluded. Retained records were then forwarded to
  preprocessing, terminology harmonization, ontology mapping, metadata harmonization,
  and evidence integration.Following data collection, records underwent standardized
  preprocessing to support integration across heterogeneous resources (Figure 1A).
  This stage included sequence validation, format standardization, annotation parsing,
  identifier normalization, and quality control. The final resource was restricted
  to peptides containing the 20 canonical amino acids and lengths between 5 and 70
  residues (38,25). Recoverable formatting inconsistencies were corrected, exact duplicates
  within each source were removed, and identical sequences reported by different sources
  were retained to preserve provenance and support evidence integration. Each normalized
  sequence was assigned a deterministic identifier generated by applying SHA-256 to
  the UTF-8 encoding of the uppercase, whitespace-free sequence. Quantitative HC50,
  LC50, LD50, and MHC observations were parsed while preserving their reported relation,
  value, unit, and provenance, and were linked to Core sequences through this identifier.
  Detailed sequence-processing, identifier-construction, and quantitative-property
  procedures are provided in Supplementary Section S1.To prevent terminology inconsistencies
  during cross-source integration, toxicity-related annotations were examined for
  alternative spellings, synonyms, endpoint-specific descriptions, and sourcedependent
  nomenclature referring to equivalent biological concepts. These variants were mapped
  to a standardized vocabulary while preserving their original biological meaning.
  For example, haemolytic was normalized to hemolytic, and cytolytic to cytolysis.Harmonized
  annotations were mapped to the MAOMAO toxicity ontology, centered on the root category
  Toxic. The ontology comprises Cytotoxic, Neurotoxic, Embryotoxic, and Ichthyotoxic
  as primary classes, with Hemolytic, Cytolysis, and Anti mammalian cells represented
  as subclasses of Cytotoxic. Hemolytic denotes toxicity involving erythrocyte lysis,
  whereas Cytolysis captures cell lysis or membrane-disruptive activity without a
  specified cell type. Anti-mammalian-cell activity denotes damage to mammalian cells
  or reduced cell viability (20,30,40). The vocabulary was restricted to distinct
  toxicity concepts supported by retrievable sequence-level evidence. Complete endpoint
  definitions, mappings, and assignment rules are provided in Supplementary Section
  S2.Following data collection, harmonization, and ontology mapping, annotations associated
  with identical peptide sequences were aggregated across sources (Figure 1A). Because
  the same sequence could be reported with different toxicity annotations, an evidence-management
  framework was applied to classify each peptide-endpoint pair as positive, negative,
  ambiguous, unlabeled, or no information. Positive states represented direct or hierarchy-supported
  toxicity evidence, whereas negative states corresponded to direct endpoint-specific
  non-toxic evidence. Ambiguous states captured conflicting or unresolved annotations.
  Unlabeled states denoted sequences retained in endpoint-specific sources without
  an explicit class assignment, while no information indicated the absence of evidence
  for that endpoint.Negative evidence was further classified as strong or weak according
  to the supporting information available in the original source. Strong negatives
  were explicitly supported by experimental evidence, whereas weak negatives originated
  from randomly selected negative sets or lacked confirmation of the absence of toxicity
  annotations. Evidence strength was retained in the metadata and provenance records,
  enabling downstream analyses to account for confidence, uncertainty, and conflicting
  evidence. Definitions, assignment criteria, and conflict-resolution rules are provided
  in Supplementary Section S2.To support transparency, interoperability, and long-term
  reuse, source-and endpoint-level metadata were normalized into a unified schema.
  Harmonized fields included source identifiers, literature references, organism information,
  annotation provenance, retrieval dates, file formats, and version information when
  available. Missing values were explicitly preserved without inference. The schema
  also retained original source labels, ontology mappings, evidence categories, publication
  references, and resource-specific annotations, providing a standardized representation
  of the integrated biological and technical information.Provenance was maintained
  by linking harmonized annotations to their source-specific processed datasets, endpoint-integration
  outputs, and metadata records. The sequence-level pivot provides the harmonized
  access layer, while provenance records, the ambiguity-support table, and the hierarchyaudit
  table document evidence aggregation, conflict resolution, and hierarchy-derived
  changes. These files allow each sequence-endpoint state to be traced to its supporting
  sources and processing decisions. Metadata fields, controlled vocabularies, and
  data types are detailed in Supplementary Section S3.To support reproducibility,
  computational reuse, and long-term maintenance, MAOMAO was organized into complementary
  functional layers (Figure 1B). This modular architecture separates harmonized biological
  data, computational representations, benchmark resources, and supporting documentation
  while preserving interoperability across the resource.The Core Layer contains harmonized
  peptide sequences, toxicity annotations, ontology mappings, evidence classifications,
  provenance information, and metadata. It additionally provides numerical toxicity
  properties and sequence profiles. Descriptor-based representations were generated
  using Roxy (11), whereas embedding-based representations were obtained with Sylphy
  (12) using publicly available protein language models. Benchmark resources were
  generated using partitioning strategies implemented through BioSieve (10). The Documentation
  Layer contains ontology specifications, metadata schemas, resource descriptions,
  licensing information, and versioning records. Supported representations, benchmark
  resources, resource organization, and file structures are detailed in Supplementary
  Sections S4-S5.FAIR principles guided the design and construction of MAOMAO to support
  transparency, reproducibility, interoperability, and long-term sustainability. FAIR-oriented
  practices were incorporated across data collection, terminology harmonization, ontology
  development, evidence management, metadata integration, and resource organization.Interoperability
  and reusability were supported through standardized toxicity terminology, ontology-guided
  endpoint mapping, harmonized metadata schemas, explicit evidence categorization,
  provenance tracking, and version-aware organization. These components enable heterogeneous
  toxicity annotations to be integrated while preserving the original information
  associated with each record.Findability, accessibility, and computational reuse
  were supported by distributing resource components in machine-readable formats and
  using standardized identifiers linking sequences, annotations, metadata, ontology
  terms, and evidence categories. The sequence-derived SHA-256 identifiers provide
  a stable key for linking corresponding records across the Core, Descriptor, Embedding,
  Benchmark, and sequence-profile resources. Harmonization procedures, ontology definitions,
  evidence-management rules, metadata specifications, and generation workflows were
  also documented to support validation, updates, and downstream use. Resource availability,
  file organization, versioning, and supporting documentation are detailed in Supplementary
  Section S5.An overview of MAOMAO is presented in Figure 2A. The resource integrates
  toxicity-related information from 56 publicly available databases, literature-derived
  resources, supplementary datasets, and manually curated repositories. Of 1,372,190
  non-deduplicated source-level records initially collected, 898,637 were retained
  after source-specific preprocessing, representing a reduction of approximately 34.5%.
  Removed records included duplicates, incomplete entries, formatting inconsistencies,
  and annotations that did not satisfy the inclusion criteria.Following terminology
  harmonization, ontology mapping, cross-source evidence aggregation, state resolution,
  and quality control, the final release comprised 71,857 unique peptide sequences
  across eight toxicity-related endpoints. These were organized into 574,856 sequence-endpoint
  combinations, together with standardized metadata and provenance records linking
  annotations, ontology assignments, evidence classifications, and source-specific
  information.Publication references, repository or server information, download dates,
  file formats, source types, and resource behavior were documented for all 56 sources.
  Annotation content and last-update dates were available for 55 sources (98.21%),
  whereas explicit licensing information was available for 15 sources (26.79%). Missing
  metadata were preserved without inference. These components provide a harmonized
  resource for organizing, disseminating, and computationally analyzing peptide toxicity
  information.The ontology-guided organization of toxicity annotations is presented
  in Figure 2B. The root category Toxic contains 57,080 available peptide-endpoint
  states, including 13,824 positives, and comprises four primary classes, namely Cytotoxic
  (25,454 available states; 7,122 positive), Neurotoxic (3,183; 1,481 positive), Embryotoxic
  (2 positive), and Ichthyotoxic (5 positive). The Cytotoxic branch is further divided
  into Hemolytic (35,960 available states; 4,714 positive), Cytolysis (343; 341 positive),
  and Anti mammalian cells (21,393 available states; 3,664 positive), preserving more
  specific biological contexts. These categories define the endpoint structure used
  throughout MAOMAO.Evidence-state distributions are summarized in Figure 2C and Supplementary
  Table S10. Across MAOMAO, 31,153 states were positive, 91,506 negative, 15,717 ambiguous,
  and 5,044 unlabeled. Because only positive annotations were propagated through the
  hierarchy, all other states remained endpoint-specific, and no-information states
  were excluded from the percentages shown in Figure 2C. Toxic contained 13,824 positives,
  whereas Hemolytic had the highest proportion of ambiguous states (19.05%) and was
  the only endpoint with unlabeled states, representing 14.03% of its available annotations.Across
  the seven non-root endpoints, 7,331 of the 9,421 peptides with at least one positive
  annotation were assigned to multiple toxicity categories. This corresponds to 77.82%
  of positive peptides across these endpoints and 10.2% of all MAOMAO sequences. Percentages
  were calculated relative to the positive sequences assigned to each row endpoint
  and should therefore be interpreted directionally. The largest overlap occurred
  between Cytotoxic and Hemolytic, with 4,128 shared sequences, representing 57.96%
  of cytotoxic-positive and 87.57% of hemolytic-positive sequences. Complete pairwise
  counts and directional percentages are provided in Supplementary Section S2.MAOMAO
  distributes harmonized annotations, numerical representations, benchmark resources,
  metadata, ontology definitions, and documentation across the functional layers shown
  in Figure 1B.The Core Layer contains 574,856 harmonized peptide-endpoint combinations
  corresponding to 71,857 unique sequences, together with ontology assignments, evidence
  classifications, provenance, and standardized metadata. It additionally provides
  169 HC50, LC50, LD50, and MHC measurement records. Most were linked to the Core
  sequence universe, while six LD50 records remained unmatched because their sequences
  exceeded the 70-residue limit. One profile per Core sequence summarizes its activity
  annotations, supporting evidence, ontology effects, toxicity targets, selected descriptors,
  and available numerical toxicity properties. The Descriptor Layer provides 41 features
  capturing sequence composition and physicochemical properties.The Embedding Layer
  includes 10 protein-language-model embedding matrices and a one-hot representation,
  all covering the complete set of 71,857 MAOMAO sequences.The Benchmark Layer contains
  300 endpoint-seed-strategy configurations generated through random and stratified
  five-fold partitioning of five eligible endpoints (Toxic, Cytotoxic, Hemolytic,
  Neurotoxic, and Anti mammalian cells) using 30 random seeds. These correspond to
  1,500 data partitions and, across the 11 numerical representations, 3,300 representation-specific
  configurations and 16,500 fold instances. Associated parameters, reports, summaries,
  and execution records are also provided.The Documentation Layer includes ontology
  definitions, metadata schemas, resource descriptions, versioning records, and licensing
  information. Files are distributed in standard formats, including CSV, JSON, and
  YAML, with detailed component descriptions and inventories provided in Supplementary
  Sections S4-S5.To facilitate direct reuse of the distributed assets, MAOMAO provides

  the resource from the original sources. The introductory workflow covers resource

  numerical representations, and benchmark partitions through stable sequence identifiers.
  Complementary notebooks support descriptive characterization of endpoint states,
  class balance, co-occurrence, sequence lengths, ontology consistency, and representation
  coverage, as well as retrieval and querying of individual sequence profiles. Generated
  tables and figures are stored separately from the released resource files.Two additional

The sequence\-derived SHA\-256 identifiers provide a stable key for linking corresponding records across the Core, Descriptor, Embedding, Benchmark, and sequence\-profile resources\. Harmonization procedures, ontology definitions, evidence\-management rules, metadata specifications, and generation workflows were also documented to support validation, updates, and downstream use\. Resource availability, file organization, versioning, and supporting documentation are detailed in Supplementary Section S5\.An overview of MAOMAO is presented in Figure 2A\. The resource integrates toxicity\-related information from 56 publicly available databases, literature\-derived resources, supplementary datasets, and manually curated repositories\. Of 1,372,190 non\-deduplicated source\-level records initially collected, 898,637 were retained after source\-specific preprocessing, representing a reduction of approximately 34\.5%\. Removed records included duplicates, incomplete entries, formatting inconsistencies, and annotations that did not satisfy the inclusion criteria\.Following terminology harmonization, ontology mapping, cross\-source evidence aggregation, state resolution, and quality control, the final release comprised 71,857 unique peptide sequences across eight toxicity\-related endpoints\. These were organized into 574,856 sequence\-endpoint combinations, together with standardized metadata and provenance records linking annotations, ontology assignments, evidence classifications, and source\-specific information\.Publication references, repository or server information, download dates, file formats, source types, and resource behavior were documented for all 56 sources\. Annotation content and last\-update dates were available for 55 sources \(98\.21%\), whereas explicit licensing information was available for 15 sources \(26\.79%\)\. Missing metadata were preserved without inference\. These components provide a harmonized resource for organizing, disseminating, and computationally analyzing peptide toxicity information\.The ontology\-guided organization of toxicity annotations is presented in Figure 2B\. The root category Toxic contains 57,080 available peptide\-endpoint states, including 13,824 positives, and comprises four primary classes, namely Cytotoxic \(25,454 available states; 7,122 positive\), Neurotoxic \(3,183; 1,481 positive\), Embryotoxic \(2 positive\), and Ichthyotoxic \(5 positive\)\. The Cytotoxic branch is further divided into Hemolytic \(35,960 available states; 4,714 positive\), Cytolysis \(343; 341 positive\), and Anti mammalian cells \(21,393 available states; 3,664 positive\), preserving more specific biological contexts\. These categories define the endpoint structure used throughout MAOMAO\.Evidence\-state distributions are summarized in Figure 2C and Supplementary Table S10\. Across MAOMAO, 31,153 states were positive, 91,506 negative, 15,717 ambiguous, and 5,044 unlabeled\. Because only positive annotations were propagated through the hierarchy, all other states remained endpoint\-specific, and no\-information states were excluded from the percentages shown in Figure 2C\. Toxic contained 13,824 positives, whereas Hemolytic had the highest proportion of ambiguous states \(19\.05%\) and was the only endpoint with unlabeled states, representing 14\.03% of its available annotations\.Across the seven non\-root endpoints, 7,331 of the 9,421 peptides with at least one positive annotation were assigned to multiple toxicity categories\. This corresponds to 77\.82% of positive peptides across these endpoints and 10\.2% of all MAOMAO sequences\. Percentages were calculated relative to the positive sequences assigned to each row endpoint and should therefore be interpreted directionally\. The largest overlap occurred between Cytotoxic and Hemolytic, with 4,128 shared sequences, representing 57\.96% of cytotoxic\-positive and 87\.57% of hemolytic\-positive sequences\. Complete pairwise counts and directional percentages are provided in Supplementary Section S2\.MAOMAO distributes harmonized annotations, numerical representations, benchmark resources, metadata, ontology definitions, and documentation across the functional layers shown in Figure 1B\.The Core Layer contains 574,856 harmonized peptide\-endpoint combinations corresponding to 71,857 unique sequences, together with ontology assignments, evidence classifications, provenance, and standardized metadata\. It additionally provides 169 HC50, LC50, LD50, and MHC measurement records\. Most were linked to the Core sequence universe, while six LD50 records remained unmatched because their sequences exceeded the 70\-residue limit\. One profile per Core sequence summarizes its activity annotations, supporting evidence, ontology effects, toxicity targets, selected descriptors, and available numerical toxicity properties\. The Descriptor Layer provides 41 features capturing sequence composition and physicochemical properties\.The Embedding Layer includes 10 protein\-language\-model embedding matrices and a one\-hot representation, all covering the complete set of 71,857 MAOMAO sequences\.The Benchmark Layer contains 300 endpoint\-seed\-strategy configurations generated through random and stratified five\-fold partitioning of five eligible endpoints \(Toxic, Cytotoxic, Hemolytic, Neurotoxic, and Anti mammalian cells\) using 30 random seeds\. These correspond to 1,500 data partitions and, across the 11 numerical representations, 3,300 representation\-specific configurations and 16,500 fold instances\. Associated parameters, reports, summaries, and execution records are also provided\.The Documentation Layer includes ontology definitions, metadata schemas, resource descriptions, versioning records, and licensing information\. Files are distributed in standard formats, including CSV, JSON, and YAML, with detailed component descriptions and inventories provided in Supplementary Sections S4\-S5\.To facilitate direct reuse of the distributed assets, MAOMAO provides five executable notebooks that operate on the released files rather than reconstructing the resource from the original sources\. The introductory workflow covers resource access, file validation, navigation of the Core Layer, and linkage of annotations, numerical representations, and benchmark partitions through stable sequence identifiers\. Complementary notebooks support descriptive characterization of endpoint states, class balance, co\-occurrence, sequence lengths, ontology consistency, and representation coverage, as well as retrieval and querying of individual sequence profiles\. Generated tables and figures are stored separately from the released resource files\.Two additional notebooks illustrate downstream machine\-learning reuse\. The neurotoxicity classification example joins a released train\-validation\-test partition with an existing protein language model representation and evaluates logistic regression, a linear support vector machine, and random forest models using fixed settings\. The quantitative regression example shows how MAOMAO&\#x27;s numerical toxicity measurements can be combined with a released sequence representation in a simple regression analysis\. These workflows are intended to demonstrate resource interoperability and reproducible reuse rather than establish state\-of\-the\-art predictors or replace the released benchmark design\.

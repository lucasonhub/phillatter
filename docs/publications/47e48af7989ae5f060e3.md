# The last human language: Artificial intelligence, translation, and the future of communication

**Autores:** Arjun Kumar

**Data:** 2026-10-14 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

The rapid development of artificial intelligence has transformed translation from a distinctly human linguistic practice into an increasingly automated process\. Large language models can translate, paraphrase, interpret, and generate communication across languages with unprecedented speed, raising a fundamental question: if machines eventually become the primary mediators between languages, what happens to the human meanings, cultural memories, and forms of difference embedded within language? Science fiction has anticipated this problem through its recurring fascination with artificial languages, machine communication, alien linguistics, and technologically mediated translation\. This article examines Samuel R\. Delany’s Babel\-17 \(1966\), China Miéville’s Embassytown \(2011\), Ian McEwan’s Machines Like Me \(2019\), and Kazuo Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun \(2021\) to examine how artificial intelligence and nonhuman communication destabilize conventional assumptions about language\. Drawing upon Walter Benjamin’s theory of translation, Ludwig Wittgenstein’s philosophy of language, N\. Katherine Hayles’s posthumanism, and recent scholarship on AI translation and linguistic bias, the article argues that translation is not merely the transfer of information between languages but a negotiation of cultural difference\. It proposes the concept of “linguistic posthumanism” to describe a future in which communication is increasingly produced through collaboration between human and artificial linguistic agents\. The article ultimately argues that the greatest danger posed by AI translation is not that machines will replace human language, but that they may silently standardize linguistic difference by privileging languages, meanings, and cultural assumptions already dominant within technological infrastructures\.

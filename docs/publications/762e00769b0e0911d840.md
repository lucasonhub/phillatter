# Neighborhood\-scale disparities in air exchange rates and inhaled pollution dose among school\-aged children

**Autores:** Emma K Walter; Matthew J\. Eckelman; Julia Varshavsky

**Data:** 2026-10-15 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

Ambient air pollution and its infiltration into indoor environments can vary substantially within urban neighborhoods due to differences in traffic density, emission sources, and housing conditions; however, this variability is often not captured in exposure studies\. This study estimates average daily inhaled dose of particulate matter \(PM 2\.5 \) and ozone \(O 3 \) for school\-aged children in Chelsea, MA, evaluating local differences in exposure\. A hyperlocal network of 84 air quality monitors was combined with parcel\-level housing data and the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory \(LBNL\) infiltration model to estimate residential air exchange rates \(AERs\) and daily inhaled doses of PM 2\.5 and O 3 across Chelsea\. Across the city, average O 3 daily doses ranged from 1\.4 to 5\.0 µg/kg, with ambient daily average concentrations varying by up to 39 µg/m 3 \. For PM 2\.5 , daily inhaled dose presented less variation, ranging from 0\.52 to 1\.4 µg/kg; however, observed spatial differences in median ambient PM 2\.5 concentrations of up to 4\.4 µg/m 3 were observed, which may still be sufficient to influence health outcomes\. Residential air exchange rates \(AERs\) were higher in block groups with greater rent burden and larger Hispanic/Latino populations\. The combination of parcel\-level data and high\-resolution monitoring revealed disparities in ambient concentrations, housing conditions, and inhaled dose that could meaningfully influence health outcomes in children\. This study utilizes a hyperlocal air quality monitoring network and parcel\-level housing data to evaluate air pollution exposure in elementary school\-aged children, finding neighborhood\-level differences in ambient concentrations and inhaled dose sufficient to influence health outcomes\. Air exchange rates varied across the study area and increased in block groups with larger Hispanic/Latino populations and higher rent burdens\. This work demonstrates the importance of using high\-resolution, microscale air pollution monitors, and individual housing characteristics in exposure studies\.

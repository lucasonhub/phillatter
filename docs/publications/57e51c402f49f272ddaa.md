# The short\-beaked echidna in South\-East Queensland: regional data gaps and conservation risks

**Autores:** Kate J\. Dutton‐Regester

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** article

## Resumo

The short\-beaked echidna \(Tachyglossus aculeatus\) is widely distributed across Australia and is often described as common, yet robust information on abundance, demography and population trends remains sparse across much of its mainland range\. This evidence gap is particularly important in South\-East Queensland \(SEQ\), where rapid urban expansion, habitat fragmentation, dense road networks, climatic warming and the spread of the red imported fire ant \(Solenopsis invicta, RIFA\) may interact with the species’ slow\-breeding life history\. Together, these pressures could affect regional population status, while current datasets provide limited capacity to detect change\. This review synthesises current knowledge relevant to echidna vulnerability and detectability and evaluates the evidence base available for SEQ, drawing on citizen science observations, wildlife hospital records and the limited peer\-reviewed field studies from the region\. The Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act \(EPBC\) listing of the Kangaroo Island echidna \(T\. a\. multiaculeatus\) is used as a cautionary case study, illustrating how chronic mortality pressures and life\-history constraints can generate conservation risk that remains undetected without structured monitoring\. Despite detection through citizen science, SEQ echidnas remain effectively unassessable at a regional scale, as for many Australian native species: existing datasets demonstrate presence, but do not permit estimation of density, survival, recruitment, connectivity, or trends\. Priorities for the coming decade include region\-wide occupancy monitoring with repeated surveys across disturbance gradients, integration of citizen science and hospital datasets, and targeted studies of connectivity and emerging threats\. Proactive monitoring is needed now to establish baselines and detect gradual change before declines become conspicuous\.

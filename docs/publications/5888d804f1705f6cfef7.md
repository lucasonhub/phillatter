abstract: 'Conversational artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from novelty to infrastructure.
  Rulebased chatbots that once nudged users through smoking-cessation scripts have
  given way to large language model (LLM)-powered agents capable of open-ended, multilingual,
  empathetic-sounding dialogue, available around the clock at near-zero marginal cost.
  Systematic and scoping reviews now document measurable effects of AI chatbots on
  physical activity, diet, smoking cessation, medication adherence, and mental distress
  across thousands of participants (Feng et al., 2025). The World Health Organization
  (WHO) itself has deployed a generative AI assistant, S.A.R.A.H., to test population-scale
  health communication (van Kolfschooten et al., 2025). Taken together, these developments
  justify treating conversational AI not merely as a clinical or commercial curiosity,
  but as an emerging layer of public health infrastructure -one that sits alongside
  water systems, mass media, and vaccination programs as a determinant of how populations
  receive, interpret, and act on health information. (Wang et al., 2024). This opinion
  argues that the digital public health community should engage conversational AI
  on exactly those terms: as infrastructure, not gadgetry. Infrastructure framing
  changes the questions we ask. Instead of "does the chatbot work?" we should ask
  who it works for, under what governance, and with what consequences for equity,
  trust, and the broader information ecosystem. I organize this argument around three
  tensions that recur across the recent literature: (i) genuine behavioral efficacy
  versus fragile evidentiary depth; (ii) the promise of expanded access versus the
  risk of a new digital divide; and (iii) the communicative power of generative AI
  versus its capacity to manufacture and amplify misinformation. I close by proposing
  that Sustainable Development Goal (SDG)aligned public health institutions adopt
  a public-interest design standard for conversational AI, rather than leaving its
  trajectory to consumer technology markets alone.The evidence base for AI chatbots
  in health promotion has grown quickly and, on balance, positively. A widely cited
  systematic review found that AI chatbots produced favorable outcomes across lifestyle
  modification domains, particularly physical activity, diet, and smoking cessation
  (Aggarwal et al., 2023). A 2026 scoping review of 43 studies (Fu et al., 2026) reported
  that 82% of behavior-change comparisons trended positive, though only about a third
  reached moderate or larger effect sizes, and cost-effectiveness data were essentially
  absent. A meta-analysis focused on adolescents and young adults (Feng et al., 2025)
  similarly found chatbots effective for alleviating mental distress and supporting
  health behaviors, while noting substantial heterogeneity in intervention design
  and outcome measurement. Three limitations temper enthusiasm. First, most trials
  are short, nonclinical, adult-skewed, and rarely randomized at scale; only about
  a third of studies in the largest recent scoping review were randomized controlled
  trials (Fu et al., 2026). Second, "AI chatbot" spans an enormous range of systems,
  from rule-based decision trees to frontier LLMs, and pooling them obscures which
  architectural features actually drive behavior change (Kurniawan et al., 2024;Schachner
  et al., 2020). Third, almost no study reports durability beyond a few months, leaving
  open whether early engagement gains represent sustained health improvement or a
  novelty effect. For a section whose remit is population-level promotion and prevention
  rather than individual clinical outcomes, this matters: infrastructure decisions
  built on inflated short-term efficacy claims risk premature, poorly targeted public
  investment (Hampsher-Monk et al., 2024).A second tension concerns reach. Proponents
  rightly note that AI chatbots are inexpensive to scale, available in multiple languages,
  and can operate where clinical workforce is scarceattributes explicitly cited in
  WHO''s own rationale for deploying S.A.R.A.H. as a research prototype for population
  health communication (Palmer & Schwan, 2025). Behavioralaccess frameworks extend
  this logic, arguing that digital health tools must be evaluated not only on availability
  but on whether populations can meaningfully engage with them across design, delivery,
  dissemination, and sustainment stages. Yet the same structural gaps that limit telemedicine
  and mobile health also constrain chatbot benefit: broadband access, device ownership,
  digital literacy, language coverage, and trust in institutional actors are unevenly
  distributed within and between countries (Banerjee, 2026). Chatbots trained and
  validated predominantly in high-resource, English-language settings may perform
  less reliably for populations with the greatest unmet need -the same populations
  digital public health is meant to prioritize under SDG 3, 9, and 11''s emphasis
  on inclusive infrastructure and sustainable, resilient communities. A chatbot that
  reduces cost-per-contact for a health system does not automatically reduce disparities
  in outcomes; without equity-explicit design, deployment, and evaluation, it may
  simply reallocate existing advantages to those already best served by digital tools,
  echoing patterns already observed in AI-enabled primary care and telemedicine expansion
  (Elangovan et al., 2025).The third tension is the sharpest. The same generative
  capacity that lets an AI chatbot deliver personalized, conversational health guidance
  also lets it generate fluent, plausible-sounding health misinformation, and do so
  faster than human fact-checkers can respond. A 2025 opinion piece in this journal
  warned that generative AI tools function as de facto triage systems and informal
  health advisors in low-and middle-income countries, where their fluency can be mistaken
  for authority even when their outputs are unreliable. A parallel systematic review
  of generative AI and health misinformation catalogued mechanisms of production,
  propagation, and mitigation across text, image, and synthetic media, underscoring
  that the problem is not hypothetical but already observed at scale (Saeidnia et
  al., 2026). Mitigation research is advancing in step. Retrieval-augmented generation
  has been used to improve the factual reliability of LLM-based COVID-19 fact-checking
  tools (Li et al., 2025), and thematic-analysis pipelines have been proposed to detect
  and "inoculate" audiences against circulating misinformation narratives (Malek et
  al., 2026). A recent risk taxonomy specifically for public health LLM adoption (Zhou
  et al., 2025) offers a structured way for practitioners to reason about failure
  modes before deployment rather than after. Conversational AI for vaccine communication,
  an earlier and instructive precedent, showed both promise for addressing hesitancy
  and clear risk of amplifying inaccurate claims when systems were insufficiently
  curated (Passanante et al., 2023). The lesson across this literature is consistent:
  the communicative power of conversational AI is not neutral infrastructure like
  a water pipe; it actively shapes the epistemic environment it operates in, for better
  or worse, depending on design choices made well upstream of any individual conversation.Evidence

  should require reporting of architecture (rulebased versus LLM-based, retrieval-augmented

  dissemination and sustainment as distinct design stages, not afterthoughts. Third,

  rather than as a discrete clinical tool has practical consequences: it shifts evaluation
  from short efficacy trials toward equity-stratified, durability-tested, misinformation-resilient
  deployment standards, and it shifts responsibility from individual developers toward
  the institutions, including public health agencies, research funders, and journals
  such as this one, that decide what counts as sufficient evidence for scale. A reasonable
  objection is that infrastructure-level governance risks slowing beneficial innovation,
  particularly in settings where any accessible health guidance outperforms none.
  That concern is legitimate and argues for proportionate, tiered oversight rather
  than uniform restriction -lighter validation for low-stakes wellness nudges, more
  rigorous requirements for agents addressing vaccine hesitancy, chronic disease self-management,
  or crisis-adjacent mental health support. What the evidence does not support is
  treating conversational AI''s current trajectory as selfcorrecting. Left to consumer
  technology incentives alone, generative health chatbots are likely to continue optimizing
  for engagement and fluency rather than for population-level epidemiological benefit
  or equitable reach. The digital public health community is well positioned, through
  funding priorities, publication standards, and partnership with bodies like WHO,
  to redirect that trajectory toward public-interest design before it further consolidates
  around existing digital and geographic advantage.'

## Resumo

Rulebased chatbots that once nudged users through smoking\-cessation scripts have given way to large language model \(LLM\)\-powered agents capable of open\-ended, multilingual, empathetic\-sounding dialogue, available around the clock at near\-zero marginal cost\. This opinion argues that the digital public health community should engage conversational AI on exactly those terms: as infrastructure, not gadgetry\. Infrastructure framing changes the questions we ask\. Instead of &quot;does the chatbot work?&quot; we should ask who it works for, under what governance, and with what consequences for equity, trust, and the broader information ecosystem\.

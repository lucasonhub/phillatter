# Eliciting Affective Behavior in Atlantic Bottlenose Dolphins \(Tursiops truncatus\) in Human Care – The Importance of Context

**Autores:** Emma Christine Miller

**Data:** 2026-10-12 · **Tipo:** dissertation

## Resumo

The study of affective behavior in non\-human animals has increased due to its importance in monitoring their welfare\. However, assessing affective states poses challenges given that context, state and behavior do not exist in a 1:1:1 correspondence\. Bottlenose dolphins \(Tursiops spp\.\) exist in complex social and physical environments that make them an interesting candidate for the study of affective states\. In Chapter 1, we identified candidate affective behaviors from existing literature and from surveys of dolphin trainers\. We tested these candidate behaviors using dolphins’ responses to a cognitive task as a quasi\-manipulation\. We expected to induce positive affect when a reward was received and negative affect when a reward was not received\. We found that certain behaviors are associated with each outcome and that these behaviors matched those that were identified by trainers\. In Chapter 2, we elicited a state of surprise by presenting dolphins with a violation of expectations\. We found that dolphins evaluated the stimulus longer and produced bubble bursts at a higher rate when expectations were violated\. Using another violation of expectations, in Chapter 3, we elicited frustration by occasionally introducing a delay in the delivery of primary reinforcement after a successful response in a cognitive task\. We assessed whether dolphins produced a response and whether this response varied based on their relationship with the trainer that produced the delay\. We found that dolphins exhibited behavior indicative of frustration, and that they vocalized in\-air longer when the delay came from a trainer with whom they had a strong relationship\. As a comparison, in Chapter 4, we examined whether the relationship affected performance at a higher effort behavior – beaching, across levels of beaching proficiency\. We found that “master” beachers did not vary in beaching performance across trainers, as expected\. However, there was no evidence that trainers impacted beaching success for beginners or proficient dolphins, contrary to our hypotheses\. This dissertation demonstrates the probabilistic nature of the relationship between affect and behavior, that careful manipulations of context can help examine affective behavior in non\-humans, and the importance of trainers’ beliefs in monitoring the welfare of animals in human care\.
